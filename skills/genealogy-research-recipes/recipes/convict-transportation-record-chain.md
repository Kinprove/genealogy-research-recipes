# Reconstruct a transported convict as a chain of record episodes

Use this recipe when you have a convict index hit, a family story about transportation, or a colonial pardon or ticket-of-leave entry for a British convict. The goal is to rebuild the sentence as a series of dated episodes. Each episode comes from its own record series, has its own locator and has its own date role. One index entry never stands for the whole chain. The discipline of keeping episodes and date roles apart is the same one used in [Soviet-repression and military episodes](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/russian-military-repression-record-episodes.md) (§ Preserve date roles and successive episodes). Rulings on identity belong in [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md). For failed platform searches, use [Ancestry search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/ancestry-record-search-recovery.md) or [newspaper search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/newspapers-com-search-recovery.md).

## Pick a direction, then list every stage

Start from whichever end you hold. In the teaching case the convict was first found in the colonial records, and the presenter then worked back to the English trial [S1]. Working forward from a trial report is equally valid [S2]. Whichever way you go, set up a worklist with one row for each stage below, and leave a row empty until it is filled from an inspected record:

1. A newspaper report of the arrest or trial: the charge, any accomplices, the verdict and the stated sentence [S1], [S2].
2. The criminal register entry from assizes or quarter sessions: the trial date, the court and county, and the sentence. These registers (The National Archives HO 26 and HO 27; on Ancestry as "England & Wales, Criminal Registers, 1791–1892") list people charged with indictable offences, with court, trial date and sentence [S2], [S3].
3. The prison hulk register entry. The hulk registers are TNA HO 9, on Ancestry as "UK, Prison Hulk Registers and Letter Books, 1802–1849" [S1], [S2], [S3].
4. The convict ship record: vessel, departure and the people shipped together [S2].
5. The colonial arrival indent (NSW State Archives series NRS 12188 and 12189). From the 1820s NSW indents record a physical description, including a "particular marks or scars" column, so expect it and transcribe it [S2]. An arrival index is not the indent; keep them in separate rows.
6. Colonial gaol entries, such as the NSW gaol books on Ancestry as "New South Wales, Australia, Gaol Description and Entrance Books, 1818–1930" [S1], [S2], [S3].
7. A ticket of leave, conditional pardon or absolute pardon. Each is its own row. On Ancestry these appear in "New South Wales and Tasmania, Australia, Convict Pardons and Tickets of Leave" (1834–1859) and the NSW register of conditional and absolute pardons (1788–1870) [S2], [S3].
8. A permission to marry, then the marriage record itself. Each is its own row. NSW convicts needed the Governor's permission, recorded in NRS 12212, Registers of convicts' applications to marry, 1825–1851, apart from the marriage register [S2].

The presenter said he found most of the chain on Findmypast, with other items from British and Australian archives [S2]. In an earlier talk he found it through the record suggestions Ancestry linked to one hit [S3]. A platform suggestion is a lead to a record series. It is not a verified episode.

## Keep each stage's date role and what it proves

- **A hulk entry does not prove conviction.** The presenter pointed out that one hulk register entry did not show whether the man was held before or after sentence [S1]. Another hulk entry he showed did give a conviction date and a received date [S2]. Record whatever the entry actually states. If it states nothing about sentence, record "stage unstated". Never fill in the conviction from the hulk row.
- **The sentence comes from the trial.** Take the trial sentence from the court register or the trial report. If a colonial record such as an indent states a sentence, transcribe it in that record's own row, but never let it stand in for the trial record. In the teaching case the newspaper gave a 14-year transportation sentence and an acquitted accomplice, and the register of assizes and sessions gave a trial on 1 August 1837 at Norwich with a sentence of transportation [S2]. The presenter did not say which court tried the case: read the court from the register entry itself, and record it as unconfirmed until you have. Keep both rows, so that each record's own statement stays visible.
- **Received, sailed, arrived and admitted are different dates.** The indent the presenter showed gives arrival on 8 February 1838; later, while describing the Newcastle gaol admission, he gave the arrival year as 1837 [S2]. A man tried on 1 August 1837 and held until October 1837 before he was put on the ship [S2] cannot plausibly have arrived in 1837, so use 8 February 1838 as the working arrival date. Keep 1837 in the gaol row with its source and the reason it was set aside (it conflicts with the trial and shipping dates). Do not delete it. The working date stays provisional until the trial and ship rows are filled from inspected records. Whenever values conflict, test each against the dates of the stages before and after it. Take the value the sequence supports as the working date, and keep every other value in its own row with its source and the reason it was set aside.
- **Ages show when the record was made.** The presenter read the convict's age as 21 at trial and 23 at the Newcastle gaol admission [S2]. Use the age to place that record in the sequence. Do not adjust it to make the rows agree.
- **An index-only row stays index-only.** One arrival item the presenter showed, carrying an Archives Authority of New South Wales notice, is a partial index of convicts arriving 1837–1842 with its own volume reference [S2]. It is an index, not the indent: give it its own row and keep the inspected indent in a separate row.

## Bound each court search by its jurisdiction

A miss in one court database only tells you about that court. One presenter described the Old Bailey Online as holding transcripts from every court of the period [S4]. Do not repeat that claim. The Old Bailey Online covers trials at the Old Bailey, London's central criminal court, 1674–1913; from 1834 that court's jurisdiction also took in urban parts of Kent, Surrey and Essex. A man tried at a county assize or quarter sessions elsewhere, such as the Norwich trial above [S2], will not appear there. Before calling any search negative, record the courts, counties and years it covered. The site's punishment search includes transportation [S4], so it can produce candidates, but each candidate still has to be matched to the colonial person. For convicts sent to America, the same presenter pointed to Peter Wilson Coldham's lists, compiled from official court and government records (for example *The Complete Book of Emigrants in Bondage, 1614–1775*), and to published abstracts of runaway notices [S4]. Runaway notices regularly include a physical description, which is their main value when no image of the person exists [S4]. Record both as compilations, not as originals, and go back to the underlying court record or newspaper where you can.

## Treat the trial-to-colonist link as unproven

Name, age, offence, place of conviction and vessel are the fields that join a British trial to a colonial convict. Record which of them each row shares with the others. The presenter said the surname was uncommon, which made the records easy to attribute [S3]. That point only holds for that surname. With a common name, look for conflicting candidates in the same court and the same arrival year. A family story or tree attachment only names a candidate. Send the identity decision to [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md), and mark each link "proposed" until that is done.

## Read pardons and marriage from the documents

Marriage did not bring an automatic pardon. Do not repeat the claim that every convict was pardoned on marriage. The same presenter said so in two talks [S1], [S3]. In a later talk he showed a conditional pardon that cited the man's good conduct since arrival [S2]. Permission to marry was a separate process that could be refused, and pardons and tickets of leave were granted through their own processes. Record the reason exactly as the pardon states it. Keep a ticket of leave, a conditional pardon and an absolute pardon in separate rows, because they are different instruments. Permission to marry is also a separate row from the marriage record. Claims about what happened after the pardon, such as a return to England, need their own records before they enter the table. The presenter mentioned such records but did not show them [S1].

## Close with an episode table

Return one row per episode with these columns: stage, record series as titled, date and its role, locator (volume/page/item and image), the identifying details actually stated, and the evidence status (original seen, transcription, index or compilation). Mark the rows that are still empty, the courts and years that were searched, and every unproven identity link. A reconstructed narrative must not fill an empty row.
