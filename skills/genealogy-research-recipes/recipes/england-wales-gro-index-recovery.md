# Read England and Wales GRO and FreeBMD index entries as registration observations

Use when an England or Wales civil-registration index entry (GRO online index, FreeBMD, or an
aggregator copy) is being used to date an event, place it, rebuild a sibling group, recover a
wife's surname or pair a couple. These entries tell you the registration quarter, district,
volume/page and, in the GRO birth index, the mother's maiden surname. They do not give an event
date, a birthplace, a pairing of spouses or parentage. For the namesake-pool and
successive-union logic, use [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).
For general FamilySearch jurisdiction and missed-person recovery, use
[FamilySearch practical workflows](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/familysearch-practical-record-workflows.md)
(§1 jurisdiction, §8 missed persons). This recipe covers only the mechanics of the index itself.

## Map the parish to its district for the period

1. Before searching, find the registration district that covered the parish or residence *at
   the event date*. A dated parish-to-district list such as UKBMD's shows each parish's district
   and the years it belonged there. Search older place forms too: Grimsby is listed as Great
   Grimsby, under Caistor district from 1837 to about 1897 [S7]. Districts changed over time
   [S3], [S19].
2. Search civil-registration indexes by the district name, not the census parish. A parish
   search can miss the entry completely. Barkston, Lincolnshire, for example, fell under Newark
   [S9].
3. Keep the district separate from the birthplace and the county. A district is an
   administrative unit. It can cover many parishes, can share its name with a town or county,
   and can cross county lines [S1], [S8], [S10], [S11]. Do not reject a candidate because their
   parish differs from the district name [S8]. Check the dates on any such pairing. One
   presenter's example puts an Aston parish entry under Birmingham district [S8], but Aston was
   its own district until 1 September 1924, when it was split between Birmingham North and
   Birmingham South. Before that date, Aston entries are indexed under Aston. If you infer a
   county from consistent census residence, label it as an inference and revise it once the
   entry is obtained [S11].

## Record the entry literally, then calculate an interval

1. Record each entry as found: surname and forenames, year, quarter, district, volume, page,
   mother's maiden surname (or dash or blank), age at death where shown, and which index
   supplied it.
2. A quarter is the registration quarter and is named after its *last* month: March = Jan–Mar,
   June = Apr–Jun, September = Jul–Sep, December = Oct–Dec [S1], [S5]. Some aggregators show the
   quarter by its first month. One presenter found Ancestry showing "April" for a June-quarter
   entry, and warns against rejecting a May birth because of it [S4]. Another reads a "July"
   label as Jul–Sep [S6]. Check how the collection you are using displays the quarter, and
   convert it to the GRO quarter name before you record it.
3. Turn each entry into a *possible-event interval*, never a date. Births had to be registered
   within 42 days, marriages were registered when they took place, and deaths had to be
   registered within five days unless there was an inquest [S1]. Assuming registration within
   42 days, a birth's interval starts 42 days before the quarter opens and ends when it closes.
   A December birth can fall in the next year's March quarter [S1], [S5], [S2]. This on-time
   window cannot rule out an earlier birth, even after 1875: the 1874 Act itself provides for
   registration after 42 days, after three months, and after twelve months with the Registrar
   General's written authority (sections 2, 4 and 5). Widen the interval for births before 1875.
   The Births and Deaths Registration Act 1874 came into force on 1 January 1875. It put the
   duty to register on parents and informants and attached penalties to it. Presenters report
   that registration became more consistent after that, and that births close to 1837 were the
   most likely to go unregistered [S15], [S3]. Never enter the quarter as the event date or as a
   "before" date [S2], [S5].

## Sweep for the full sibling group

1. Search by surname only (forename blank) plus the mother's maiden surname
   [S12], [S14], [S13], [S18]. The GRO search window is at most ±2 years, so each search covers five
   years. Each search also takes one sex at a time [S12], [S13]. Start at, or just before, the
   marriage year. Run consecutive, non-overlapping windows, female and male, until the end of
   childbearing. Presenters stop 25–30 years after the marriage [S14] or at the census of
   interest [S7], [S13]. Log every window: year, ±, sex, district, maiden-name spelling.
2. Presenters prefer the GRO "similar sounding" option to the broader phonetic option, which one
   finds too wide [S13], [S15]. These options have changed over time. In 2021 the GRO removed
   "similar sounding" variations for the surname field in birth and death searches, kept them
   for the mother's maiden surname, and later reinstated them. Record which option you used on
   which field, and check what the form currently offers. Rerun with maiden-surname spelling
   variants [S14], [S15]. Do not centre on a census age at ±2 and stop there. Census ages are
   often off by a year or two [S15].
3. Leave the district blank first, or widen it after a miss. Boundaries change and families
   move [S13], [S19]. When a hit falls in an unexpected district (Watford, a Hertfordshire
   district, in a Buckinghamshire family's search [S13]), check that district's coverage and
   log the entry as unresolved. Do not attach it [S13]. Accept a candidate only when surname,
   maiden surname, district and sibling spacing agree, and note how common the surname is
   locally [S13].
4. Reconcile hits with every census household. The 1911 census asked for years married and
    children born alive and still living [S12], [S17]. Families sometimes counted children from
    other unions [S12]. A child older than the marriage, or more children listed than the
    couple's count, points to an earlier marriage [S17]. A child born after one census and
    dead before the next can be missing from both censuses yet appear in the civil-registration
    indexes [S12], [S20].

## Identify the wife from the maiden-surname field

1. Compare the maiden-surname field across several children. Do not rely on one child
    [S15], [S16], [S7]. Prefer a later-born child with an unusual name, because births close to
    1837 more often went unregistered [S15]. A forename or middle name can repeat the mother's
    surname. Use it as a search lead, not a conclusion [S15].
2. A different maiden surname for the same father splits the group. It means separate
    marriages, or separate same-name couples [S17]. Presenters report men remarrying a woman
    with the same forename, so a matching wife's forename does not join the groups [S16]. With
    several same-name children in one place, the maiden surname is the main way to tell them
    apart [S15].
3. If the marriage entry gives a different surname from the children's maiden-name field,
    check for an earlier marriage. Do not assume the marriage surname is her birth name [S7].
    Co-resident in-laws are clues, but a widowed mother-in-law may carry a later husband's
    surname [S15].

## Recover children registered under the mother's surname

1. If a child, especially the first child, is missing under the father's surname, search the
    mother's maiden surname, including dates before the marriage [S19], [S21], [S20]. If no
    marriage can be found, search all the children that way. In one presenter's case the
    presumed father's surname appeared as the children's middle name [S20].
2. A dash or hyphen in the GRO maiden-name column generally means the mother's surname matches
    the child's, often because she was unmarried [S21], [S22], [S14], [S19]. It is not proof. One
    presenter warns that the parents' surnames can coincidentally be the same [S22]. Confirm
    from the entry before attaching the child [S21].

## Treat a marriage page as a candidate list

1. In marriage indexes such as FreeBMD's, everyone listed on the same quarter, district,
    volume and page is only a *candidate* spouse. The page can hold several couples, and the
    index does not show who married whom [S23]. The spouse's surname appears in the index only
    from 1912 [S6]. Before 1912, the page is your only link between bride and groom.
2. For each page, list the candidates and rule each one out using census, address and the
    children's maiden-name evidence. Take the pairing, fathers' names and occupations from the
    marriage record itself [S23], [S2]. Do not create a couple or parentage from the index entry
    [S23]. From 1912, a matching spouse surname narrows the page but still does not replace the
    record.

## Reconcile copies of one index

1. The FamilySearch "England and Wales birth registration" entries and Ancestry's
    GRO-titled index reproduce the GRO index [S5], [S7]. Match copies by district, quarter,
    volume and page, and count them as one observation, not as independent support.
2. Copies do not all carry the same fields. The GRO online birth index gives the mother's
    maiden surname, and FreeBMD and other copies may lack it [S13], [S19], [S21]. In one example,
    FreeBMD had no maiden name for a child whose GRO entry showed a dash [S21]. FreeBMD is
    volunteer-transcribed and incomplete [S21]. Its coverage starts with the September quarter
    of 1837. Rule: when FreeBMD or an aggregator finds an entry, rerun its exact spelling in the
    GRO index to get the maiden-name field or the age at death [S21], [S19]. A blank maiden name
    in a copy is not a dash.
3. Before linking a same-named death to a child, read the GRO age at death [S19], [S14].
    Searching by age needs a wide margin; one presenter uses at least five years [S14]. Also
    run the search without it, and compare the age you see.
4. A failed search is not a finding. Births before 1875, especially near 1837, are the most
    likely to be unregistered [S15]. Entries can be misindexed or indexed under another form of
    the forename [S20], or under the mother's surname. Report the windows actually searched.

## Order the certificate and close the packet

1. Order using the full reference: name, year, quarter, district, volume and page [S2], [S21].
    Without an index reference, the GRO searches only a three-year period centred on the year
    you give [S6]. Supply the reference. Take event dates, birthplace, parents, informant and
    the couple's pairing from the certificate or register [S2], [S3].
2. Return: the observation register (literal fields plus which index supplied each); the
    calculated on-time interval for each entry; the sibling sweep table (windows by sex, year,
    district, spelling, search option) with the unresolved out-of-district hits; a
    candidate-spouse list for each marriage page with the reason for each elimination; and the
    certificates to order. Proof rulings go to evidence-proof-judgment.md.
