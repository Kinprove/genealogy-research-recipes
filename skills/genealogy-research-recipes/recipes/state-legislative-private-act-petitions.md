# Recover a state legislative private act and its petition

Use this recipe when a person lived somewhere before its county courts were working, or when a state legislature granted one person or family relief by statute. Examples include legitimation, a name change, a divorce, or a gratuity or pension. In these cases, search the state's index of legislative acts, gather every entry that shares the hit's locator, and then retrieve the act and the petition or application behind it.

The most common mistakes are:

- stopping at the one-line index description or the printed act;
- ignoring other entries at the same locator;
- skipping state legislative records because the event looks like a county matter;
- assuming that a private bill in Congress that failed left no papers.

Related recipes:

- For federal military pension files, use [pension-file recovery](us-military-pension-file-recovery.md).
- For identity and kinship rulings, use [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## Decide whether the legislature is in scope

Don't rule out the state legislature just because the event sounds like county business. In early Tennessee, residents petitioned the General Assembly for private acts, including divorce, legitimation and name change. Brady explains that where a county was not yet organized, this was the only way to get the legal action done [S1], [S2]. The Acts of Tennessee index covers 1796–1850 [S2]. Brady advises anyone with family in Tennessee in roughly 1800–1830 to check it, even if the family only passed through [S1].

The legislature was not always the only route. The publisher's store description of Alan N. Miller's *[Nineteenth-Century Tennessee Adoptions, Legitimations, and Name Changes](https://genealogical.com/store/nineteenth-century-tennessee-adoptions-legitimations-and-name-changes/)*, read in a web-archive copy, reports that legitimation in Tennessee was done by private act until 1805, when the lower courts could also hear it, and that from 1827 the circuit courts could also change names [S5]. The book itself was not consulted. Use those dates as leads to check both the acts index and court minutes, and verify the applicable law before drawing a legal conclusion.

For a Pennsylvania pension or gratuity, search the session laws by surname and county, then ask for the application in the House files. In Denyse Allen’s interview, archivist Aaron McWilliams describes following an Erie County index entry for John McWilliams to a special act and then to the application [S3], [S4].

When a War of 1812 widow did not qualify for a federal pension, follow Craig R. Scott’s advice from the available excerpt and search state session laws for a private relief act and state legislative papers for her petition [S23]. Search under both the widow’s and the veteran’s names. Record the state petition and any award separately from the federal claim.

In Maryland, use Index 52 (MSA S1425) to trace state payments to the military pension roll [S24]. Search the General Assembly journals for the widow’s petition even if the payment index has no entry [S25]. Use the act cited on an index card to locate the session law [S26].

When Virginia land faced escheat, search the session laws for both the deceased and the petitioner. In October 1786 Mary Arvin obtained land left by her son John Askins, who died intestate and without issue. Reuben Coutts petitioned over a different interest: his father’s will had given him a life estate, and the reversion was at risk [S9]. Apply the descent law in force at the death. The act effective 1 January 1787 allowed inheritance through the mother and recognized paternal legitimation upon the parents’ subsequent marriage and the father’s recognition of the child [S8]. For a reported Bedford County petition over a 200-acre farm, use county, acreage and approximate date to seek the family and petition. Neill supplies neither the names nor a file reference [S10].

The Library of Virginia's Legislative Petitions collection holds nearly 25,000 items from 1776 to 1865, searchable by name, locality and keyword, and petitions can include wills, deeds, affidavits and judgments. Its search is moving to a new platform, and Virginia Memory holds the full collection [S11 @2026-11-14].

For a Revolutionary-era Virginia Loyalist claim, take the presenter's advice from the available excerpt [S16] and check Virginia legislative petitions for a possible appeal to the General Assembly [S19]. Record any Virginia legislative petition and any application to Britain's Loyalist Claims Commission as separate proceedings [S21].

Search the Library of Virginia website separately for “loyalist claims” and for the Revolutionary-era claimant's name [S20 @2026-11-18].

For any other state, search its session laws and the legislature's own petition files (in Pennsylvania, the House files), and establish when that state's county courts began operating. Don't carry Tennessee's dates over.

For a historical divorce, check the law in force in the state and year to determine whether to search session laws and legislative papers, court records, or both. Use the jurisdiction-first approach from the available excerpt [S29]. In Delaware, include the *Laws of the State of Delaware* for 1773–1897 and check court records for years when courts also had jurisdiction [S30]. Use the 1897 constitution's court-only rule to date the end of the legislative route [S31].

For an Alabama divorce governed by the 1819 constitution, follow the two-record lead from the available excerpt [S29]. Match the chancery decree to the General Assembly's sanction. Article VI, section 13 required the sanction of two-thirds of both houses before the decree took effect [S32].

For a suspected annulment, include private acts where the legislature had authority to void a marriage [S27], [S28]. Search the session laws under both spouses' names and title terms such as “annul the marriage contract.” Read the operative clause to determine what the act did [S28].

## Read the index entry and everything sharing its locator

Search by surname and transcribe the printed headings. Tennessee’s acts index separates last name, first name, year, serial, chapter and section, and description [S6]. Record each field before opening the session laws.

- the literal name;
- the year the act passed;
- the locator values, exactly as printed and labeled (in Tennessee, serial number and chapter/section);
- the description.

Separate matters filed under the same name. Stephen Cantrell’s 1796 entry concerns an auditor for Davidson Academy (serial 4, chapter/section 4.2). His 1807 entry concerns funding for state academies (14, 104.11). Neither locator belongs to the 1803 family act [S6].

For the 1803 entry on the children, copy **serial 10, chapter 50** from the TSLA headings [S6]. Brady’s method was to collect every name on the page with the same year and numbers [S1], [S2]. Use the printed act to determine each person’s role. Chapter L names eight children of Molly Cantrell, formerly Molly Blakemore: Stephen, Sally, Alfred, Ota, William, Zebulon, Polly and Darby Henley. It changes their surnames to Cantrell and makes them lawful heirs of Stephen Cantrell senior [S22].

Authored extension: Brady describes only the entries on the same index page. If the index can be searched by locator, or if the parties may be indexed under another surname (for example, the mother's), check those sections too. Record which sections you actually read. A co-indexed name is a candidate member of the group until the act or petition names them.

## Retrieve the act, then the petition or application

Treat the index as a finding aid only [S2]. Retrieve the act from the session laws. Then look for the underlying petition or application in the legislature's own files. The Pennsylvania example used the House files [S3], [S4].

For a legislative divorce, follow the petition-first method from the available excerpt [S29]. Search the petition series even when the session laws contain no divorce act. Record the claimed marriage date and place, grounds, children mentioned, and any proposed division or restoration of property as statements or requests in the petition [S33]. Connecticut's *Lotteries and Divorces*, Second Series, indexes Margaret Waldo's denied petition (September 1806–May 1807, II:108) [S33].

If a private divorce bill was introduced, follow the advice from the available excerpt [S29] and trace it through the published journals or proceedings of both chambers. Use the session and bill number or title to trace referrals, votes and the last recorded action. Capture any debate the volumes report and cite each chamber separately [S34].

When a North Carolina land grant may have followed a legislative petition, follow the presenter's suggestion from the available excerpt [S16] and look for the authorizing act alongside the petition. Start with the grant year and follow references to earlier acts [S17]. For the 1784 session laws, open volume 24 of the *State Records of North Carolina* [S18]. Choose by record type as well as by year. The *Colonial Records* (volumes 1–10) extend through 1776, and the *State Records* (volumes 11–26) also include laws from 1715 through 1776 in volume 23 [S18].

Keep a separate extraction for each document.

- **Cantrell.** Transcribe the eight children and their mother from the printed act [S22]. Brady recalled about ten children and called their mother Mary Blakemore in one talk [S1]. In another she reported that the petition supplied the children’s birth dates and the parents’ marriage date and place [S2]. Those details still require the petition image or a copy.
- **McWilliams.** Record Chester County and 1794 as the service lead attributed to the House application in Allen’s interview [S3], [S4]. Use Erie County separately to look for evidence connecting the applicant to the later resident.

Brady obtained the Cantrell petition in person in Nashville [S2]. To request that file, give the archive the act’s year, chapter and names under both surnames [S6], [S22]. Keep the repository’s reply and any petition locator beside the session-law citation.

TSLA’s petitions guide indexes subjects and names in the petition text, excluding people listed only as signers. Most petitions went back to their petitioners, and the legislature retained some. The guide gives a route for ordering an indexed record [S7 @2026-11-19]. Record which index and spellings you searched and whether you obtained the petition.

## Check the act against the application

Transcribe the reason for relief separately from the service claimed in the application. McWilliams recalled broad Revolution or Indian Wars wording in the gratuity act and described the application as evidence of Whiskey Rebellion service from Chester County in 1794 [S3], [S4]. The two recordings reproduce the same interview. Cite each original when recovered and keep the discrepancy visible.

Keep both readings, each with its locator. Don't correct the act silently. Treat a special act as relief granted to one person, whatever it says the grant was for.

For a legislative divorce, use the sequence from the available excerpt [S29] to record the petition as a request and use the enacted text to establish the grant. Check how the bill became law under the rules in force that session, including the governor's approval where required. Pennsylvania's 1844 act annulling the Hamilton marriage (no. 1, p. 9) carries the clerks' certification that it became law without the governor's signature [S28].

If a spouse returned and disputed a desertion divorce, use the lead from the available excerpt [S35] to search for a later petition to set the decree aside where the legislature could hear such a challenge. Record the original decree and the later disposition on their own dates. Require a disposition expressly setting aside the divorce before changing the family timeline. In Connecticut the Assembly granted a hearing on John Bird's second petition to set aside his divorce, then sustained the respondent's plea in abatement (*Lotteries and Divorces*, Second Series, I:30–37) [S33].

Test a same-name identification against an independent residence, tax or service record. McWilliams knew of one John McWilliams in Erie County at the relevant time [S3], [S4]. That narrows the search but leaves the link to the Chester County applicant open. For a name change, extract the legal relationship the act establishes and assess biological kinship separately [S22].

## Search Congress for a private relief bill

Search Congress for a private bill even when the Pension Bureau rejected a widow’s application, then check later sessions for a renewed claim [S12], [S13]. Sarah Maynard’s petition listed eight children with birth dates. The House committee recommended H.R. 2550 on 24 April 1872, and the Senate rejected it on 6 February 1873 (42nd Congress, Senate Report 398). Look for exhibits in the claimant’s own file. Mary E. Wainwright’s separate file contains a notarized marriage record (43rd Congress, Accompanying Papers 43A-D1, RG 233) [S13].

Find the Congress, session and bill or report number first. The Serial Set's indexes of private claims, arranged by claimant, give the Congress and session, the committee, any reports, and the number and fate of related bills [S13]. The Library of Congress reported in July 2021 that it had loaded the text of over 30,000 bills and resolutions of 1799–1873 from its Century of Lawmaking site into Congress.gov; Senate bills start in 1819, and the 12th Congress is missing [S14].

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> A request for the papers names the claimant and family; for a 20th-century bill they may be living. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before sending it.

Ask the National Archives' Center for Legislative Archives for the papers, giving the claimant, the Congress and session, and the bill or report number. It holds the claims records of Congress, House and Senate alike [S13 @2026-11-14], [S15 @2026-11-14].

## Return a legislative-act packet

Return the following:

- the index name, year, locator values (with the index's column labels) and description exactly as shown;
- the index sections inspected;
- the list of co-indexed persons at that locator;
- the act's citation and text or an extract, if found;
- what you extracted from the petition or application (birth dates or years, the mother, the marriage date and place, the county and year of service), each with the document it came from;
- a table of differences between the act and the application;
- for a divorce or annulment, the petition and bill locators, the disposition and the record establishing it, and any required court decree or later disposition, each with its own date;
- for a federal private bill, the Congress, session, bill or report number, the bill's fate, and what the committee papers contain;
- the retrieval status: online image, index only, or needs an on-site visit or copy request.

If the petition was not found, name the legislative file series you searched or still plan to search, and the extent you covered.
