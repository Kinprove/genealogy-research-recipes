# Recipe: Recover and inventory a U.S. military pension file

## Contents

- Goal
- Before starting
- 1\. Choose the record type and present custodian
- 2\. Preserve the complete index-card locator
- 3\. Obtain the intended file product and record its limits
- 4\. Inventory the pages and reconstruct the claim's sequence
- 5\. Read the claim against the act it was filed under
- Prompt pattern

## Goal

Move from a military-service clue or pension index card to the appropriate application file,
then inventory the received documents and the progression of the claim. Preserve the distinction
between an index, a service record, a pension file and an abbreviated copy product.

## Before starting

List the person's supported name forms, residence, approximate service period, branch and unit
when known. Mark a family story or uncertain service identification as a lead. Keep competing
soldiers separate. Choose a research question that the pension documents could answer.

If the man appears in the 1910 or 1930 census, read its military column before you choose a war to search. In 1910, column 30 marks a survivor of the Union or Confederate army or navy as UA, UN, CA or CN; in 1930, column 31 names a veteran's war or expedition, with CW for the Civil War and WW for the First World War [S48], [S58]. In 1910, columns 30–32 are often overwritten with tabulators' figures such as 2-1-0-0, which are not answers [S48]. Carry a marked answer into a routing row as a service lead.

This workflow concerns historical pension applications. It does not establish benefit eligibility
or replace the current instructions for requesting recent military personnel records.

## 1. Choose the record type and present custodian

1. Compare the service clue with a conflict, branch and unit. Save the source of that context;
   an unusual name alone does not establish the correct soldier.
2. Search a pension index covering the relevant service and claimant category. Do not assume
   every person who served applied for a pension. Start with the federal series below. Several
   are free online, so check them before paying for copies. The hosts are those named by NARA or
   Fold3 on 2026-11-07; check each host's current coverage. [H4–H8]

   | Service | NARA series | Online copies |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | Revolutionary War | M804, the full pension and bounty-land application files | Ancestry, Fold3, FamilySearch, NARA Catalog |
   | War of 1812 | M313, index to the pension application files | Index on Ancestry and FamilySearch; the files on Fold3 |
   | Regular service, 1783–1861 ("Old Wars") | T316, *Old War Index to Pension Files, 1815–1926* | FamilySearch |
   | Mexican War | T317, *Index to Mexican War Pension Files, 1887–1926* | FamilySearch |
   | Indian campaigns, 1817–1898 | T318, *Name Index to Indian War Pension Files, 1892–1926* | FamilySearch, Fold3 |
   | Civil War and later, Army and Navy | T288, *General Index to Pension Files, 1861–1934*: claimant names and class, application and certificate numbers | Ancestry, FamilySearch |
   | Service between 1861 and 1900 | T289, *Organization Index*, filed by state, then unit; a card can give the veteran's date and place of death | Fold3, FamilySearch |
   | Navy widows and dependents, approved claims, 1861–1910 | M1279, "Navy Widows' Certificates", by certificate number | Fold3 |
   | Navy veterans, approved claims, 1861–1910 | M1469, "Navy Survivors' Certificates", by certificate number | Fold3 |

   For a Navy veteran's own approved claim, use M1469, *Case Files of Approved Pension
   Applications of Civil War and Later Navy Veterans*, arranged by certificate number
   [H9 @2026-11-13]; Fold3 has it as "US, Navy Survivors' Certificates, 1861-1910"
   [H10 @2026-11-13].

   A widow who drew a pension on the service of more than one husband usually has a single
   file, under the surname of her most recent husband. Search the remarried widows' indexes
   under her later names: M1784 covers the War of 1812, the Indian wars, the Mexican War and the
   regular army before 1861, and M1785 covers volunteer service in the Civil War and later wars
   and the regular army after the Civil War. The index gives the claimant, the soldier and his
   unit, and the pension number, and NARA names Ancestry and FamilySearch as hosts of both
   indexes [H5 @2026-11-14].

3. Distinguish the pension application from the service record and any bounty-land application.
   Record which of these you are trying to retrieve.
4. Consult the current repository instructions before choosing a request route. NARA's older-record
   page separates service requests from pension/bounty-land requests; NATF 85 concerns eligible
   federal pension/bounty-land applications, rather than state or Confederate pensions.
5. For a state or Confederate pension lead, identify the relevant state's custodian and holdings.
   For a transferred file or uncertain repository, ask the custodian with the copied identifiers
   instead of treating a historical storage rule as a current guarantee. [S1, S3, H1, H2]

Extend the index search to the soldier's brothers, sisters, sisters-in-law, widows and other
dependent claimants, and give each of them a separate candidate row. A relative's file can hold
family Bible pages, dates, or an affidavit in which a woman gives her married name and her
relationship to the applicant. Record each affiant's stated name, relationship and date as a
second-party claim. Correlate that claim with other records; on its own it does not prove a
marriage. The [Thomsen state-records talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8i254jbCQDQ) and the
[Wilcox Patillo example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HG6sY_S-Neg) motivate this step. Neither
shows how often collateral files contain such material.

When the target has no file of his own, or his file says little about his service, also search
the files of men from his company. A man who never applied often testified for others, so search
other applicants' files for his name [S26]; fellow soldiers' declarations can describe service
they shared with him [S51]. List each witness in the target's own file, with the relationship the
papers give (neighbor, relative or fellow soldier), as an associate lead [S51]. Record another
man's account of a shared tour as that man's claim about the target.

Compare the person's age with every conflict he lived through, including regional or Indian wars,
and give each conflict its own routing row. A Confederate veteran may also have a federal file for
earlier service, and that file can be fuller than a state pension
([Derenthal Mexican War example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cLMzNfMNHic)). For a Confederate
veteran or widow, presenters describe the relevant state as the one where the applicant lived when
applying, which may not be the unit's state; verify the series' residence requirements. Establish postwar residence from other records, check each later state of
residence, and bring the unit and postwar counties to the inquiry
([Anderson overview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OcAeLJxT9XU);
[Lefler's Alabama example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tD9lgibwBRE)). These sources give only
scattered examples of acts and state rules, not a complete set, so confirm eligibility and coverage
with the custodian.

**Artifact:** A routing row: candidate ID, service evidence, conflict/branch/unit, record type,
claimant category, index/series coverage, current custodian, retrieval route and date checked.

**Check:** A service record is not a pension file. A pension-index miss describes that search,
not proof that no application exists in any series.

Where an available pension-abstract or full-text collection covers the question, search
supported target-name forms in the text as well as the claimant index. The target may appear in
another applicant's file. Retain the actual applicant/file locator and the abstract passage,
then seek that file and inspect the named person's stated role in the original document. Keep an
unresolved locator or identity open; a mention does not establish that the target applied for a
pension, served in the stated unit or was related to the applicant. The Southern Campaign
teaching example motivates this route without verifying the narrated case or promising
comprehensive coverage. [S16]

**Before you call a miss "no pension", check whether any act covered the man or his widow while
they lived** (section 5), then search the routes a federal pension index does not cover.

A man with no pension file can still have a bounty-land application. For War of 1812 service,
NARA files the pension and bounty-land applications together when the veteran applied for both,
and files the bounty-land application separately when he received bounty land but died before
he could qualify for a pension [H5 @2026-11-14], [S57 @2026-11-14]. Search that separate series
(NAID 567388) as its own route. NARA calls it only partially digitized on Fold3, where its index
is "US, Bounty-Land Warrant Applications Index, 1812-1855" [H5 @2026-11-14], [S39 @2026-11-14].
When the name is not found there, order the file from NARA (section 3) instead of recording a
miss.

For a Revolutionary War claim, M804 holds only federal applications, approved or rejected;
pensions that a state granted and paid are not in it [S26], [S34]. Under the resolution of 26 August 1776 the states examined, granted and paid
the disability pensions; the federal government took over the payments under an act of
29 September 1789, but not every state pensioner was moved to the federal roll [S25], [S27].
Fires in the War Department on 8 November 1800 and during the British capture of Washington on
24 August 1814 destroyed most of the papers of the acts before 1818; the surviving federal files
are mainly those of the 1818 and later acts [S25], [S27], [S34], [S63]. When a man was pensioned
only before 1818, a missing federal file is expected loss, not evidence that he had no pension.
Search the state's own pension records and published compilations, including Lloyd DeWitt
Bockstruck's *Revolutionary War Pensions Awarded by State Governments 1775–1874, the General and
Federal Governments Prior to 1814, and by Private Acts of Congress to 1905*, which reconstructs
such pensioners from state records and private acts of Congress [S27], [S63]. Under the act of
1818 the applicant swore his declaration before a federal district judge or before a judge or
court of record of the county, state or territory where he lived, so look for it in that
court's records as well as in the federal file [S35], [S54].

If no general act covered the claimant, or the claim was rejected, search Congress's private
claims. A veteran or widow could petition Congress directly, and Congress passed private acts
for people the general pension laws did not reach [S27], [S28], [S54]. The petitions needed the
same kinds of evidence as a pension application, so read one as you would a pension file [S28].
Search NARA's House and Senate records for them with the terms private claims, petitions and
memorials, and committee papers [S28].

## 2. Preserve the complete index-card locator

1. Open the index image when available. Copy the soldier and applicant names, service designation,
   claim category, application number and certificate number exactly as supplied.
2. Preserve prefixes, suffixes, filing dates, residence/state, transfer marks and marginal notes.
   Do not combine an application's number with another claimant's or category's certificate.
3. Keep the index collection/series, card/image locator and a saved image where permitted.
4. Compare the card's service and applicant context with the intended candidate.
5. Continue investigating an application with no certificate number or a rejection notation.
   It may still have an application file and subsequent supporting documents; do not report
   “no file” solely from an unsuccessful claim.
6. If a mark suggests transfer, carry its exact wording into a custody inquiry. This recipe does
   not assign a universal meaning or current storage location to every prefix. [S1, S2, S3]

For a Revolutionary War file in M804, read the letter before the file number first. S marks a
surviving soldier's application whose widow did not apply; W a widow's application, filed alone
or with the soldier's own; R usually a rejected claim but sometimes a Virginia veteran's claim
for half pay for life; and BLWt a bounty-land warrant application [S26], [S53]. A W file can therefore hold the soldier's own declaration, and an R does not prove that
every claim in the file failed: many files under the Virginia act of 5 July 1832 were numbered R
although the claims were not rejected [S25], [S26].

A pension index can exist in more than one hosted version. The versions may be arranged
differently and may carry different fields, such as a death date or place or a dependent's name.
Before ordering, check each version. Give each version its own locator row naming its series, and
note the fields it adds or contradicts. Do not merge the versions' fields into one card. Record a
miss separately for each series and search route. Where a version is arranged by state, unit,
company and surname, browse the candidate's company as well as searching by name. Transcribed
names, units and dates can be wrong, and a regiment may be named several ways. The
[Family History Fanatics regiment project](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P9g7JwVmnIM)
([part 10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8h90dEdVBYc)) motivates this step. It does not
establish each version's coverage or accuracy, that browsing is complete, or that a site's
arrangement stays the same.

When another index-card version or series is actually available for the same supported claim,
compare its image with the first card. A later file identifier may appear on one version and be
absent on another; the interview in S14 illustrates this with an XC number. In each version's
locator row, keep its available filing/transfer dates and number, preserving both images
where permitted. Carry an additional identifier into the custody check without overwriting the
earlier application/certificate locator. An unresolved mismatch remains open; a missing field on
one card does not establish that no later identifier exists. Do not assume a particular website
always supplies the fuller card or that a prefix establishes the file's present location. [S14]

Read a card's claimant details as they stood at that filing. For the illustrated card
series, presenters describe the state as where the claimant lived when filing, not the enlistment or
unit state; verify the series' field definition, then treat a different state as a migration lead ([Findmypast walkthrough](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Wo-M7iIWj78)). The widow
named on the card is the claimant, not necessarily the wartime wife. Take the marriage date from
the file and check for earlier wives
([regiment spreadsheet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cpulm6BuAJ8)). When a card shows both a
veteran's and a widow's claim, copy both numbers and try the widow's certificate number too,
because the papers tended to be combined under it
([Martinez overview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mRt2jTgEqVw)). If a widow remarried, search
again under each later surname and link the new locator rows. A roll entry that only notes an
application is a pointer ([Thomsen Georgia example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8i254jbCQDQ)).
These are tendencies and single examples, not rules for every war, state or index.

**Artifact:** An index-locator table with a separate row per candidate and claim/category.

**Check:** The request identifies the intended claimant and application. An index card supplies
a locator; it does not substitute for reading the application documents.

If an application or certificate number is unreadable, check whether a separately arranged
index covers that service and claimant category. Use supported regiment/company and applicant
context to find a candidate; reconcile the claim category and application-versus-certificate
fields before using its number. Preserve both index locators and literal readings with the
resolution basis; unresolved digits stay unreadable. The
[historical regiment-index teaching example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dSVVQE7iVgo)
supplies this recovery route, not a verified matched-card pair or current hosting/coverage
guarantee. A second index can resolve a locator without independently confirming its genealogy.

## 3. Obtain the intended file product and record its limits

1. Check whether the result is only an index image, a selected extract, or a multi-image
   application file. Inspect its description and image sequence rather than its search-result title.
2. Compare multiple apparent file matches against the locator table; a name can produce more than
   one candidate file.
3. If ordering eligible older federal pension records, use NARA's current pension request
   instructions and the identifiers you copied. The order form is NATF Form 85, which covers
   federal service from 1775 to 1903, not state or Confederate service. Its product choices
   distinguish a complete application file from a Pension Documents Packet containing selected
   document types. NARA's fee page, read on 2026-11-07, lists:
   - complete pension application file, pre-Civil War: $55;
   - complete pension application file, Civil War and later: $80 for up to 100 pages, then
     $0.70 a page, with a quote first;
   - Pension Documents Packet: $30;
   - bounty-land warrant application file: $30.

   A compiled military service record is a separate order on NATF Form 86 ($30). A land entry
   file is ordered on NATF Form 84 ($50). [H1, H4]
4. Choose the product that fits the question. For a question requiring the claim's progression
   or witness material, request the complete-file option rather than silently substituting the
   smaller packet.
5. Record the selected product, request identifier, submission date, custodian and any page limit
   or remaining-copy instructions in the actual order. Check current terms instead of carrying
   a video's historical price or delivery estimate into the request.
6. When copies arrive, record what was received and any stated omissions. Follow up on a
   disclosed page limit or incomplete delivery when the missing material affects the question.
   [S1, H1]

**Artifact:** A retrieval receipt: file identifiers, product requested, online/download/request
route, request date/ID, expected scope, received files/images and disclosed limits.

**Check:** “Complete” in a product name is not your image-count or delivery verification.
Record the actual package and unresolved omissions. Do not claim a request was sent unless it was.

When identifiable prior copies, dated notes or internal references describe particular papers,
compare them with the newly received package. Queue each unmatched item with its earlier
locator or observation basis and its current retrieval status. Keep absent testimony unrecovered;
a note about it does not recreate the document. The [Sarah Cook re-retrieval example](https://dna-explained.com/2023/01/09/sarah-cook-1774-1775-1863-epitome-of-perseverance-52-ancestors-386/)
motivates this comparison without establishing misfiling or the missing paper's contents.

## 4. Inventory the pages and reconstruct the claim's sequence

1. Read the received file through once, including covers, administrative notes, rejected claims
   and reconsideration material.
2. Give each received image a stable ID in its original order. Group connected pages into
   documents while preserving every image's original locator.
3. Record the document type, date, creator, applicant and named people; distinguish an asserted
   relationship from your interpretation.
4. Create a chronological view of the documents without renaming away the original order.
   Track applications, supplied proof, objections, decisions and later actions when present.
5. Transcribe relevant passages with uncertain readings marked, and check them against the image.
6. End with a claim-action timeline, source-linked leads and explicit gaps. Send identity,
   relationship or conflicting-evidence conclusions to the evidence/proof workflow. [S1, S2]

On an affidavit that does not state where the affiant lived, copy the venue line and the county
named in the notary's or justice's certificate. A notary usually acted only within the county of
his appointment [S49], [S59]. Record that county as where the affiant was on the affidavit's date,
and as a residence lead only: it does not show that he lived there [S59].

In a Union invalid pension file, expect a series of dated examining surgeons' certificates, not a
single examination. The act of 14 July 1862 required invalid pensioners to be examined every two
years, some were examined yearly or twice a year, totally disabled pensioners were exempt, and an
examination could reduce a pension or remove it from the roll [S40], [S56]. Until 1882–83 a single
physician examined in most counties and boards of three or more in larger cities; from then a
board of three examined every claimant [S40]. Give each certificate its own row in the inventory
and the claim-action timeline, and read the series for changes in the rating.

For a Revolutionary War veteran's postwar household, look for a property or income schedule
submitted to demonstrate need, together with its accompanying household statement or letter.
Build a dated household-and-means row: named people, stated relationships and ages, residence,
occupation, ability to work, assets, debts and income, recording only fields actually supplied.
Keep the applicant's statement separate from a clerk's or witness's assessment. Such papers can
name family members not individually named in a contemporary census return; retrieve the pension images
before treating an article's extract as an inspected original. Preserve the declaration's
need-supporting purpose: its valuations are reported claims, and the listed household is not a
complete list of the veteran's relatives. [S17, S18, H3]

Never read a filing date as a death date. Take death dates from a document that states them, such
as a drop report, a widow's declaration, or a later widow's proof that an earlier marriage had
ended ([Lisson's death-date session](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bc92E8Oq4zU)). Record such a
date as the claim's statement; it may be approximate or second-hand. A date dropped from the rolls
is not automatically a death date. A widow's late application may reflect a later change in
eligibility rather than a recent death. Do not name a statute unless the file or an authoritative
source does ([Thomsen overview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C2_9AVcodcg)). Treasury final
payment cards and ledgers form a separate series from the application file. They may give the
paying agency, the act, payment dates and a death. Cite their own locator and confirm the series
citation with the custodian
([Strauss Revolutionary War talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nJsQ9ftU9iM)). None of these
sources sets the proof requirements of any particular law.

The Treasury payment ledgers for the acts of 1818 through 1858 are NARA's T718. They are
arranged by act, then by pension agency, then roughly by the first letter of the surname, and
give each pensioner's allowance, the date payments began, a mark for each payment, and remarks
such as a death or a transfer to another agency; payments were usually made twice a year, in
arrears [S29], [S60]. Build a dated row for each agency from the payment marks and transfers. It
places the pensioner by the agency that paid him, not by an exact residence. Treat a transfer as
a lead for a move, not proof: in NARA's worked example the pensioner moved in 1819 but his
transfer was entered only in 1826, and in 1831 his pension passed automatically to the newly
created Albany agency [S29]. Compare the agency with residence and burial evidence before you
call a transfer a migration [S54]. Letters the Pension Office sent can survive outside the file,
in its Letters Sent, 1800–1866; some volumes are indexed by addressee, so search the names of
people who wrote on the pensioner's behalf as well as his own [S29], [S53].

FamilySearch has the T718 images as "United States, Revolutionary War Pension Payment Ledgers,
1818-1872"; despite the title, the volumes include widows and pensioners of later wars
[S32 @2026-11-14], [S60 @2026-11-14].

Follow a death or last-payment remark to the final payment. Under the act of 6 April 1838, heirs
could claim from the Treasury the pension accrued between the last payment and the death, and
those settled accounts often give the date and place of death and the heirs' names [S29], [S55].

Fold3's "US, Final Payment Vouchers Index for Military Pensions, 1818-1864" indexes final and
last payments to more than 65,000 pensioners of the Revolutionary War and some later wars. Each
slip gives the paying agency, the act, the date of payment and the death date, and a star marks a
voucher that survives among NARA's selected final payment vouchers [S29 @2026-11-14],
[S31 @2026-11-14]. Only the Delaware and Georgia vouchers are imaged on Fold3, and NARA says the
series is closed for digitization [S30 @2026-11-14]. Cite the index slip and the voucher as
separate items.

When "Invalid" appears on the jacket of a military service record, read it first as the noun for a disabled or infirm soldier and look for evidence of a claimed service-related injury or disability; taking it to mean "void" can wrongly suggest that the enlistment or record had no force. [S20], [S21] The Legal Genealogist traces this usage in American service from the Revolution through Civil War pension forms. [S20] Confirm the reading from the injury or disability evidence in the record. [S20], [S22]

Treat the service described in a pension file as what the claimant chose to declare, not as a
complete military history. Search federal and state records separately for other regular,
volunteer, militia or opposing-army enlistments. Link any you find only after comparing identity
evidence, and do not guess why service was left out
([Strauss regular-army example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M72-nL_Yb2I);
[Confederate-then-Union example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CPSlx8VfKps)). Conversely, if an
index lists the candidate under a unit that a published roster omits, keep the candidate and mark
the conflict unresolved. Record whether the claim appears allowed or rejected, and check further
service sources ([regiment project](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P9g7JwVmnIM)). An application
alone does not prove service, and a gap in a roster does not disprove it.

If a Continental soldier or officer is linked to a corps of invalids under the 26 August 1776 resolution, read that as placing the man among those entitled to a disability pension but found capable of guard or garrison duty, and do not assume that an "invalid" was unfit for any further service. [S20], [S23], [S24] Do not date the eight-company Corps of Invalids from that resolution, because it came from a later one of 20 June 1777. [S23], [S24] Do not infer a man's actual duties from the resolution alone. [S20], [S23], [S24]

**Artifact:** Two linked tables:

| Table | Fields |
| --- | --- |
| Image/document inventory | Image ID, original order/locator, document ID, type, date, creator, reading status |
| Claim-action timeline | Date, action/decision as written, claimant, linked document IDs, named-person leads, unresolved question |

**Check:** An early rejection may be followed by further submissions or a later decision.
Read the received sequence before describing the outcome; never turn missing pages into an inferred
final disposition.

## 5. Read the claim against the act it was filed under

Record the act each application, certificate or ledger entry names, then read that act's terms
before you interpret the claim. The terms say what the claimant had to prove. Read a first
application filed soon after a new act as a sign that the act made the claimant eligible, not as
new need or a new disability, unless the papers say otherwise [S52], [S61]. A first pension in
1832 or later is not a sign of short or doubtful service either: the man may not have qualified
under the stricter earlier acts [S52]. Take the terms from the statute or from a summary of the
acts themselves, as the tables below do, not from a talk or a general history. The tables give
the main acts; the act the file names governs.

### Revolutionary War

| Act | Who qualified |
| --- | --- |
| Resolution of 26 August 1776 | Officers, soldiers and sailors disabled in service, at half pay; the states examined, granted and paid the claims until the federal government took over payment under an act of 29 September 1789 [S25], [S27] |
| 24 August 1780 | Widows and orphans of officers who died in service, at half pay for seven years; not the families of enlisted men [S25] |
| 18 March 1818 | Nine months' service, or service to the end of the war, in the Continental Line or the Navy, and need; militia service did not count [S25], [S35] |
| 1 May 1820 | The 1818 pensioners had to file certified schedules of income and property; those found not in need could be struck off [S25] |
| 7 June 1832 | Six months' service in the Continental Line, state troops, militia, navy or marines; full pay for two years or more, less for shorter service; no test of need or disability [S25] |
| 4 July 1836 | The widow of a man who qualified under the 1832 act, married before his last service ended [S25] |
| 7 July 1838, extended in 1843, 1844 and 2 February 1848 | A widow married before 1 January 1794 [S25] |
| 29 July 1848 | A widow married before 2 January 1800 [S25] |
| 3 February 1853 | No limit on the date of the widow's marriage [S25] |
| 9 March 1878 | The widow of a man who served 14 days or was in any engagement [S25] |

Read the bracket for the widow's marriage date from the act she applied under, and take the date
itself from her proof. A widow pensioned under the 1853 act may have married the veteran long
after the war and need not be the mother of his children [S25], [S52], [S63].

Read a rejected Revolutionary War claim as a failure of proof under the act, not as evidence
that the service was false. In a random sample of 34 rejected federal claims, just over half of
the soldiers' rejections were for good cause, most often service short of the act's minimum (nine
months under the 1818 act, six months under the 1832 act); widows' claims failed mainly on proof
of the marriage date [S26]. First total the tours the applicant proved against the act's minimum
[S63]; then look for the stated reason in the file or in *Rejected or Suspended Applications for
Revolutionary War Pensions* (1852) [S26]. Some rejections were the Pension Office's own errors,
such as requiring a militiaman's name on a roster when its regulations exempted militiamen [S26].

### War of 1812

| Act | Who qualified |
| --- | --- |
| Before 1871 (the "Old Wars" series, indexed in T316) | Men disabled by their service, and the widows of men who died of it [S33] |
| 14 February 1871 | 60 days' service and an honorable discharge, for men who had not supported the Confederacy; widows married before the peace treaty took effect on 17 February 1815 who had not remarried [S36], [S38] |
| 9 March 1878 | 14 days' service or any engagement, and an honorable discharge; their widows, with no limit on the marriage date [S37] |

Most War of 1812 files come from the 1871 and 1878 acts, and most of their applicants were
widows or minors [S33]. A veteran who was not disabled by his service and died before 1871 could
not get a pension; search for his widow's claim and for bounty land (section 1) [S50], [S51]. If a
widow qualified only under the 1878 act, compare her marriage date with 17 February 1815 before
looking for another reason [S36], [S50].

### Union Civil War and later

| Act | Who qualified |
| --- | --- |
| 14 July 1862 | Men disabled by a wound received or a disease contracted in service and in the line of duty; for a death from such a cause, the widow, then children under 16, then a dependent mother, then dependent orphan sisters under 16 [S64] |
| 27 June 1890 | 90 days' service, an honorable discharge and a permanent disability, not caused by vicious habits, that prevented earning a support by manual labor; widows with no other support than their own labor, married before 27 June 1890, without proof that the death came from service [S43] |
| 5 August 1892 | Women employed as army nurses for six months or more, honorably relieved, and unable to earn a support [S44] |
| 3 March 1901 | A widow who was his wife during his service, was pensioned for a death from service and was dropped for remarrying, restored when widowed again or divorced on her own application without fault on her part, and left with no other support than her own labor [S65] |
| 6 February 1907 | Age 62 or over, with 90 days' Civil War service (or 60 days in the Mexican War) and an honorable discharge [S45] |
| 19 April 1908 | Widows of men with 90 days' service and an honorable discharge, married before 27 June 1890, with no test of support [S46] |
| 8 September 1916 | The 1908 widows' pension extended to widows married before 27 June 1905; widows dropped for a later marriage, restored when widowed again or divorced on her own application without fault on her part [S47] |

Read the date and type of each Union application against this table before treating a late or
repeat claim as new illness or hardship; many veterans moved from their earlier invalid pensions
to the 1890 act because it paid more [S41], [S56]. Read a denied claim, such as an application
number with no certificate number, as one that did not qualify under the law in force when it was
filed, not as evidence that the man did not serve, and order it as you would an approved file
[S62]. Search the indexes under a woman's own names for a claim as a nurse, not only as a widow
or dependant [S44], [S56].

A continuing Union widow's pension with no remarriage record does not show that she lived alone.
Remarriage ended a widow's pension, the Pension Office reported widows who lived with a man
without marrying for fear of losing it, and it could not end a pension for that cohabitation
[S40], [S56]. From 7 August 1882, open and notorious adulterous cohabitation ended a widow's
pension from its start [S42]; look in a later file for an investigation and a termination. Where
no marriage record existed, the Pension Office accepted evidence of cohabitation and general
recognition as husband and wife as proof of marriage, so read the witnesses' testimony before
treating a missing marriage record as no marriage [S40].
