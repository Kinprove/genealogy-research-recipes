# Take in, capture and hand over an inherited family collection

Use this recipe when you help take in, inventory, plan the capture of, or hand over an inherited family collection. That covers boxes, albums, loose photographs, papers, and scans someone has already made. It applies to a physical collection, a capture plan or existing scans, and it never requires you to see or handle the originals. The common mistake is to optimize for tidy files. The model renames files by person, re-orders items, scans fronts only, imposes one resolution or format, treats a received crop or a stitched assembly as the master, and assumes an export kept the captions. Each of those destroys part of the record that later identification depends on: who held the item, the order it arrived in, its reverse, and the surfaces nobody has inspected yet.

This recipe covers intake, capture and hand-off only. It prescribes no conservation treatment; it does set handling limits for capture.

- Other recipes cover the next steps. Family photograph attribution and dating covers identifying and dating the people in photographs. Family papers and Bible register dating covers dating letters, papers and Bible entries. Family interviews and oral tradition covers recording relatives' memories.
- To transcribe a hard-to-read inscription, or to judge a restored, enhanced or colorized image, use [AI for documentary records](ai-for-documentary-records.md).
- Identity rulings and other disputed conclusions belong in [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).
- Cite items with [source citation](source-citation.md). Record sessions in the [research log](research-documentation.md).

## Bound the assignment and record what arrived

1. Work with the requested subset. Keep a record of where it sits in the larger collection, but do not expand the assignment.
2. Before reorganizing, record what arrived, who supplied it, when and how it was received, and who holds the originals when known. Keep a short collection note alongside the files. Link the source email or the relative's note rather than silently paraphrasing it into established facts. An unknown creator, holder or date stays unknown [S1], [S2].

If the user asks which papers to keep, check for an accessible library copy or digital equivalent before marking a book or record copy as replaceable, according to the podcast's discussion with Lisa Alzo [S50†], [S53], [S54]. Give priority to handwritten notes and family narratives [S55]. Inspect books and record copies for family annotations or loose notes before recommending disposal. Keep that material with its source.

## Give every item a stable reference tied to its place

Give items stable references that connect their physical location and received grouping to their files. Preserve album and page order, and link individual captures to their parent page. Capture every page in sequence, blank pages included. If a folded item opens safely, capture it closed and opened, and link both views to the same page or item. Do not lift or remove adhered material to finish the sequence [S3].

Keep existing files and the received order while preparing the packet. Do not detach mounted items, dismantle albums or prescribe conservation treatment. When access would require intervention, record the affected surface as inaccessible and continue with the material that is safely available. An inaccessible reverse is not a blank reverse [S3], [S4].

When the work moves on to photograph, papers or interview work, connect it back through these same stable item references.

For a small task, an item reference, source and location, the files with their roles, the original's dimensions and format when accessible or stated, and a gaps note are enough. Record the original's physical dimensions and format whenever the original is accessible or its holder states them: a digital file's pixel size does not reveal the original's size, and size and format help date a photograph. Never infer object size from a resized image, and never ask anyone to handle a fragile original merely to fill a field. Add album position or capture settings when they are relevant [S2].

## Keep each statement about an item separate

Keep four kinds of statement distinguishable: a received caption, a relative's account, an observed object detail and an interpretation [S1], [S2], [S5].

Never compare faces, and never assign identity by appearance. When the user or a relative recognises someone, record that recognition as their attributed claim, together with its basis. Resemblance is never evidence by itself.

## Plan capture from the media actually present

Inventory the media and their observed condition before proposing capture [S4]. Record the equipment actually available, the intended reuse and the settings chosen for each material, rather than one resolution or file format for everything. Match the method to the object:

- **Flat prints in sound condition** can go on a flatbed [S6], [S13]. Never put an old photograph through an automatic feeder [S6].
- **Mounted prints, and prints that are curled, brittle, cracked or flaking,** go under a camera on a copy stand, with nothing pressing on them. Do not flatten them or roll them backwards [S13], [S16].
- **Cased images** (daguerreotypes, ambrotypes and tintypes in cases) are photographed with a camera, not laid on a flatbed. Open a case gently if it opens freely, but do not force the hinge, take the image packet apart, or remove or clean the cover glass; that is work for a conservator [S13], [S14].
- **Glass-plate negatives and glass lantern slides:** never press on the glass, and hold a plate by two opposite edges. A sound plate can go emulsion side down on a flatbed with a transparency unit, with nothing on top of it, or under a camera over a light source. A cracked, broken or flaking plate is not captured until a conservator has stabilized it [S13], [S14], [S15].
- **Slides and negatives on film that is not nitrate and not deteriorating** need a scanner built for film and slides [S6].
- **Film that may be cellulose nitrate** stays out of routine capture, on a flatbed and on a film scanner alike. Nitrate film was made from 1889 into the early 1950s, and acetate film overlapped it from the 1920s. Only Kodak published end dates. Amateur roll film was often unmarked, and a copy can carry its original's "NITRATE" edge printing. So, except for dated Kodak film, neither the film's date nor its edge marking settles whether it is nitrate [S17], [S18], [S19]. Nitrate is a fire risk: keep suspected nitrate apart from other material, record it, and leave its copying to a person or company trained to handle nitrate [S17], [S18].
- **Film that smells of vinegar** (deteriorating acetate) is kept apart from other film, because film in good condition absorbs acid from it. The smell shows that the decay has already begun [S18], [S19].

**Bound volumes** (a family Bible, diary, ledger, scrapbook or bound album) are captured open only as far as the binding opens without force. Never flatten one for capture, whether face down on a flatbed or under a lid or glass: that pressure can break the spine or loosen brittle pages [S27], [S28]. Rest a volume with weak joints or a restricted opening in a book cradle of polyethylene foam blocks or rolls, under capture equipment that adjusts to its height [S28]. The Library of Congress lists a binding that opens less than 90 degrees as a condition problem [S28]: capture what that opening shows and record the hidden gutter as inaccessible.

**Loose paper.** Capture fragile or valuable originals one sheet at a time. Use a flatbed only when the sheet can rest safely and completely on the glass. Use an overhead camera when flatbed capture would require pressure or leave part of the sheet unsupported [S56], [S39]. Reserve automatic feeding for expendable, sturdy, unfastened sheets of similar size and paper stock. If both sides contain information, use a feeder that captures both sides or capture the sides individually. Check that both sides appear in the resulting files [S39].

**Flatbed settings for a print's master.** Capture the print as it is. Turn off the scanning software's automatic correction and sharpening, and leave a margin around the print rather than cropping to its edge; straighten, crop and retouch only a derivative [S24], [S25], [S26]. Save the master as TIFF, not JPEG, which holds only 8 bits per channel; keep JPEG for working and sharing copies [S25], [S26]. Set resolution by print size, so that a smaller print gets more: 300 ppi for 8×10 inches or larger, 600 ppi for about 6×4 inches, and 1200 ppi for 2×3 inches or smaller [S25]. Scan a black-and-white print that may need restoration in full colour at 16 bits per channel [S24], [S25]. The Library of Congress's 2014 basics select grayscale for black-and-white photos [S26]; use grayscale only where no restoration is planned.

Handle each item with clean, dry hands or clean nitrile gloves, by its edges, without touching the image [S20]. Do not use rubber bands, paper clips, staples, sticky notes, tape or glue on photographs, and do not laminate them [S20], [S21]. Label the enclosure rather than the photograph. If a paper print must be marked, write lightly in soft pencil on the back near the edge, never on the front [S16], [S20]. A glossy resin-coated print does not take pencil: if one must be marked, use a waterproof, non-acidic archival pen instead [S16].

Gloves are for photographs and film, not for paper. Handle letters, documents and bound volumes with clean bare hands, washed and dried before you start [S28], [S29]. The Library of Congress names photographs, film, metal and ivory as the materials that need gloves; on paper, gloves dull touch and make handling clumsier, and for rare books they are generally not recommended [S29], [S30]. Wear clean gloves for a volume that contains photographs or has metal or ivory decoration, and impermeable gloves whenever mould is suspected [S29], [S30].

If suitable capture is unavailable, leave an explicit next action. Do not mail originals; arrange copying where the original is, locally or at the holder's home [S6].

## Capture every accessible surface and the page around it

For loose photographs you can safely reach, capture front and reverse and bind both to one object reference. Copy inscriptions literally and keep them separate from your interpretation. Mark each side as missing (not present), uninspected (reachable but not yet examined) or inaccessible (reachable only by intervention). For an album whose items are stuck down, such as a magnetic album, capture the whole page rather than lifting items, and keep that full page beside any individual crop. Save the last item or page actually completed, and the views still remaining, so another session can resume [S3], [S4].

When a print is stuck to an album page and a phone is the available camera, photograph it in place. Keep the phone parallel to the page and check the edges in the preview. If a wide-angle view bends the edges or the phone casts a shadow, step back and use a longer optical setting if the phone offers one. Use even, diffuse daylight or daylight-balanced light and avoid mixing light sources. If the page still reflects light, move the light or tilt the supported album and realign the phone. Use any locally saved scan-mode crop or perspective correction only as a working copy and retain the full page [S67], [S68], [S69].

## Keep received files, derivatives and versions apart

Keep the full initial capture or received file. Make crops, resized copies and edits as separate derivatives. If a supplied image is already enhanced or cropped, describe it as received; do not claim that an untouched master exists. Record the source and role of each file. A new format extension cannot recover detail that the input lacks [S2]. An authored display or scrapbook page is also a derivative. It does not replace the source photograph, and it does not establish who is depicted.

For a new scrapbook, mount prints made from the photo scans and print document copies on acid-free paper or as photographic prints. Keep the originals in their enclosures and record which source item each print came from [S74], [S75].

## Bind the parts of an oversized item

If an oversized item is captured in portions, bind each part to the item and to the position it covers. Keep the parts beside any assembled working derivative. At the joins, inspect text, lines, color and retained edges for continuity or missing extent. Record any unresolved seam instead of calling the assembly complete or lossless [S9].

## Look for a missing item under another association

If a known photo, document or recording is missing from the expected person's view, check its associations with the spouse and other close relatives first; a document missing from the expected profile may have been attached to the spouse’s profile [S10]. Then inspect a collection-wide media list if one is available, using the item's retained object details. Mark the item either as found under another association or as not located, and never record one as the other. An association does not establish the identity of the person depicted or recorded.

## Record what a recipient actually agreed to take

When preparing to return a found album, record the named recipient, giver, place and date from the dedication under the album's reference. Start with the named recipient in historical records [S40]. Use the dedication as a lead to the album's history and keep the identities of individual sitters unresolved until supported [S41].

If historical records show that the album recipient's direct line ended, trace a sibling's branch for a possible family handover [S40]. A documented relationship identifies a candidate for the handover. Keep the question of authority open and follow the [Living people rule](../SKILL.md#living-people) when the trail reaches anyone who may still be living.

In the United States, a signed deed of gift transfers ownership of the physical or digital items. ## Return

Close with what was captured, how to find its source, the actual stopping position and the remaining gaps. A readable scan establishes neither an identity nor complete coverage.

The packet holds:

- **A collection note:** what arrived, who supplied it, when and how, who holds the originals, and links to the supplier's own messages. Unknown values stay marked unknown.
- **An item register**, one entry per stable reference, with:
  - physical location, received grouping, and parent page or position
  - each file and its role: initial capture, received file, derivative, part, or a version made before or after an alteration
  - the status of each surface: captured, missing, uninspected or inaccessible
  - literal inscriptions, kept apart from any received caption, relative's account (with who gave it), observed detail and interpretation
  - the original's physical dimensions and format when accessible or stated by the holder; album position and capture settings when relevant
- **Version notes:** for an altered original, each copy's date and maker and whether it predates the alteration. For embedded scan fields, who set them and whether a date is an estimate.
- **Assembly notes** for oversized items: the parts, the position each covers, and any unresolved seams.
- **A capture plan with a progress marker:** media and condition, equipment and settings for each material, items set aside for a specialist (suspected nitrate film, damaged glass plates), explicit next actions, the last completed item or page, and the views remaining.
- **Location results:** each missing item marked as found under another association or as not located.
- **A handover record:** the recipient, what they agreed to take, any exclusions, what the deed of gift says about copyright, and the material still unresolved.
- **The hand-off check result:** which delivered item was opened, what it led to correctly, which fields the export dropped, and where those fields are preserved instead.
