# Interpret a historical Canadian census entry as a de jure listing

Use this recipe when a historical Canadian census line is about to be used as evidence of where someone lived, whether they were alive, who was in the household, or their marital status. Also use it when a name search of a Canadian census, such as 1931, misses a household and you have to browse the images by geography. The default mistake is to read a Canadian listing the way you would read a "present on census night" listing. For year-specific U.S. counting rules, double enumeration and district carry-over, use [U.S. census workflows](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/us-census-practical-workflows.md). For stated statuses and merge conflicts in general, use [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## Record the enumeration basis before reading the line

For any census from 1871 onward, write on the observation that the enumeration was de jure. People were listed where they were supposed to belong, as reported by an informant, not where they physically were at a cutoff time. Both presenters stress this as the critical difference from de facto systems, and Ep. 203 contrasts it with the English census [S1], [S2]. The 1852 census (1851 series) is the exception. It mixed de facto and de jure counting and included visitors. Do not apply the presence, absence or duplicate consequences below to 1852 without first checking that year's rules, and read a listed visitor there as someone physically present at that household. Name the informant if the record does. Otherwise mark the informant as unknown.

Before reading an 1871-or-later entry as a home, check whether the instructions placed it where the person was found. In 1931, families or persons in temporary residence, such as summer residents, were entered on the regular schedule with an asterisk in the left margin, and an Absentee Family Card credited them to their fixed home. Read an asterisked entry as the place the person was found, not a reported home. Treat an ordinary household entry as a reported legal residence and nothing more. Do not conclude any of the following from it:

- **Presence.** A wife could list a husband who was a thousand miles away looking for work, or who never meant to come back [S1], [S2].
- **Being in Canada.** Obee says a person living abroad can still be counted at the home address [S1].
- **Survival on census day.** A family that did not know about a death could still list the person [S1], [S2].

The Ep. 203 guest says the residence rules let a wife report a husband who had left up to 25 years earlier. He also says she might have lost contact with a man who had since remarried, emigrated or died [S2]. Cite the 25-year span as his claim, not as a rule.

## Apply the de jure consequences decisively

- **Duplicates:** expect one man to be listed twice, at the family home (Nova Scotia in the presenter's example) and where he actually was (Alberta) [S2]. Log the two lines as a possible duplicate enumeration of one person, not as two people. Hand the merge decision to the evidence/proof recipe linked above.
- **Earlier death record:** never reject an earlier death certificate because the person turns up in a later census. The Ep. 203 guest describes making exactly that mistake with his own family [S2]. Record the census line as a report by the household, not as an observation of a living person.
- **Absence:** the Ep. 154 guest, talking about a New Brunswick family, says the Canadian census lists "the people who should have been there", that overnight visitors do not appear, and that not finding a man in the household does not mean he wasn't living there [S3], [S4]. For 1871 onward, apply this as the default, because it follows from de jure counting: an overnight visitor is not listed in the household he was visiting, and a usual resident missing from his own household was left out by the informant or the enumerator; the omission does not establish whether he was away on the night. Do not turn an absence into non-residence. The documented exception is 1931, where the instructions (§§61–62) put a temporary resident on the regular schedule with an asterisk. Read an asterisked line as a person found there, not a household member. Do not apply this bullet to 1852, which included visitors.

## Check form wording and timing for that year

- **Marital status options:** never infer a spouse's death from "widowed" alone, in any year. The informant may have been wrong or out of contact [S2]. Read the field options on that year's form before interpreting the answer. The Ep. 203 guest says a couple of census returns had no "divorced" option, so a divorced person had to be entered as widowed [S2]. He does not name the years. If the form for your year has no divorced option, or you have not checked it, record "widowed" as possibly standing for divorce.
- **Bilingual headings:** compare the French and English column headings. The same guest translated every French heading and found slight differences in wording in several years, so the two languages could be asking different questions [S2]. Write down both wordings for the field you are interpreting. If they differ in a way that changes the meaning of the answer, record the answer as ambiguous between the two readings. Do not pick one.
- **Instructions:** look for that year's enumerator instructions. The Ep. 203 guest also points to newspaper notices printed before an enumeration, which explained rules such as de jure counting [S2].
- **Remote and northern districts:** if the entry comes from a northern or remote district that enumerators reached rarely, flag the observation with a possible timing gap and check the printed census reports for that district. Obee says such areas might get one visit a year. Information there could be taken about two months before or after the official date, so a person interviewed in person might have died by census day [S1]. Take the population and age reference date from that year's instructions. For 1931 it is 1 June 1931, and a late visit does not admit a child born after that date.

## Browse by title-card geography when the name search misses

Since 1 June 2023 the 1931 images have been public, and at launch they could only be browsed. The presenter's workflow comes from that period [S5]. A free name index has since been added to Library and Archives Canada's Census Search, built in partnership with Ancestry and FamilySearch, and Ancestry and MyHeritage have their own indexes. Search those first. Use the browse below when the index misses a household you have good reason to expect. A missed name search does not show the household is absent. For index and search-scope failures on a platform, use [Ancestry search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/ancestry-record-search-recovery.md).

1. **Earlier census location.** From the nearest earlier census, record:
   - enumeration sub-district number
   - municipality
   - township, range and meridian
   - street address, if the household was in town

   The presenter used the 1926 census of the Prairie Provinces (Saskatchewan, Alberta, Manitoba), searched through Library and Archives Canada's census search tool [S5]. Also note who the neighbours were.
2. **City directory.** Check a directory for the target year to confirm the address [S5]. For how to read directory editions, use the [city-directory recipe](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/city-directory-observation-workflow.md).
3. **Start with the earlier number, but do not trust it.** Districts were redrawn as population grew. In the presenter's example, the household's 1926 sub-district number 8 covered neither the same municipality nor the same township in 1931 [S5]. A miss in the reused number does not mean the household is missing.
4. **Step through title cards.** The first image of each sub-district is a title card showing township and municipality [S5]. First check the sub-districts on either side of the old number, since the area may not have shifted far. Then, if there is still no match, open every sub-district in order from number 1, which is how the presenter worked, and look for a township-plus-municipality match [S5]. That key fits Prairie records with numbered townships, as in the presenter's Regina example. Elsewhere, match the locality, ward, parish or street address that the title card and schedule actually give. Log every card you check, including partial matches (right township but wrong municipality, or the reverse), and keep going [S5].
5. **Plausibility screens.** For a promising sub-district, the presenter:
   - looked at image 2, the first page of residents, and compared the neighbourhood's make-up with the earlier census's neighbours
   - checked the township column against the recorded number
   - measured distances between the known street and the street names on the pages
   [S5]

   These screens only decide where to keep looking. A neighbourhood that matches by ancestry or a nearby street does not identify anyone.
6. **Locate and cite.** Find the entry and cite the sub-district and image number. In the presenter's example the exact match was sub-district 11, image 18 [S5]. Then read the entry under the de jure rules above.

## Return an annotated observation

For each census line, return:

- the literal entry and locator
- the enumeration basis (de jure for 1871 onward; mixed for 1852)
- the entry type: an ordinary household entry or an asterisked temporary-resident entry
- the informant (named or unknown)
- the form's option limits for any interpreted field, plus both heading wordings where they differ
- any remote-district timing caveat
- a possible-duplicate check against other provinces or households
- for an ordinary household entry, a plain statement that the line does not establish presence, survival on census day or residence in Canada

For a browse, also return:

- the name searches tried and where
- the log of title cards checked
- partial matches
- the screens applied
- the final sub-district/image citation

If no title card matched, report the sub-districts covered. Do not report the household as absent.
