# Recover Dutch civil acts and the church register kept before civil registration

Use this recipe when a Dutch person or couple is missing from an online index, when a civil marriage has to be carried back to the church baptism registers kept before civil registration began (1811 in most of the country), or when a compiled tree supplies a father, a death date or a sibling set that the original entries may not support. It covers the ten-year tables, marriage supplements and separate contracts, choosing a church register, and the roles of dates in Dutch church registers. For an intention to marry (ondertrouw, banns) compared with the marriage itself, and for testing a reused given name, use [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md). For AI extraction of event types, use [AI for documentary records](ai-for-documentary-records.md). For FamilySearch Catalog and film navigation, use [FamilySearch workflows](familysearch-practical-record-workflows.md).

## When the index fails, open the ten-year tables

A failed search on WieWasWie or Open Archieven (openarchieven.nl) does not show that the event is missing from the municipality's records. Search both indexes and the municipality’s ten-year tables (tienjarige tafels). The regional archive identifies incomplete civil coverage, and Hoitink describes archives represented in only one portal [S19], [S20]. In a film spanning 1812–1842, Robertson used the headings to jump between decades, event types and surname groups [S3]. Record which sections cover the date and municipality you need.

- Identify the sort key and copy both spouses in the order written. In one marriage table the groom appears surname first and the bride given name first [S3]. Search the groom’s surname column before interpreting a miss for the bride.
- Copy the date under its heading and use it to retrieve the full act. The regional archive describes dates of registration in the ten-year tables and warns specifically that both births and deaths may have occurred earlier [S19]. Even when the two dates coincide in an example [S3], transcribe the event date from the act.
- Go to that year's register for that event type, browse to the date, and read the full act. Several candidates with the same name can turn up. Keep each one as a separate candidate until the full act ties it to your family. [S3]
- If the event may fall outside the block you read (for example a death), repeat the search in the next decade's table. Record which blocks and event groups you read. [S3]

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: third-party-tool --> MyHeritage receives the deceased person's name and any death date or place entered. Keep relatives' details local.

For a death in Zuid-Holland in 1900–1942, use MyHeritage's *Netherlands, South Holland, Civil Deaths, 1900-1942* (collection 21023) [S28 @2026-11-20] [S29 @2026-11-20]. Check the collection's list of civil registration offices. Search by the deceased's name and any known death date or place. Compare the indexed birth year, death date and place, and the parents' and spouse's names where present [S29 @2026-11-20]. Follow a candidate to the full civil act. If the office is unlisted or the search comes up empty, continue in the municipality's death tables and register.

A miss can also mean that the act is not yet public. Throughout the Netherlands, birth acts become public after 100 years, marriage acts and their supplements after 75 years, and death acts after 50 years [S19]. The Zeeuws Archief gives the same terms and says registers with recent acts cannot be inspected [S12]. For an act still within its term, a miss in the public records says nothing about whether the event happened.

## Once civil registration starts, use the marriage supplements to reach the church

Date the start of civil registration by region before you choose between civil and church records. Do not treat 1811 as one national date:

- **Zeeuws-Vlaanderen:** 1796. Registration became official on 1 July 1796, and separate civil registers began on 22 September 1796. Entries made before that date went into confiscated church registers, the provisionele burgerlijke stand (provisional civil registration). [S12], [S13]
- **Limburg:** 1796 in most of the province. Check the first year of each Limburg municipality's registers. [S14]
- **Vlissingen:** 1 April 1808. [S12]
- **Everywhere else:** 1811. [S12] Take the first date of each municipality's own birth, marriage and death registers before you choose between civil and church records; they can start months into 1811. Schiedam's first civil marriage act is dated 23 June 1811, and its births and deaths start a month later. [S15]

The civil marriage act names the couple's parents and places of origin. The marriage supplements (huwelijksbijlagen) hold the proofs each spouse had to file: a birth or baptism proof, parental consent where it was required, and death proofs for parents who had died. If a spouse was born before civil registration began in the birthplace, the proof is a baptism extract. That extract names the church and its denomination, which tells you which register to search next. [S2]

When tracing a person from a civil act into a church register, search the given name with the recorded surname, then repeat with the patronymic in place of the surname [S30], [S31]. If the person's patronymic is missing, use the father's given name from the civil act or marriage supplements to form a candidate. Try both searches in early civil acts too. Those acts can record the patronymic alone or alongside a fixed surname [S31].

Check the municipality's name-adoption declarations (naamsaanneming) for both the old and adopted names. Compare the family members and any children's ages in the declaration with the church and civil entries before linking them. If the declaration omits the old name, use the family details to test the church-register candidate. [S32]

Inventory each marriage supplement by person, issuing authority, document type and date [S2]. Choose the consent rule by place and date. Under the French Code civil, a son under 25 and a daughter under 21 needed their parents' consent. Older children asked their parents’ advice by a formal acte respectueux, or their grandparents’ if both parents had died or could not express their wishes. Without consent, sons under 30 and daughters under 25 repeated the request twice at monthly intervals [S16]. The Dutch code took effect on 1 October 1838 and in Limburg on 1 January 1842 [S18]. It required parental consent for minors (then under 23) and required adults under 30 to request it [S17]. Read the document itself before assigning an age or relationship.

Hoitink's table of Dutch ages of majority gives 23 for 1838–1901, 21 for 1901–1988 and 18 since 1988 [S24]. So a spouse of 21 or 22 was of age after 1901; for a marriage in the first years after 1901, check which age applied on the marriage date.

## Check separate contracts and guardian agreements

A `huwelijkscontract` or `huwelijkse voorwaarden` is a prenuptial agreement, a separate target from the marriage act and its `huwelijksbijlagen`. Check the locality's finding aids for the relevant notarial or, where applicable, voluntary-court series. Known banns and wedding dates give a starting search window, not a reason to exclude other dates. Hoitink describes this route; the actual series must be established for the place and period. [S6]

If a surviving parent remarried with minor children, also ask whether a separate agreement with the children's guardians survives. Keep it distinct from the couple's contract and ordinary guardianship appointments or accounts, each with its own locator. This is Hoitink's document lead, not a claim that an agreement was legally required or created in every case. For the wider guardianship search, use [probate case inventories](probate-case-document-inventory.md). [S7]

For a death from 1806 on, also look for the memorie van successie (estate-tax return) in the series of the tax office that covered the person's last place of residence. Record the years that office's series covers: the Zuid-Holland series runs 1806–1927, and other series differ. [S22], [S23] Heirs in the direct line paid no tax until 1878, so a return filed before then can be short. [S22], [S23] If no return turns up, record the result as "not found in that series".

## Choose the register by denomination and place

Look up the town in W. Wijnaendts van Resandt’s *Repertorium DTB* or the local archive’s inventory of baptism, marriage and burial registers. Check the edition’s abbreviation key. In Robertson’s demonstration D marked baptisms and T marriages. A place cross-reference can take you to the town under which the book is described [S2]. Copy the denomination, years and reference before opening images.

Search both the home town and the church that served it. Robertson recalled relatives travelling elsewhere for Catholic ceremonies and was unsure of the destination [S2]. The Leiden Catholic baptism book in the Nationaal Archief inventory (3.04.16.073, no. 1, 1623–1629) includes children from Katwijk aan den Rijn, Noordwijk and other places [S25]. Use the register’s stated coverage to search in either direction, then check the entry for the family’s actual residence.

Put the Mennonite (Doopsgezind) and Walloon Reformed registers on that list too, before you call a church-period event missing. Walloon Reformed registers are written in French, so read them with French record terms. [S8], [S10]

## Give each church-register date its own role

- **Burial:** the date in a pre-1811 burial register is usually a burial date, not a death date. Many entries are really fee records (lists of amounts paid for the burial). Some entries do also give the date of death. Copy a death date only when the entry states one, and never put a burial date in a death field. [S2]
- **Household-head search key:** if a married woman is missing from a burial or burial-fee book, also search under her husband's supported name; for a minor child, try the father's. Hoitink describes entries filed under the household head rather than the person buried. Read the actual entry and keep an unnamed wife or child unassigned until other evidence identifies the person. [S5]
- **Unnamed child:** an entry such as "a child of [father]" often gives only the father. A baptism followed soon after by a child burial is an assumption, not an identification. Keep the burial unassigned unless other evidence names the child. [S2]
- **Marriage:** the date in a church marriage register is usually the ondertrouw date (when the couple registered their intent to marry), not the wedding date. Read the register's heading or introduction, then label the date "ondertrouw". Use "proclamation" only when the heading or introduction says the entries are banns postings, and "marriage" only when the entry records the ceremony. [S2]
- **Baptism:** a baptism date is not a birth date unless the entry records both. Typical gaps give an estimate only. Catholic baptisms usually took place within one or two days of birth, often on the day of birth. Reformed baptisms usually took place on the following Sunday, and in some northern provinces up to several weeks later. Record a birth date estimated this way as "on or before [baptism date], estimated", never as a calculated birth date. [S2]
- **Mennonite baptism:** Doopsgezind congregations baptized only adults, so their baptism date is an adult baptism. Never use it as a birth or infant-baptism date. Find the birth in another source. [S8], [S9]

## Different surnames do not rule out siblings

When siblings have different surnames, reconstruct the household before the eldest married or left home. Check each sibling’s patronymic and any name-adoption declaration (naamsaanneming). Married children could already be using a different surname [S1]. The regional archive dates many registers to 1812/13 [S19]. Castillo’s Groningen city demonstration showed 1812 and 1826 under the Catalog subject “Names, personal” [S1]. Check the specific municipality and later rounds. In nearby Haren eight declarations survive from 1826, including Albert Jans taking Van Dijken [S26]. Use the names and children actually written in each declaration to test the link.

A patronymic kept as a second name can point to the father's given name. These end in -sen, -szoon, -szen or -se for men and -dochter or -dr for women. Treat this as a search clue only, never as proof of parentage. [S1]

## Read recognition wording before accepting a father

If compiled trees agree on a father, transcribe the act’s wording for the unmarried mother, the child and the person making any recognition (erkenning). In the recorded search participants debated an abbreviated word and a father repeated across several trees [S4]. The act was not independently read for this example. Obtain the complete entry, any marginal recognition and the marriage supplements for both spouses before assigning a father. Record a legal acknowledgment in the role the document gives.

## Search a couple as two people, then check the spouse

For Willem Kool and Neeltje Noordzij, try a paired search using the prefixes Wil (Willem or Wilhelm), N for Neeltje and Noordz before the variable ij/ei ending. Leave the patronymic blank and sort by date [S3]. Check each person’s role before copying an event. In the Charlois marriage index for 26 July 1848, Willem and Neeltje appear as parents of the groom, Cleis Kool. The entry points to Rotterdam City Archives, archive 2, inventory 545, act 1848.13, folio 16v [S27]. Retrieve that folio to verify the index.

Add a child's baptism, a death or a marriage to the couple's events only when the record ties it to that couple. If the record names a spouse or mother, it must be the same person; a different name excludes the record. If it names no spouse, as many baptism and burial entries name only the father, keep the event as a candidate until other evidence ties it to the same couple. Several men in one town may share a name. Treat a given name reused after a child's death as a hypothesis, and test it as the proof recipe describes.

## After the population registers, request the CBG card

Most municipalities began a bevolkingsregister (population register) in 1850, and some kept one earlier; take the first year from the municipality's inventory. [S21] After the bevolkingsregisters, many municipalities kept gezinskaarten (family cards). Persoonskaarten (personal record cards) replaced them from 1939. [S11]

- **Death between 1939 and 1 October 1994:** the CBG holds the persoonskaart of almost everyone who died while registered in a Dutch municipality. [S11]
- **Death after 1 October 1994:** the CBG holds the persoonslijst (personal record list). [S11]
- **Where to ask:** request an extract from the CBG, not the regional archive. The cards and lists cannot be searched online. [S11]
- **Gaps:** for a death between 1939 and 1 October 1994, a person who had emigrated is not in this register. A Dutch national who died abroad after 1 October 1994 can be in it as a non-resident registration, even after emigrating; a miss in those lists is not a negative. Grown children who had already left home in 1939 are often missing from their parents' card. [S11]

## Return a recovery packet

Return the following:

- the ten-year table blocks and event groups you read, with what each table date means (act date or event date)
- each act locator (municipality, register type, year, date, act number if shown)
- a supplements inventory
- separate marriage-contract and guardian-agreement locators, with their document roles kept distinct
- the repertorium entry and the church and denomination you chose, including any neighbouring-town registers you searched
- every church date labelled with its role (burial/fee, ondertrouw/proclamation, baptism, estimated birth)
- name-adoption register findings
- the civil-registration start year used for the place, and any CBG card or list requested
- the recognition wording, quoted literally
- the questions still open: which spouse is meant, which child is buried, and who the father is
