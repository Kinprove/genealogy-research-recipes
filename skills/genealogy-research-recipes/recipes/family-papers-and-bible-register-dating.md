# Separate writing, mailing and event dates in family papers and Bible registers

Use this recipe when a date, place or hand on an inherited letter, card, envelope, address book, family Bible register or inscribed book is about to go into a timeline, a tree or a proof argument. The common mistake is to read every date and place on these papers as an event fact: a postmark as the date of the news in the letter, a return address as a birthplace, the earlier entries in a Bible register as written when the events happened, the latest address-book entry as one dated snapshot, and an inscription in another copy of the same book as dating the family's copy. Each of these records something else: a mailing, the sender's address at that time, a later copying or recollection, a keeper's layered updates, or a different object.

This recipe covers what these dates, places and hands establish, and how to record them so that writing date, event date and copy stay apart.

## Letters and address records

### Letters, cards and envelopes

1. **Keep a letter or card bound to its envelope under one item reference.** Note whether that pairing was received or reconstructed later. When a saved card was still in its original envelope, the presenter in [S1] read its postmark, its return address and the address it was mailed to.
2. **Record the postmark, return address and delivery address separately from dates and places in the text** [S1].
   - A postmark dates the mailing. It is the latest possible date for the writing and for any event the text reports as already past, but it does not date those events: a birth, death, visit or move reported inside a card needs its own date [S1].
   - A return address is the sender's stated address at that time and a lead to where that branch of the family lived. It is not an emigrant's birthplace. In [S2], letters from relatives in Mexico to cousins in the United States are recommended because their return address can locate the family.
3. **Record whether pages, signature or date are present.** An unsigned item's author stays unknown. Do not take it from the recipient's later spouse or from family assumption. In [S3], three pages of an unsigned 1910 love letter survived. The researcher identified the addressee, found her marriage later that year, and judged the husband to be in the right place at the right time to have written it. Months later the final page turned up folded in another file, and it was signed by a different man.
4. **If a separated page turns up, link it to the same item reference.** Record where it was found and the textual continuity supporting reunion [S3].

### Address books and card lists

1. **Transcribe each entry with its page position.** Include struck-through text, arrows and added names, dates or addresses in visible order. Treat them as layers of the keeper's updates rather than one dated snapshot [S4], [S6]. A card list may be a single sheet kept with the holiday cards [S4].
2. **Treat a replacement address or surname as an undated lead** to a move, a correction or a name change. In [S4], the keeper marked out an old address and wrote the new one beside it, and marked out a maiden-name entry and added a married name.
3. **Do not read a cancellation as a move or a death.** A cancellation does not say whether the person moved, died or was dropped, nor when. In [S5], one keeper marked out both people who had moved and a person who had died.
4. **Read a heading as the keeper's name for that person**, not necessarily the person's current surname. A speaker in [S6] kept a remarried relative's updated entry under the surname she had always called her by.
5. **Keep a relative's explanation as an attributed account.** Do not assume who made the edits.
6. **Treat listed people as associate leads, not established kin.** One presenter says you have to work out who is a friend and who is family [S4]. Another says people appear in the book because of some relationship, whether or not they are related [S5].

## Family books and Bible registers

Begin with the book's stable item reference and received provenance from the Family collection intake and capture recipe. Distinguish this object from comparison copies and from transcriptions of it. Inspect every surface that can be viewed safely, including the endpapers and the back of the title page, on the object or on a supplied image; record any surface you cannot view as uninspected.

### Read the register entry by entry

1. **Record the imprint or title-page date** [S7], [S8].
2. **Record each entry separately:** stated event date, apparent hand, ink or medium, and position [S7], [S9].
3. **Entries for events before the printing were written later, from another record or from memory** [S7], [S8]. **A multi-generation run in one hand and ink points to batch copying: check for it, and decide each entry's writing occasion separately** [S9]. Copied entries point to an earlier register: look for it in other branches of the family, and record it as documented only when a document names it. In [S8], a Bible with an 1853 flyleaf date belonged to a couple married in 1854. One page carried births from 1756 to 1848 in what the presenter read as one hand. She later found a document naming an earlier family Bible held by the husband's father.
4. **A claimed owner who died before this copy was printed cannot have started it.** One whose recorded events merely predate the printing may have acquired it later and copied them in; check the hands [S8].
5. **Keep each entry's apparent writing occasion apart from its event date.** Many hands make recording near the events more likely [S7]; they do not prove it.
6. **A relative's attribution of a hand stays attributed.**
7. **Keep transcriptions and online copies separate, with transcriber, sender, poster and holding branch.** In [S7], a transcription signed as "true and accurate" still carried a date that other records contradicted. In [S10], an online-tree item posted in 2017 carried a Bible transcription made by a genealogical-society officer and sent to the poster by a third person. The Bible itself had come down through a different branch, the eldest son's line.

### Edition, binding and inscriptions

1. **Separate the work's first publication, this edition or copy, any later alteration, and a claimed acquisition or inheritance** [S11].
2. **An inscription in another copy shows only that its edition existed by that date.** It does not date this copy, its acquisition or its owner.
3. **The same cover design on several copies indicates a publisher's or trade binding.** It does not identify who rebound this copy, or show that it was rebound at all.
4. **Keep the family's account and a seller's description apart from what this copy shows** [S11].

In [S11], the family copy's title page carried no date, and the work was first published in 1858. A copy offered for sale with the same title page had a 1909 inscription. The author took it as support for a much later acquisition, "perhaps 1909". Photographs of other copies showed the same etched cover design. Under this recipe the 1909 inscription shows that the edition existed by 1909; it does not date the family's acquisition. The shared design points to a commercial cover; it does not say who rebound the family's copy.

## Return

- **For each letter or card:**
  - the item reference
  - whether the envelope pairing was received or reconstructed
  - the postmark, return address and delivery address as separate fields
  - the dates and places in the text
  - whether pages, signature and date are present or missing
  - the author, as named on the item or marked unknown
  - any reunited page, with where it was found and the continuity supporting it
- **For each address book or card list:**
  - entry transcriptions with page position and visible layer order
  - each later address or surname flagged as an undated lead
  - cancellations recorded without a reason
  - listed people recorded as associate leads
  - relatives' explanations attributed to them
- **For each register:**
  - the imprint or title-page date
  - an entry table: stated event date, apparent hand, ink or medium, position, and apparent writing occasion (later copy or recollection, consistent with near-event, or undetermined)
  - any earlier register, recorded as documented (with the document) or not yet documented
  - every copy, with transcriber, sender, poster and holding branch
- **For each family book:**
  - a short item-linked comparison: proposed claim, this object's observation, identified comparison copy, supported date or range, and remaining gap
  - the next missing surface, edition or provenance question
  - original images and received descriptions, kept beside the comparison
