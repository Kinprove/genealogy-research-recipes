# Trace an enslaved ancestor through the British colonial slave registers and compensation records

Use this recipe when a person of African descent is documented in a British colony after emancipation and the next step is the slave registers of 1813–1834 and the compensation records of 1833–1842. It works back from the owner's population at emancipation, not forward from the first register. It prevents these failures:

- searching the registers by estate name or by the family's later surname and reading a miss as "no record"
- reading a changes-only return as a full list, and an absence from it as death or sale
- matching a person alive after 1834 to a same-named entry in the first full list without checking the later returns
- treating the person named at the head of a return as the owner, and missing returns made by attorneys, executors, guardians, trustees and receivers
- searching the compensation index for an enslaved person's name
- assuming one colony's years, columns and full-list years hold for another
- linking people by surname, or inferring a father from a shared surname or a shared estate

## Inputs to gather first

- the earliest record of the person after the colony's emancipation date (§ 2): baptism, marriage, burial or later civil record, with its parish, district or estate and the age given
- the colony and parish as written at the time, and the present-day name
- every estate name, owner name or "belonging to" phrase attached to the person or the family
- the mother's name, and any second name, "alias" or home name
- the colony's register years and which of them are full lists (§ 1)

## 1. Fix the colony's register years and mark each return full or changes-only

Registration began in Trinidad in 1813 and reached most Caribbean colonies by 1817; Anguilla's registers start only in 1827 [S13]. An 1819 Act set up the Office for the Registry of Colonial Slaves in London, which received copies of the colonial registers. These copies are The National Archives series T 71, 1813–1834 [S10]. The years differ by colony [S13]:

| Colony | Register years |
| --- | --- |
| Jamaica | 1817, 1820, 1823, 1826, 1829, 1832 |
| Barbados | 1817, 1820, 1823, 1826, 1829, 1832, 1834 |
| Trinidad | 1813, 1815, 1816, 1819, 1822, 1825, 1828, 1831, 1834 |
| Demerara-Essequibo | 1817, 1820, 1823, 1826, 1829, 1832 |
| Berbice | 1818, 1819, 1822, 1825, 1828, 1831, 1834 |
| Antigua | 1817–18, 1821, 1824, 1828, 1832 |
| St Lucia | 1815, 1818, 1822, 1825, 1828, 1831, 1834 |
| St Vincent | 1817, 1822, 1825, 1827/8, 1830/31, 1834 |
| Tobago | annual, 1819 to January 1834 |

- **Mark each return as a full list or a change return before reading it.** A colony's first register is a full list. Later returns list only increases and decreases since the previous return: births, purchases and transfers in, deaths, sales, transfers out and other losses [S13], [S1]. In one Jamaican estate's papers, the 1823 and 1829 returns list only changes to the 1817 registration [S15].
- **Barbados has full lists in 1817, 1820 and 1834.** Its 1823–1832 returns give increases and decreases only [S13]. In Anguilla the first and last registers are full lists [S13]. A few owners elsewhere filed their whole population in a change year [S1].
- **Absence from a change return means no change.** A person who stayed with the same owner from 1817 to 1832 appears in Jamaica's 1817 list and in no later return.
- **Do not carry Jamaica's columns to another colony.** Jamaica's returns give name, age, colour, origin (African or Creole) and remarks, usually the mother [S15], [S1]. Trinidad's returns carry more columns, including occupation and physical description [S1]. A Bahamas return dated 31 July 1834 gives age in years and months, colour, origin and the work done [S8]. Read one full return of the colony before you search it.

## 2. Anchor at emancipation, then rebuild the owner's population at the last return

- **Place the anchor record against the colony's emancipation dates.** In the British Caribbean, emancipation began on 1 August 1834. Children under six were freed that day, and older people became apprentices bound to their former owners until 1838 [S12], [S18]. Antigua and Bermuda gave immediate freedom without apprenticeship [S18]. At the Cape, emancipation came on 1 December 1834 and apprenticeship ended on 1 December 1838 [S23]. In Mauritius, apprenticeship ran from 1 February 1835 to 1839 [S24].
- **Find a child born after the last return in the compensation claims.** No register lists the child. The original claims and certificates record such births (§ 5).
- **Read baptisms and marriages of the apprenticeship years in full.** An apprentice's entry can name the former owner or master, which leads straight to the returns [S2].
- **Start from the last full list where one exists.** In Barbados that is 1834 [S13].
- **Elsewhere, rebuild the owner's population at the last return.** Take the first full list, add every later increase and remove every later decrease. The ancestor must be someone still in that population. A same-named person in the first list who later appears as a death, sale, transfer or manumission is excluded. In one Jamaican query, a person alive into the twentieth century had been matched to an 1817 entry whose later return records that person's death [S1].
- **Test age progression.** Each return should add the years since the previous one. A man baptised in 1820 "aged about 44" cannot be the man aged 42 in an 1832 return of the same estate. The researchers treated the 1832 man as a younger relative, and that kinship stays a hypothesis [S3].
- **Keep every same-named candidate.** One owner's population often holds several people with the same name [S1]. Carry them all until mother, age, colour and origin separate them.

## 3. Search under whoever made the return, not only the owner

- **Index by owner and by maker.** Most registers are indexed under owners or plantations [S10]. Some indexes give only the person who made the return, not the owner [S11].
- **Read the header of each return.** It names who made the return: the owner, or a representative acting for the owner or a dead owner's estate. The representative can be an agent; a planting attorney, who managed an estate for an absentee owner and was not a lawyer; a trustee under a will; an executor; or a receiver appointed in a property dispute [S1]. The registers of Grenada and Jamaica also name guardians [S13].
- **Expect colony differences.** Barbados returns usually state the maker's capacity, such as attorney or executor. Tobago returns name the registrant, not the beneficial owner. Virgin Islands registrants are people to whom the enslaved "belonged" or by whom they were "possessed", so a lessee can appear [S13].
- **Bridge a change of representative.** A return's header can name the previous receiver or attorney, which tells you whose name to search in the earlier years [S1].
- **Include town households and free owners.** Free people of colour made returns as owners [S6], [S4]. Town owners of one or two people made returns too: a Kingston return of 1817 lists a single person [S4].
- **Search every name.** List each owner, joint owner, attorney, executor, guardian, trustee, receiver and lessee linked to the estate across 1817–1834, and search each one in the owner field.

## 4. Bridge an estate name to its owner

- **Do not search Jamaica by estate name.** Most Jamaican register entries do not name the estate [S13]. Find the owner of the estate first, then search under the owner and the representatives [S1].
- **Use the Legacies of British Slavery estates database.** Search by estate name, colony, parish and division. An estate record lists the people linked to it, each with a role (owner, attorney, mortgage holder, executor, trustee) and a date range, the yearly numbers of enslaved people, and a link to the compensation claim [S14]. It also cites each register entry by T 71 piece and folio, for example "T71/1 222-223" for 1817. Go straight to those folios.
- **Check the match.** The same estate name occurs in several colonies and parishes, spellings vary, and some links are marked as tentative [S14]. Filter by colony and parish, and carry a tentative link as a candidate.
- **Use Jamaica's almanac lists.** The Jamaica Almanac printed lists of proprietors and properties by parish, taken from the "givings-in", and these are transcribed online. An almanac reflects the givings-in of the previous March quarter, so date its observation to the year before the edition [S14]. In Montserrat, annual Poll Tax Acts list owners and numbers of enslaved people [S13].
- **Use an estate named in a baptism.** An 1820 St Elizabeth baptism describes a man as "the property of" a named plantation. An estate map gave its owner, and a search of the 1832 register under that owner found entries of the same surname [S3].

## 5. Use compensation records for claimants, counts and details missing from the registers

- **Know what was paid and to whom.** The 1833 Act paid £20 million to owners [S18]. Nothing went to the people freed [S1]. The Slave Compensation Commission met from October 1833 to the end of 1842 [S10].
- **Find the claim in the LBS database.** It names each claim by colony, parish and number, for example "Jamaica St Catherine 550". Each claim gives the number of enslaved people, the amount, the claimants and whether the claim was contested [S14]. The database also works as an index to the compensation returns in T 71 [S11].
- **Know where the originals are.** T 71 holds valuers' returns, registers of claims with indexes, original claims and certificates, counter-claims, adjudications in contested cases and lists of awards [S10]. The National Debt Office's payment registers (NDO 4) give claimants' names and agents' signatures. AO 14 holds further lists of awards [S12], [S10].
- **Search the compensation index by claimant, then read the original claims and certificates.** Find the claim through the claimant's name in the LBS database [S11]. The claim forms record numbers and values by type of work [S12]. The original claims and certificates, signed by the claimant, add details missing from the registers: children born after the last registration, usually with the mother's name [S21]. Read the counter-claims as well, for details on enslaved people that predate the registers [S21].
- **Use the claim for three things.**
  1. It shows who held the people at emancipation.
  2. Every claimant and counter-claimant, such as a mortgagee, executor or heir, is a name to search in the register indexes.
  3. Its count tests your rebuilt population.
- **Compare counts at their own dates.** Count the births, deaths and transfers between the last return and the claim before you diagnose a missed return, a split holding or the wrong owner. One Nevis estate registered 179 people in 1834 and was compensated for 181, because two children were born in the seven months between the last register and the claim [S22].

## 6. Follow each change entry to its other side

- **Follow transfers in.** Increase entries record births, purchases, inheritances and transfers in. A transfer can name the seller or the estate the people came from. In one St Andrew estate's return of the early 1820s, a group of 30 to 40 people entered "by vendition" (by sale), and the seller is named [S1]. Search the seller's returns for the same people as decreases, then follow them back there.
- **Do not read "runaway" as a removal.** A man noted in an 1823 return as having run away was still carried as a runaway in the same owner's 1832 return [S4].
- **Treat "manumitted" as a lead to a deed and an identity check.** Registers mark people as manumitted [S6]. In one Jamaican case, a man was called free in an 1818 baptism yet listed as manumitted in an 1820 register. The panel flagged that the two entries had not been shown to be the same man [S6]. Look for the manumission deed in the colony's deed records.
- **In Barbados, read the deed volumes in order.** The Barbados deed volumes are handwritten transcriptions made in the late nineteenth century. They run mostly in date order, with batches out of sequence that refer back to earlier sections [S20]. The deeds are not digitised, photography is not allowed in the archive, and the indexes miss entries [S5]. Read the volumes for the period page by page, in person or through a local researcher.
- **Search both Trinidad series.** Trinidad has two volumes for each year, "Plantation Slaves" and "Personal Slaves" [S13]. A person missing from one may be in the other.

## 7. Read alias entries and baptism names as two names of one person

- **Read the alias form.** Early returns record mostly single names [S1]. An entry such as "Rose alias Jane Duncan" usually gives the name used before baptism and then the baptismal given name and surname [S1]. Other forms occur: "William Seal, also called Cornwallis" [S15]; "Good Luck alias Richard" [S4]. Record both names, search both in every return, and take the surname form to the baptism registers.
- **Do not expect the given name to survive baptism.** A woman entered as Sarah could be baptised Rebecca Jones [S1]. Baptismal names may not match the register names [S15].
- **Expect group baptisms to stay mostly unlinked.** People from one estate were often baptised in a group [S1]. In 1819 a Westmoreland rector baptised 101 people "belonging to" one estate. Only 8 could be linked conclusively to its 1817 return, and 22 possibly [S15]. Link a baptism to a register entry only on estate, age and mother together.
- **Browse the Jamaican baptism images.** FamilySearch's "Jamaica, Church of England Parish Register Transcripts, 1664-1880" holds images of the transcripts with handwritten indexes. It is only partly indexed, so browse the parish volume. The originals are at the Registrar General's Department in Spanish Town [S16]. Baptisms of enslaved people name the estate or owner [S3], [S15].
- **Carry a surname as a lead only.** Surnames came mainly through baptism, and after that often from the mother. Some owners gave a whole estate one surname, and people also took names familiar to them [S1]. One man's children took either half of his two-part name as their surname [S1]. A planter with the family's later surname tells you whose returns to read. In one example, a two-year-old Creole child appears under such a planter in the 1826 Jamaican register [S7]. The register shows the owner; it does not prove where the surname came from.

## 8. Link by mother, age and origin, not by surname or a presumed father

- **Follow the mother line.** Jamaica's remarks column usually names the mother, because legal status passed through the mother [S1]. In one estate's 1817 return, 51 of 101 identified entries name a mother [S15]. From a child's entry, find the mother's own entry, then her mother's. This works only while each woman stays in the same owner's population [S1]. When she leaves it, follow the transfer (§ 6).
- **Do not infer a father.** The registers record the mother because the law needed her, and rarely record a father [S1]. Two men of one surname at one estate in 1832, aged 42 and 14, were proposed as father and son; nothing in the return states it [S3]. Record co-residence as co-residence.
- **Match on the record's own descriptors.** "Creole" means born in the colony and "African" means born in Africa [S15], [S3]. A Creole child of an African mother is common: one return lists a 22-year-old Creole man as the son of an African woman, both held by the same owner [S4]. Use origin, colour and age as linking fields, recorded literally.

## 9. Use the newer online copy and search the owner fields

- **Use the 2026 Ancestry collection.** The older collection, "Slave Registers of former British Colonial Dependencies, 1813-1834", can be searched by enslaved person's name, year of birth, owner, colony and sometimes parish [S11]. It comes from black-and-white microfilm and stops at 1834 [S1]. Some T 71 volumes are not on it [S11]: it lacks Bermuda, the Cape of Good Hope, Montserrat and parts of Guyana [S1], [S2]. The newer collection, "Former British Colonies Enslavement Registers", covers 20 colonies from 1813 to 1840, with nearly 4 million records on about 300,000 pages [S17]. It has colour images and more indexed fields, and needs only a free Ancestry account [S1].
- **Search as Ancestry's presenter does.**
  - Put the owner or representative in the owner field, not the general name fields, and sort the results by role.
  - Put variant spellings of a place that no longer exists, in quotation marks, in the keyword field.
  - Use the landing-page map to browse a place when you have no name [S1].
- **Browse the images.** Look at whole returns to learn the layout before trusting index hits [S1].
- **Use the names of the time.** Search Demerara, Essequibo and Berbice (now Guyana), St Christopher (St Kitts), Honduras (Belize), Ceylon (Sri Lanka) and the Cape of Good Hope [S1], [S13].
- **Map Jamaica's old parishes.** Jamaica cut its parishes to 14 in 1867, so register parishes often no longer exist [S19]:
  - St Dorothy, St John and St Thomas in the Vale became part of St Catherine.
  - Vere became part of Clarendon, and St David part of St Thomas.
  - St George was split between St Mary and Portland, and Metcalfe went into St Mary.
  - Port Royal was split between Kingston and St Andrew.
- **Go behind a missing image.** The Kew volumes are London copies of registers kept in each colony [S10]. When an image is missing or unreadable, search the T 71 catalogue by colony and parish [S11]. Then ask the colony's archive whether its own register survives. For the Cape, the registers are mainly in the Cape archives as well as in T 71 [S9].

## Close with a register packet

Return:

- **Anchor row:** the post-emancipation record, its locator, the parish then and now, and the owner, estate or "belonging to" phrase it gives.
- **Holder chain:** for each return year, the owner, the maker of the return and the maker's capacity, with the source of each link (LBS estate or claim, almanac year, header text).
- **Return rows:** colony, year, full list or change return, T 71 piece and folio or image link, and the entry exactly as written: name, alias, age, colour, origin, mother, remark.
- **Change events:** each increase or decrease with its cause and its other side (seller, buyer, source estate, deed), or "not yet found".
- **Name map:** each single name tied to a baptismal name, with the record that ties them.
- **Compensation row:** claim identifier, claimants and their roles, the count, the count from the rebuilt population with the births, deaths and transfers between their dates, and any person named in the original claim or certificate.
- **Gaps:** returns not searched, colonies or years not online, and every miss with its reason (change return, name filed under a representative, estate not named).

The model assembles this packet. The researcher decides identity and kinship.
