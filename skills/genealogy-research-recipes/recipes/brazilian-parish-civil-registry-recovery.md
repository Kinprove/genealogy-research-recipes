# Recover a Brazilian ancestor in parish books, registry-office records and their substitutes

Use this recipe to find a baptism, church marriage or burial, or a civil birth, marriage or death entry, for someone who lived in Brazil. Use it also to rebuild a family when the parish books are lost. It prevents these failures:

- searching only the registry office (cartório) or parish of the place named in a record, or of today's municipality, and calling the record missing
- missing that a newer town's events were registered or baptized in the older town it was split from, or in a parish across a state line
- starting civil searches in 1850, or before 1889, or using Portuguese or Spanish start dates
- treating a FamilySearch image or a short-form certificate as the final copy when an official use needs a certified full-text copy (certidão de inteiro teor)
- reading a civil registration made decades late, or by a modern court order, as independent evidence
- reading Brazilian surnames by Portuguese or Spanish rules
- stopping at lost parish books instead of turning to inventories, marriage files, voter lists, land registers and household lists

## Inputs to gather first

Gather these before searching:

- the event type and approximate year, and the birth years of every known sibling
- the place exactly as each record writes it: farm or estate (sítio, fazenda, engenho), village (povoado), district, municipality and state
- the religion (Catholic, Evangelical or Lutheran, other) and, for an immigrant, the country of origin
- any locator already seen: book (livro), folio (folha, fl. or fls.), entry number, registry office, FamilySearch film or image number
- the purpose: research only, or an official use (citizenship, court, inheritance) that needs certified copies

## 1. Build the dated jurisdiction chain before you search

- **Work out the history of the place first.** Many Brazilian municipalities are recent. The town in a record may have been a village or district of an older municipality on the event date [S1].
- **Take the chain from the IBGE municipal history.** Each municipality's history in the IBGE library has a "Formação Administrativa" section. It gives the law and date that created the district, the law that made it a municipality, and the municipality it was split from (desmembrado de). Follow the chain back one step at a time, and record every former name [S2].
- **Worked example: Parelhas, Rio Grande do Norte.**
  - Parelhas became a district of Jardim do Seridó in 1920. It became a municipality in November 1926 and was installed on 1 January 1927 [S2].
  - Jardim do Seridó, earlier called Conceição do Azevedo, was split from Acari in 1858. Acari was split from Príncipe, later Caicó, in 1833 [S2].
  - So a 1915 or 1921 Parelhas birth belongs in the Jardim do Seridó registry [S2], [S3]. On FamilySearch, Parelhas civil books start only in 1927 [S4]. Search Jardim do Seridó for any Parelhas civil event before 1927 [S2]. For church records, follow the parish chain instead: the Jardim do Seridó parish from 4 September 1856, before that its mother parish of Nossa Senhora da Guia in Acari [S46], then Caicó [S4]. For probate, search the orphans' court of Jardim: an 1883 inventory of a Parelhas couple was filed there [S31].
- **Keep the civil chain and the church chain as two separate rows.** A parish (freguesia or paróquia) can be older or younger than the municipality. Its mother parish (matriz) is not always the parent municipality. In Pernambuco's Agreste, search the books of the mother parish of Bezerros for Caruaru, São Caetano and Gravatá families before about 1850 [S5].
- **Keep border parishes in the chain.** In one case, siblings born near Parelhas around 1915–1917 were baptized one in Pedra Lavrada, Paraíba, and one in Parelhas, Rio Grande do Norte [S3]. A sibling's baptism in a neighbouring older parish tells you which registry and parish to search for the target.
- **Search inside the estate first.** In the Northeast, baptisms were often performed in estate chapels and entered by chapel and estate (engenho). Keep each search on the family's estate before you widen it: a wider area fills the results with same-named people from other estates [S6].

## 2. Send a civil entry to the registry district of its date, then up the chain

- **Civil registration started on 1 January 1889.** Decree 9.886 of 7 March 1888 set the rules, and Decree 10.044 of 22 September 1888 fixed the start date [S7], [S8].
- **A birth went to the clerk of the parish district where it happened.** The 1888 rules gave registration to the justice-of-the-peace clerk (Escrivão de Paz) of the first or only district of the parish where the birth took place [S7]. Early civil books therefore follow the parish districts of 1889, not today's municipalities.
- **Expect moved books.** Most books are now at the local civil registry office (Cartório de Registro Civil das Pessoas Naturais). Older books can be in a municipal or state archive. The records of a dissolved municipality can be in a neighbouring one [S9].
- **Search in this order, and log each step:**
  1. the registry of the district that covered the birthplace on the event date;
  2. the registry of each parent municipality in the chain from step 1;
  3. the registry of the parents' residence: a birth could be registered in another town than the one where it happened, and small villages reported births to a central office [S9];
  4. older neighbouring towns, including across a state line, chosen from the siblings' records.
- **Do not take 1850 as the start.** An 1850 law and Decree 798 of 18 June 1851 ordered birth and death registers. Decree 907 of 29 January 1852 suspended them [S10]. One class presents the 1850 law as the start and says the records came much later [S1]. Use 1889.
- **Search for earlier civil books only for known exceptions.** From 1863, non-Catholic marriages went into a book kept by the municipal council secretary where one spouse lived. Non-Catholic births and deaths went to the local justice-of-the-peace clerk [S11]. FamilySearch notes some offices with books from the 1860s and 1870s, and others that did not start until the 1920s [S9].
- **Civil marriage starts on 24 May 1890.** From that date only a marriage made under Decree 181 had civil validity. The couple could hold the church ceremony before or after the civil one [S12]. Search both registers for every marriage after that date, and expect two different dates.

**Read the marriage rules by date.** From 1 January 1889 [S8], the 1888 rules required every couple, whatever their religion, to have their marriage entered within three days by the justice-of-the-peace clerk of their parish of residence, from the celebrant's certificate or statement [S7]. For a church marriage of 1889 or early 1890, search the civil books too. These rules replaced an earlier regulation of 25 April 1874, Decree 5.604 [S7], [S48]. They took effect across the Empire only on 1 January 1889 [S8], so do not take 1874 as the start either. Decree 521 of 26 June 1890 then removed the couple's choice to hold the church ceremony before or after the civil one [S12], [S49]. Under it, the civil marriage was always to come first [S49]. A minister who held the religious ceremony before it faced six months in prison and a fine [S49]. The decree took effect in each jurisdiction three days after the local judge published it [S49], so the date varies by place.

## 3. Search forward for late registrations, and weigh how they were made

- **Search the following years.** One presenter usually finds a late birth registration within five years of the birth, and has seen 10-, 15- and 20-year delays [S13]. Some people born after 1889 were never registered [S14].
- **Search the amnesty books.** Decree 19.710 of 18 February 1931 allowed fine-free registration of unregistered births until 31 December 1932 [S15]. In one family, sisters born in 1922 and 1923 were registered only in 1933, after a fine exemption [S16]. For anyone born between 1889 and about 1930 with no entry near the birth, read each candidate registry's books for 1931 to 1933.
- **Keep the registration date and the stated birth date in separate fields.** Under the 1888 rules a late declaration needed an order from the justice of the peace and carried a fine [S7].
- **Do not cite a modern court-ordered late registration as evidence of the facts it states.** One presenter obtained, through a lawyer and a judge, a civil birth registration for a woman born in 1843. The court built it from the sources the presenter submitted [S17]. Such an entry restates the petitioner's evidence. Cite the sources behind it.

## 4. Choose the record that can answer the question

- **For grandparents, use the civil birth.** The 1888 rules required the birth entry to give legitimacy or foundling status, the parents' names, birthplaces, occupation and residence, the parish where the parents married, and both sets of grandparents [S7]. One civil birth therefore adds two generations at once [S13], [S18]. The same rules let the entry leave out the father's or the mother's name if naming them would cause scandal [S7], so a blank parent in a birth entry is not proof that the parent was unknown.
- **Use the youngest sibling.** When the older siblings were born before 1889, the first child born after 1 January 1889 was required to name both sets of grandparents, who appear in none of the older siblings' baptisms [S7], [S19]. Read a blank grandparent field as a gap to fill, not as an unknown grandparent: take the grandparents from the other siblings' civil entries [S19].
- **For an adult's parents, use the civil death.** The 1888 rules required the death entry to name the deceased's parents, the surviving spouse, and the legitimate or recognised children with their ages [S7]. One presenter finds that a Catholic burial of a married adult usually names only the spouse [S14].
- **Do not take a baptism as the adult's full name.** Brazilian baptisms usually give only the child's given name, then the parents [S20], [S14]. Pair a baptism with the person's marriage or death entry before you use it to identify an adult.
- **Copy the status words exactly:** legítimo (born to a church-married couple), natural (born outside a church marriage), exposto em casa de … (a foundling left at a named house) [S20]. Hold a foundling's link to that household as a hypothesis to test. In one case, children baptized as foundlings at a priest's farm door were identified as his own through his will [S21]. A civil foundling entry records where and when the child was found and its apparent age, and the child's belongings went to the orphans' judge [S7], so search that court's papers too.
- **After 24 May 1890, read "natural" in a civil entry as a possible church-only marriage.** A church marriage then had no civil effect [S12]. Search the church marriage before you call the parents unmarried. A later margin note (averbação) can record the parents' civil marriage and the child's changed status [S16].
- **Compare church and civil entries for immigrants.** A priest who shared the family's language could write a name more accurately than the civil clerk [S16]. Take the name form from the baptism and the civil entry separately, and keep both in the name register.

## 5. Use FamilySearch to find the entry, and order the inteiro teor when the copy matters

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> An order for a second original or a full-text certificate (inteiro teor) sends the registered person's identity and your relationship to them, and the full text can show filiation and other sensitive details about people who may be living. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before you order one.

- **Browse the images; do not trust the index.** Brazilian indexes are usually ordered by the child's or the groom's given name. The number in an index can be a page or an entry: "L" is the book, "fl." or "fls." the folio, and "v" the back of the folio (verso) [S1]. Civil books often have a name index at the start or end of the volume [S22].
- **Check where the online run ends.** FamilySearch can stop years before the register does. For Pombal, Paraíba, it held marriages only to 1900 and deaths to 1889, and the parish found a 1904–1906 marriage in its own books [S23].
- **Look outside the neat book list.** A São Paulo marriage missing from a parish's catalogued book (106 pages) was in a broader collection that combines several books (792 images). FamilySearch's image search listed a town (Patu, Rio Grande do Norte) that catalog search did not [S24]. Check each book's catalogued date range against its images: one book catalogued as starting in 1898 held an 1889 birth. Browse mixed books (livros mistos) for marriage investigations [S25].
- **Treat a FamilySearch image as a research copy.** For citizenship and other official uses, order a recent second original (segunda via) in full-text form (inteiro teor) from the cartório. The Italian consulate in São Paulo, for example, requires Brazilian birth, marriage and death certificates as recent second originals in inteiro teor, apostilled, with an apostilled sworn translation [S26]. A film made decades ago also cannot show margin notes added after filming.
- **Know what each certificate form needs.** A short-form certificate (breve relato) is open to anyone, with no request or identification. A full-text certificate (inteiro teor) needs a signed written request that identifies you, states why you want the full text, and gives your relationship to the registered person and whether that person has died. A death certificate needs no request or court order in any form [S47].
- **Know what a short-form birth certificate leaves out.** It must not show the parents' civil status, the nature of the filiation, or the place and registry of the parents' marriage (Law 8.560/1992, art. 6) [S28]. Order the full text for those fields.
- **Ask a court only where the rules require it.** The registered person, a legal representative or a specially empowered agent receives any certificate, full text included, without a court authorisation. A third party needs one for a full text with sensitive data, a restriction that ends with the registered person's death, and for a full text that shows a birth outside marriage. A reference to legitimate filiation needs no court order, even for a third party [S47], [S28]. Legitimation statements stay out of certificates unless a court orders otherwise, and the archived order behind an adoption is certified only by court order [S27]. Record a refusal under these rules as an access state, not as an absent entry.
- **Order through the official portal.** registrocivil.org.br is run by the national civil-registry operator (ON-RCPN). It reaches registry offices across Brazil and delivers electronically, by post or for pickup [S29]. One presenter orders "inteiro teor com cópia reprográfica" in electronic form and gives the book and folio when known [S18].
- **Record a parish "certidão de inteiro teor" as a transcription.** Parishes issue signed and stamped transcriptions of baptisms and marriages, sometimes from a transcription the requester supplies [S20], [S23]. Log it as a derivative of the book entry, with the issuing parish and the date.

## 6. Find the parish book under its mother parish and its present custodian

- **Expect regional losses and gaps.** In the Northeast, parish books mostly reach back only to about 1800. One presenter reports that Dutch-war losses left a single pre-war book for the old Diocese of Olinda [S6]. In Bezerros the parish began in 1768, but the marriage books start in 1782 and the early baptisms are badly damaged [S5].
- **Ask the diocese as well as FamilySearch.** Many Pernambuco books were gathered at the archdiocesan curia in Recife, and some FamilySearch films of them are poor black-and-white images or are not online [S6]. Books digitised later with a diocese's permission can be viewable only at the diocese [S5].
- **For a colonial-era event, ask for every status-specific book of the parish.** Colonial parishes kept separate books by legal status [S30], and a São Paulo parish listing shows a separate 1768–1826 book of enslaved people's marriages [S24]. Search the books for free and for enslaved people for the same years before you call an entry missing.
- **Search Protestant registers separately.** German-speaking Evangelical and Lutheran congregations kept their own registers, in German until the late 1930s. These can name the home district or village where civil and Catholic entries give only "Alemanha" or a German state. Search both churches when a conversion or a mixed marriage is possible [S22].

## 7. When the parish books are lost, rebuild from the substitute series

Look in each series for a dated document that ties a couple to one more relative. One presenter builds families only from such dated "couple plus one" documents, because undated reconstructions collapse under same-named people [S6]. Mark each series searched, found or not found.

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> When a civil marriage is recent, a request for its application file (below) sends the couple's names and asks for papers about the couple and their parents, who may be living; answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before you send it.

- **Probate inventories and wills (inventários, testamentos).**
  - Search the orphans' court (Juízo de Órfãos) of the town that held jurisdiction on the death date. An 1883 inventory for a couple living in Parelhas was filed in the court of Jardim [S31].
  - Inventories are held by courthouses (fóruns), state and municipal archives, universities, museums and private owners. Access can need a petition to the judge, and some judges admit only lawyers [S31], [S23], [S32].
  - Read the whole file. A creditor entry can carry filiation [S31]. Court files can preserve priest-issued copies of baptisms from books that are now lost [S33].
  - Search several years after the death: one presenter has seen an inventory opened ten years later [S32].
  - The court and notarial papers of São João do Cariri, Mamanguape and João Pessoa, Paraíba (2,020 items, 1778–1918), were digitised under the British Library's Endangered Archives Programme as EAP853 [S34].
- **Church marriage files.** Banns papers (banhos, proclamas) stayed in the parish archive, and dispensations went to the bishop's chamber. The São Paulo archdiocesan archive (ACMSP) files both together as "dispensas matrimoniais" [S35], arranged by year and then by the groom's name [S36]. Marriage files at the Mariana archdiocesan archive can give three generations and state the kinship impediment [S21]. For reading the impediment, use the Catholic holdings recipe.
- **The civil marriage file at the cartório.** From 1890 a couple applied to the civil registrar with proof of age or a substitute proof, a declaration of their own and their parents' status and residence (or where the parents died), and two witnesses' statements. The banns notices went into their own book at the registry [S12]. Ask the registry for the application file (processo de habilitação de casamento) and the banns notices (editais de proclamas) when the marriage entry is missing or a spouse was an immigrant. One such file gave an Italian-born bride's baptism town and her father's name [S37], [S4]. Anyone may obtain a certificate of a paper that is public with unrestricted access; the other papers go to the parties, their representatives, a court-authorised requester and, after the holder's death, anyone who presents the death certificate [S47].
- **Voter lists.** From 1875 the parish boards' voter lists had to give each qualified voter's age, marital status, occupation, literacy, filiation, domicile and income [S38]. Look for them in town halls and municipal archives [S23].
- **The vicar's land register (registro do vigário).** Under Decree 1.318 of 1854 the vicar of each parish registered land declarations: the possessor, the parish, the place name, the extent and the boundaries. The loose copies stayed in the parish archive, and the books went to the provincial land office [S39]. Use it as a dated list of who held land where in the 1850s. Treat a declaration that names an inheritance as a kinship lead.
- **Household lists.** São Paulo's annual household lists (maços de população, 1765–1836) are at the São Paulo state archive. They give each household member's name, age, colour and birthplace, and some are online [S40]. In Minas Gerais, one researcher combined a 1795 confession roll with the 1831 and 1838 provincial nominal lists for one parish, and found that a household list could leave out children under seven [S41].
- **Notarial deed books (livros de notas).** A sale, dowry, donation or settlement often states how a property came by inheritance, which can name a spouse's parent [S6].
- **Published local histories and family books.** Use them for leads and for the folios they cite, then confirm each link in a primary record.

## 8. Read Brazilian names by Brazilian practice

- **Do not apply a fixed surname rule.** Brazil never had a standard way of forming surnames. Daughters often took the mother's surname and sons the father's [S42]. Siblings could carry very different surnames, and people changed surnames during their lives [S43], [S41].
- **Search by given name and parents.** Given names were more stable than surnames [S43]. Search the given name with the parents' given names and the place, then each surname alone. In indexes, search with one surname [S1].
- **Allow for a married woman's chosen name.** A civil marriage entry can state the name the bride will use [S1].
- **Allow for immigrant patterns.** Immigrant families often gave sons only the father's surname [S42]. A father's given name could become the family's recorded surname when officials misread the order on a foreign document [S37].

## 9. Record access states, not absences

- **Cite the archive law in the written request for a church register before 1917.** Law 8.159/1991 (art. 16) identifies civil-status registers in religious archives made before the Civil Code took effect (1 January 1917) as of public and social interest [S44], [S45]. Quote art. 16 in the request, and record the custodian's answer.
- **Record fees and image limits as access states.** A diocese can charge far more than a registry office for the same death entry [S45], and some diocesan agreements bar sharing images [S5].
- **Log every refusal** with the custodian, the rule given and the date. A refused inspection is not a search result.

## Close with a jurisdiction and holdings packet

Return:

- **Jurisdiction rows:** the place as written; its district, municipality and state on the event date; each parent municipality with its law and date; the parish, its mother parish and the diocese.
- **Register decision:** parish, civil or both for each event, with the reason (1 January 1889, 24 May 1890, religion).
- **Holdings rows:** custodian, series, years, locator (book, folio, entry, film or image number), level inspected (index, image, parish transcription, cartório certificate) and result.
- **Late-registration rows:** registration date, stated birth date, and the basis (amnesty, court order).
- **Substitute-series rows:** inventories, wills, marriage files, voter lists, land registers, household lists and deeds, each marked searched, found or not found.
- **Name register:** every name form, by record.
- **Access states:** refusals, restricted certificates, court petitions, and images viewable only on site.

The model assembles this packet. The researcher decides identity and kinship.
