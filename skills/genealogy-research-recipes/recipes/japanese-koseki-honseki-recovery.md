# Recover the kanji name and honseki before requesting a Japanese koseki

Use this recipe when someone wants a Japanese family register (koseki) for an emigrant ancestor and has only a romanized name, a remembered region or a prefecture. It covers the steps that come before the request: get the exact kanji name and the full historical permanent domicile (honseki), then trace that domicile to the municipal office that holds the register today. It also covers eligibility and retention checks. The record routes in steps 1 and 2 are for emigrants to Brazil. Routes for other destinations such as Hawaii, the US mainland or Peru are not covered here. Do not use this recipe to rule on identity. A passenger-list or emigrant-list hit is a candidate, so route identity questions to [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md). Record locators with [source citation](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/source-citation.md).

## Avoid the usual errors

- **Searching on a romanized name.** One spoken reading can be written with many kanji combinations, so a romanized name does not identify the person. The request needs the exact registered Japanese spelling, kanji or kana as recorded ([Kagoshima City naming FAQ](https://www.city.kagoshima.lg.jp/faq-kurashi/shimin/q113.html)). Never construct that spelling from a romanized reading. The presenter had heard that some offices may accept a romanized name, but do not plan around that. [S1], [S2]
- **Sending the request to the region the family "came from".** The register is held by the municipal office for the registered domicile ([Kagoshima City FAQ](https://www.city.kagoshima.lg.jp/faq-kurashi/shimin/q97.html)). Japan has 1,718 municipalities plus Tokyo's 23 special wards ([MIC](https://www.soumu.go.jp/kouiki/kouiki.html)). The presenter rounds this to roughly 2,000 offices. In his example, a family believed to come from western Japan had a honseki near Tokyo, and the western office his friend planned to visit would have reported no record. [S1], [S2] Since 1 March 2024, wide-area issuance lets an eligible requester who comes in person get the register at any office (see step 4), except some registers that have not been computerized. Those, and proxy requests, still go to the honseki office.
- **Reading the honseki as a residence.** In older registers the domicile is a lead to where the family lived, not established residence. In modern registers the honseki can be any existing lot address in Japan and need not match where the family lived ([Metropolis](https://metropolisjapan.com/japans-honseki-system/)). The presenter's examples are a baseball stadium and a place where someone married or proposed. [S1]
- **Treating a "no record" reply as proof that no register existed.** The reply may come from the wrong office or a merged municipality. The register may also have been discarded or destroyed (see step 5). [S1]

## 1. Get the exact kanji from emigration records

Start with the emigrant ship's passenger list. For Japanese emigrants to Brazil, the presenter uses the passenger database of the Japanese immigration museum in São Paulo. He demonstrates searching it by ship, prefecture of origin, period, given name or surname. In his demonstration a romanized search returned the name in kanji, along with the ship, departure and arrival dates, and the family members who travelled together. Keep that family group as context for identification. [S2]

Record the kanji exactly as shown, together with the database, the search terms and the returned entry. A kanji form taken from one index entry is still a reading to confirm against the passport or emigrant record or the koseki.

## 2. Get the full honseki from passport and emigrant records

A prefecture alone is not enough. You need the complete address, including the house or lot number. [S1], [S2]

- Prewar emigrant passport-issuance records are held at the Diplomatic Archives of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Tokyo ([NDL research guide](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/rnavi/business/travel_records)). The presenter describes entries that give the passport number, the name in kanji, the place of origin and address, age and birth date. Not all of them are digitized. For undigitized volumes you have to read entry by entry around the departure date. [S2]
- The National Diet Library (NDL) holds almost none of the passport-issuance records. What it does hold is the emigration-company lists 『伯剌西爾行移民名簿』 (1908–1963), and only part of that set is digitized ([NDL research guide](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/rnavi/business/travel_records)). In his demonstration, the presenter searched the NDL digital collection for the kanji name and narrowed the results to the Brazil emigrant lists. He got one entry with a passport number, prefecture, issue date, occupation and birth date. [S2] Cite such an entry as an emigrant list, not as the passport-issuance register. If it lacks the full address, go to the Diplomatic Archives records.

Record the entry's locator and the domicile exactly as written, before any normalization.

## 3. Trace the historical domicile to today's office

Treat the recovered domicile as a set of historical place names: prefecture, county and village. Trace the village through its municipal mergers to the present municipality. The presenter uses the county and village articles on Wikipedia, with machine translation if needed. His example goes from a Wakayama Prefecture county and village to its successor town. [S2]

Record the historical name and the current municipality side by side, with each step in the merger chain and where you found it. Send the request to the current successor's office, not the one nearest a remembered hometown. Confirm the merger with the municipality if the chain is unclear.

## 4. Check eligibility before requesting

- Under Family Register Act Art. 10, a register can be requested by the persons recorded in it, their spouses, and their direct ascendants and descendants ([Art. 10 text](https://ja.wikibooks.org/wiki/%E6%88%B8%E7%B1%8D%E6%B3%95%E7%AC%AC10%E6%9D%A1)). A spouse has no entitlement, simply as a spouse, to the partner's ancestors' registers. The descendant has to request those. Art. 10-2 separately allows third parties who have a legitimate purpose, such as exercising rights or inheritance. [S1]
- Requests go up the direct line: yourself, then parents, grandparents and further back. Collateral relatives' registers (aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, cousins) are not available as of right. [S1]
- A proxy can request on the descendant's behalf with a letter of authorization (委任状) signed by the requester. The proxy presents the request form, the 委任状 and the proxy's own ID ([Edogawa City FAQ](https://www.city.edogawa.tokyo.jp/e031/qa/kurashi/todokede/kosekishomei08.html)). The presenter asks his clients for a notarized power of attorney. That is his own practice, not a general legal requirement. [S1]
- Prove direct descent with ID plus birth and death certificates and immigration records that link you to the ancestor. If you hold your own koseki, ID plus that koseki is enough to start, because it names your parents. [S1], [S2]
- Each koseki is organized by family unit and names the parents. Request one generation at a time until no earlier register survives. Genealogy requests of this kind are common, and they can take weeks ([Fukuoka City](https://www.city.fukuoka.lg.jp/shimin/kusei/life/koseki_kouikikoufu.html)). [S2]
- **Wide-area issuance (広域交付).** Since 1 March 2024, an eligible requester can get koseki, closed registers (除籍) and pre-revision registers (改製原戸籍) at any municipal office in Japan. Some koseki and closed registers that have not been computerized are excluded, so request those from the honseki municipality. The requester must appear in person, proxies are not allowed, and it covers only the requester, the spouse and direct-line relatives. Requests for older registers may need a reservation and can take several weeks ([MOJ](https://www.moj.go.jp/MINJI/minji04_00082.html)). Most overseas descendants cannot appear in person, so for them this route does not help.

## 5. Allow for registers that were discarded or lost

The retention period for closed registers was extended from 80 to 150 years by an ordinance amendment that took effect on 1 June 2010 ([commentary](https://www.mc-law.jp/kigyohomu/17566)). While the 80-year rule applied, some municipal offices discarded older registers. Disasters and wars destroyed others. [S1] If a register cannot be found, record it as missing with the cause unconfirmed. Record discard or destruction only when the office's reply or other specific evidence says so. A missing register does not show that the family was never registered there. The presenter advises requesting soon. [S1]

## 6. Hand off land and temple leads

These are follow-on leads, not substitutes for the koseki.

- **Old land registers (Legal Affairs Bureau).** The presenter says these list the assets for an address and the people tied to it, along with land size and tax in old units. He describes the people as household heads. Treat them as owners (所有者), because that is what the register records. He also says the registers are free and that anyone may request them, with no descent requirement. Before you rely on that, confirm access and cost with the bureau that covers the area. Some municipalities hold their own copies under different rules. Since 1 April 2025, old map certificates (地図等証明書) cost 500 yen by written application, 470 yen online with postal delivery and 440 yen online with counter collection ([MOJ fee schedule](https://www.moj.go.jp/MINJI/TESURYO/index.html)). They are charged per parcel (1筆) ([Legal Affairs Bureau notice](https://houmukyoku.moj.go.jp/morioka/content/001439200.pdf)). For an older honseki, expect the land-register address to match. For a modern honseki it may differ, so check it against an old map before linking the two. [S1]
- **Family temple (bodaiji) and its death register (kakochō).** In the Edo period everyone had to be affiliated with a Buddhist temple (the terauke system), which was tied to the ban on Christianity ([Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%AA%80%E5%AE%B6%E5%88%B6%E5%BA%A6-1363649)). Use the oldest honseki to identify the family's affiliated temple, then ask about its death register and graves. The presenter warns that entries may give only a Buddhist posthumous name that differs from the koseki name. Temples may also refuse to show records to people who have no existing connection. [S1]
- **Local history journals.** Look in local libraries and history museums. Many are not digitized, so contact them directly. [S1]

## Return a koseki request packet

Return the following:

- The exact registered name (kanji or kana as recorded), with the source entry and locator.
- The historical honseki as written, the merger chain, and the current municipality and office.
- Proof of the direct-descent line and the requester's role: descendant in person (wide-area issuance possible), descendant by post, or proxy with a signed 委任状.
- Retention and loss notes.
- Land, map, temple and local-history leads, each marked as a lead.

If a step was not completed, name it as a gap. Do not fill it with a guessed office or address. This recipe does not cover postal request forms or fee payment from abroad, so confirm those with the target office.
