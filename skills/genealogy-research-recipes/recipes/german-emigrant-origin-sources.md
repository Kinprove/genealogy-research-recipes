# Trace a German emigrant's home town through emigration files, indexes and the parish register

Use this recipe when a U.S. record names only a German state, region, partial place, regiment or congregation, and you need the home town. The main mistake to avoid is treating a miss in official emigration-permission files or a regional index as proof that the person did not come from that state. Before you read a miss as absence, check four things: which office created the files, whether the item is an official record or a compiled resource, what the index says it covers, and whether there is a separate series for people who left without permission. Confirm every index hit in the original birth or baptism register of the place of origin.

This recipe does not cover these steps, so use the linked recipes for them:

- Arrival and manifest work: [U.S. immigration search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/usa-immigration-search-recovery.md).
- Working from a destination clue to a locality and handing off to the archive, shown for a different emigration system: [Russian-Empire emigrant origin recovery § 5](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/russian-empire-emigrant-origin-recovery.md).
- Catalogue, film and image crosswalks: [FamilySearch record workflows](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/familysearch-practical-record-workflows.md).

## 1. Find the office that created the emigration files

Translate the U.S. clue into the historical territory (the lectures call it the "nobility area"; in German, Territorium or Land). Then list that territory's upper, middle and lower government levels. The presenter uses a chart that names, for each territory, the office that kept emigration records. Search the catalogue under that office, not just under the state name. [S1], [S2]

Do not rule out a middle level just because the territory is not Prussia. The presenter says only the Kingdom of Prussia had a middle level, but that is wrong. Bavaria set up mid-level Kreise/Regierungsbezirke in 1808, and by 1871 most states of the Empire had adopted the idea. The same lecture also shows a Bavarian middle-level catalogue entry (§ 2). Check every level the territory actually had. [S2]

Hessen example (the Grand Duchy of Hesse, called "the duchy of Hessen" in the lecture): the upper level was three provinces (Oberhessen, Rheinhessen, Starkenburg). The presenter says emigration records were not kept at province level. They were kept by the district (Kreis) offices. In his catalogue example, a district-level emigration series holds the file for one family's emigration, dated 16 October 1854. [S1], [S2]

Expect these files to be handwritten, with several documents per emigrant. The presenter treats them as a last resort after indexes. [S1] A second presenter, who has translated many of them, says permission files record [S6]:

- whether military service was completed or excused;
- the wife and minor children travelling with the emigrant, by name and birth date;
- sometimes, occupations.

Record only what the opened file actually shows.

Access: search the FamilySearch catalogue under the town and under the district office for your years before you plan anything around these files. Finding an office does not mean its files survive, were filmed or are online. [S6]

## 2. Separate official records from compiled resources

Classify every catalogue item by its author:

- **Government office as author:** an official record.
- **Individual compiler as author:** a resource, even if its title looks like an official emigration register or it is catalogued under a government district. [S2]

Examples from the lectures:

- A Bavarian middle-level catalogue entry that is compiled by an individual. [S2]
- Two Rheinhessen emigration compilations listed under the province: one on emigration in the 1700s and 1800s, credited to Helmut Schmahl, and one on emigration to Texas, credited to Hans Richter (names spelled from the lecture audio; the Richter work has not been located). [S2]

The presenter says these compilations were built from passenger lists and port records, so they often include people who left without permission and are missing from the official files. [S2] Search them, but list them in your source register as compilations. Agreement between a compilation and the passenger list it was built from is not two independent sources.

## 3. Record each index's stated coverage before searching

For every published state or district index, write down its stated geography, date range, arrangement and whether it covers permitted emigrants only, before you search it. Indexes the presenter describes [S1]:

- **Württemberg** emigration index by Trudy Schenk and Ruth Froelke: eight volumes (1986–2002). Fields are emigrant, birth date, birthplace and emigration date, plus Oberamt, destination and film number. Do not assume it covers every Oberamt: check the published volume-by-volume list for yours. It lists only people who had permission to emigrate, 1808–1890. A person who left without permission can be missing from it legitimately.
- **Former Duchy of Braunschweig** (Gruhne, *Auswandererlisten*, 1846–1871): excludes the city of Braunschweig and Landkreis Holzminden. A "born in Braunschweig" person can therefore be missing from it legitimately. Note too that "Braunschweig" was both a city and a duchy.
- **Duchy of Nassau**, 1806–1866 (Struck, *Die Auswanderung aus dem Herzogtum Nassau 1806–1866*).
- **Westphalia**: the Prussian province had three government districts (Münster, Minden, Arnsberg). The work the presenter shows indexes one of them. Published Müller/Holz emigrant lists exist for both Münster and Minden. Record which district's list you searched. A miss covers only that district.
- **Westerwald**: a landscape area that cuts across several territories and provinces. The book the presenter shows covers people who emigrated from it to America in the 1700s.
- **Bremen–New York passenger lists** (Zimmerman & Wolfert, *German Immigrants: Lists of Passengers Bound from Bremen to New York*): four volumes (1847–54, 1855–62, 1863–67, 1868–71). They include only passengers whose specific place of origin was given. Entries give the home place with a territory abbreviation, for example "Khe" for Kurhessen. A passenger without a stated origin is not in them.

State-archive emigrant databases need the same coverage note. Forum participants used two of them:

- **Baden-Württemberg:** the emigrant index reached through the LEO-BW portal. One thread used an entry from it to date a departure (§ 7). [S9]
- **Hessen:** the Hessian emigrant data in LAGIS, built from the Marburg HESAUS database and older Darmstadt card indexes. Another thread matched one village's LAGIS entries to settlers at the destination (§ 5). [S11]

The Baden-Württemberg database comes from 1970s index cards centred on 1850–1900. Do not assume either database covers every year or every district. Write down the years and places that your village's entries actually span. A miss outside that span means nothing.

A miss counts only for the index's stated extent. Record it as "not in [index], which covers X and excludes Y". Do not record it as "not from [state]". Also search across levels and landscape areas, not just the one unit you first identified. [S1], [S2]

## 4. Search the series for people who left without permission

According to the presenters, permission required paying fees, filling out forms, paying off debts and completing military obligation, and many people left without it to avoid those steps. Others left after the failed 1848 revolution because they faced prosecution. Such people are absent from the regular permission files. [S2], [S3]

Look for a separate series or volume for them. The presenter's example is Müller's two-part work for the Regierungsbezirk Minden in Westphalia [S3]:

- Part I lists authorized emigrants, 1816–1900;
- Part II lists unauthorized ("heimliche") emigrants, 1814–1900.

Use the back index in Part II: take the person's assigned number and page, then open the entry. In his example the entry gives birthplace, birth date and parents. [S3] Write down the number, page and volume. An index line alone is not the entry.

### Other home-side records that name emigrants

Once you have a candidate home town or district, look for local records made because someone was leaving or had left. These can name a person who is missing from both the permission files and the indexes:

- **District gazettes (Kreisblatt, Amtsblatt).** A forum participant collected 1867 notices from one district gazette. They included statements of intent to emigrate, giving residence, occupation, the wife's maiden name and the number of children. They also included creditor calls and auctions of the same man's livestock, household goods and house, spread over several months. Read the notices together to date the break-up of the household. [S8]
- **Municipal council minutes and subsidies.** One participant posted council minutes from a Bavarian village (1852 and 1864) granting community money towards poor residents' passage to America. The same post lists that village's emigrants as reported both to the district office and to the regional government. Note which level each list came from (§ 1). Another post records that a Baden municipality gave a penniless woman a travel subsidy in 1849 so she could join her husband in North America. [S7]
- **Guardianship, estate and power-of-attorney files.** A published inventory of emigration sources in the Rhineland-Palatinate and Saarland state archives lists guardianship and estate files with inheritances for children who had emigrated. Another post cites a 1787 power of attorney sent from Maryland to manage a house, property and an inheritance at home (Landesarchiv Saarbrücken, HV K 55). Emigrants kept appearing in such files for years after they left. [S7]

Limits: the gazette notices were chance finds, not a complete run, and a notice shows intent, not departure or destination. The thread gives only inventory summaries and one archive reference, not the contents of the files. Treat a name in these records as a candidate. Then confirm it in the original birth or baptism register (§ 7).

## 5. Check U.S. church-extract series against their congregation list first

The German Immigrants in American Church Records series is ongoing. The presenter said there were 40 volumes in April 2024. The publisher now lists through Volume 48 (August 2026). Volumes are grouped by U.S. state, but not strictly one per state: some cover a single city, several states, or "Revisited" parts. Each volume has an every-name index in the back. [S4]

Steps [S4]:

1. Check the congregation list for the ancestor's area before any lookup. The presenter says the project website has a state-by-state index of the congregations extracted.
2. Search women under both married and maiden surnames. The presenter says women are indexed under both when the records give both.
3. Treat each entry as an extract from congregational registers, some of which the presenter says are available only on site. Pursue the original register for anything you rely on.

The presenter says the series is print-only and recommends buying a volume only once you know it extracts your ancestor's church. If the ancestor's congregation is not on the list, a miss says nothing about them. [S4]

### Read the destination community for chain migration

If a passenger list or census places the emigrant in a small place that was not a common destination, suspect chain migration from one home village. A forum example shows how to test it [S11]:

1. Read the census page around the ancestor, including nearby lines.
2. Look for a local settlement history or newspaper article that lists early settlers.
3. Compare those names with the candidate village's emigrant list and church book.
4. In the example, several neighbours and early settlers matched emigrants from one Hessian village. A group passenger-list entry giving that village and the destination tied them together.

Record the years the village's emigrant list covers. Settlers who arrived before it begins stay unresolved; do not count them as non-matches. A shared surname is only a lead for each person. Identity needs that person's own records.

## 6. Identify the contingent of a Revolutionary-era "Hessian"

Do not equate "Hessian" with Hesse-Kassel. German auxiliary troops came from Hesse-Kassel, Hesse-Hanau, Waldeck, Brunswick, Ansbach-Bayreuth and Anhalt-Zerbst. [S5]

For a German who first appears in America in that period, search HETRINA (*Hessische Truppen im Amerikanischen Unabhängigkeitskrieg*), a six-volume printed index whose data is online in LAGIS Hessen, searchable by name or place of origin. Its fields include [S1]:

- entry number, surname and given name;
- birth year and birthplace (the presenter also lists area and country of origin);
- rank, regiment or battalion, and company;
- type of occurrence (for example called up, wounded, deserted);
- date and archive source.

Coverage: HETRINA covers only Hesse-Kassel, Waldeck and Hesse-Hanau. A miss for a soldier of the Brunswick, Ansbach-Bayreuth or Anhalt-Zerbst contingents is expected and says nothing about him. Record it as "not in HETRINA (Hesse-Kassel, Waldeck, Hesse-Hanau only)".

Pin the soldier to a specific town before you search church records. Otherwise a same-named man who stayed in Europe can be taken for him. [S5] Loyalist sources are a separate route and are not covered here. [S1]

## 7. Confirm the hit in the original birth or baptism register

Take every index candidate to the original birth or baptism register of the named place that fits the candidate's confession and date: the parish church register, the Jewish community register, or the civil register once civil registration had begun. The presenter's Württemberg example shows why [S1]:

- the register marked the child as born out of wedlock. Under the rule the presenter describes, that child received the mother's surname;
- it named the father;
- it recorded that the parents later married and the father legitimated the child. The presenter says that without legitimation the son could not have carried his father's surname or inherited from him.

Record illegitimacy, a named father, legitimation and any later surname change exactly as written. If several census or index candidates fit, compare them on birth year, spouse and residence first, then confirm in the register. [S1] Identity rulings belong in the evidence/proof recipe linked above.

**Bound the departure window.** For each candidate, set two limits:

- the earliest possible departure: the last event at home, such as a child's birth or baptism there;
- the latest possible departure: the first record in the U.S., such as a remarriage there.

Search the emigrant index and the passenger lists first inside that window. Widen it if the bounding events do not prove the person was present, since a man could leave before his last child was born. In one forum example, the window was 1851–1853, and a LEO-BW emigrant-index entry narrowed it to 1852 and showed that he left without his wife. If a spouse is missing from the departure, search the home parish's death register within the window. Her absence does not prove she died. [S9]

For a man of military age, yearly conscription-list (Musterungsliste) entries also help:

- an entry recording that he was actually mustered (examined) that year gives an earliest departure date; an entry without a recorded examination is only a lead;
- the field for the conscript's current residence can name an interim town worth searching. [S10]

## 8. Return an origin worksheet

Deliver:

1. **Jurisdiction worksheet:** territory; upper, middle and lower levels; and the office that created the emigration files.
2. **Source register:** each source marked official or compiled, with its stated geography and dates, exclusions, and whether it covers permitted emigrants, unauthorized emigrants or both.
3. **Candidates:** each with index number, volume and page, and its departure window with the two events that bound it.
4. **Birth/baptism-register status for each candidate:** confirmed, conflicting or not yet inspected.
5. **Gaps:** offices, series, index extents and local records (gazettes, council minutes, estate files) that were not searched, or that are known not to cover the person.
