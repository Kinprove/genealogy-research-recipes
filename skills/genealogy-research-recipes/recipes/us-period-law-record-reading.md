# Read U.S. records under the law and legal language of their own place and date

Use this recipe when a U.S. record uses a legal word, names a court or office, or invites an inference from what the law allowed: a young bride, a widow's share, an intestate father's land, a bond, a juror list, a court date. It shows how to find the rule for that place and date, and how far the record can be read under it.

"The author" in this recipe is Judy G. Russell of The Legal Genealogist, whose posts are cited as S1 to S23. Other bloggers are named where they are cited.

Errors this recipe blocks:

- applying today's law, or one uniform "American rule", to a record from another place and date
- reading a legal word in its ordinary modern sense: a `next friend` as a non-relative, a `Court of Ordinary` as a routine sitting, an `adr c t a` as an intestate estate, `late of` a place as dead, an `orphan` as a child with no living parent, a Revolutionary Virginia `commissioner` as one office
- ruling out a child because a will omits her or him, without asking whether a portion was already paid or what the omitted-child statute allowed
- searching for dower and dowry as if they were one record
- turning a legal requirement into a fact about a person: land ownership from jury service, an unlawful marriage from a young bride, a clerk's error from a December 25 court date, no kinship from witnessing a will
- reading a bond's penal sum as money paid or as the estate's value, or an enforced bond as proof of theft
- carrying a rule checked for one state into another state, or across a date when the law changed

## Inputs to gather first

- the date of the event, and the date of the record if it differs
- the place as it was on that date: county, then colony, territory, state or contested claim
- the record type, and the court or office that made it
- the legal words exactly as written, with abbreviations and Latin kept

## 1. Find the rule in force for that place and date

- **Search in order.** Look in the colonial charter or state constitution, then the statutes in force, then common-law principles such as Blackstone. Silence in the statutes does not mean no rule existed [S22]. This is a research order, not a rule stated for every state. It fits Georgia, where the 1784 adopting act received English law only so far as it did not conflict with the state's constitution and laws [S95].
- **Do not treat Louisiana as all one system.** It received common law only in part: its 1805 act construed named crimes according to English common law [S96], [S97]. Test the question at hand.
- **Fix the governing authority before statehood.** For a record made in the Vermont area, do not apply a later Vermont statute. First work out which competing authority could apply. Statehood took effect on 4 March 1791, and New York recognized Vermont in 1790 [S10], [S77], [S79]. Massachusetts and New Hampshire have no clean end dates: *Vermont v. New Hampshire* reports findings of recognition by New Hampshire in 1777, which New Hampshire contested, and by Massachusetts in 1781 [S77], [S78]. The author says there is no easy answer about whose law applied [S10].
- **Follow a bill through the session journal.** Identify the state, chamber and session [S11], then locate the corresponding journal in the Library of Congress guide [S196]. Trace introduction, committee referral, amendments and votes. Journals often omit debate, so a recorded vote can establish a change without explaining its cause [S101].
- **Say when you did not find it.** If no rule turns up, write "rule not found for this place and date" and leave the inference open. Do not fall back on a rule from another state or on today's law.

**Read the statute text in force on the event date.** Look for the session laws and the revised code of that year, not today's code, in Google Books and other scanned-book collections [S173 @2026-11-14]. Debbie Mieszala's Law Library Index on The Advancing Genealogist links historic statutes and, for some, case law for 29 states and territories, from Arizona to Wisconsin; it has no entry for Virginia, North Carolina or Texas, for example [S174 @2026-11-14].

## 2. Read the word in a dictionary of the period

- **Use a dictionary close to the record.** When a term in an older statute is missing from Black's 1891 dictionary, try an older one closer to the statute, such as Tomlins's 1797 revision of Giles Jacob [S12], [S103], [S104].
- **Find abbreviations where the dictionary files them.** In Black's, look among the abbreviation entries at the start of the letter, not under the word the abbreviation accompanies, then look up the expansion. This was checked only for the letter C of the 1891 text [S18], [S105].
- **Use a modern explanation only to understand the definition.** When a period definition rests on further unexplained terms, Wex, the Cornell Legal Information Institute's dictionary, is plainer [S15]. Wex is modern and may not cover an older term, so check that its explanation fits your period [S106].
- **Consider a misprint.** If a statutory word appears in no period dictionary, a printer may have changed it. Hening's printing of Virginia's 1785 inheritance act has `parency` for `parcenary`: intestate land passed to kindred as joint heirs. An 1830 Virginia opinion quotes the act as "in parcenary" [S12], [S38], [S80], [S81].

## 3. Legal terms that an ordinary reading gets wrong

| Term | Read it as | Do not read it as | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `dower` and `dowry` | Dower is a widow's provision from her husband's lands, assigned in records after his death. A wife's release of it can appear in her husband's conveyance while both are alive [S41]. Dowry is property the wife brought, found in the marriage contract in Civil Law places [S1], [S24] | Two names for one record, or a release in a deed as proof of widowhood | Quebec and New France also had dower (*douaire*), often set in that contract. Louisiana did not recognize dower [S51], [S52], [S53]. The word dowry is also used in common-law places [S1] |
| `widow's thirds` | In land, dower: usually a life estate in one third of the husband's lands [S5], [S69], [S70]. In an intestate's personal estate, her distributive share: under the English Statute of Distributions, "one third shall go to the widow of the intestate" [S131] | A life estate in everything, or a share of his lands she owned outright | "Usually", not always. Check the distribution act in force for the place and date |
| `Court of Ordinary` | The probate court in Georgia and South Carolina. Search probate holdings under that name [S3], [S26], [S57], [S59] | A routine court session | Georgia renamed it Probate Court in 1974. Georgia's Inferior Court held probate from about 1798 to 1852, with the Court of Ordinary before and after, and bound out orphans [S54], [S55], [S56]. A library note says Charleston County's Probate Court replaced it in 1868 [S58] |
| `next friend` | A competent person acting in the name of a minor, married woman or other person lacking capacity, often a parent or other relative [S15], [S49] | Proof of no kinship, or an appointed guardian | Not every next friend was kin. A court could name one without making that person a guardian [S84] |
| `sui juris` | A person with full civil rights and capacity. Someone not sui juris needed a representative such as a next friend [S15], [S39], [S45] | Latin to skip | |
| `void` and `voidable` | Void has no legal force and nothing can cure it. Voidable can be cured by act or confirmation [S16], [S34] | Synonyms | For underage marriage see step 4 |
| `mainpernor` | A surety distinct from bail: could not surrender the defendant early and answered for his appearance on all charges [S6], [S50] | A synonym for bail | The author infers greater trust and suggests checking for kinship. That is an inference, and no source tests it [S6] |
| `puts himself upon the country`, `ponit se super patriam`, `po. se.` | A tender of issue submitting the case to a jury; in criminal records, the term on a not-guilty plea [S4], [S27], [S28], [S29] | A statement about geography | |
| `take the body ... and him safely keep`, `capias ad respondendum`, `ca. resp.` | A capias ad respondendum, usually called simply a capias: the writ by which actions at law were often begun, ordering the sheriff to take the defendant and keep him so as to have him in court to answer the plaintiff [S112]. An arrested defendant stayed in custody unless he gave a bail bond [S113] | A summons only, or a sign of a crime or of flight | Read the sheriff’s return and seek the bail bond [S113]. A 1786 Wilkes County writ directed the sheriff to hold a defendant, but the writ alone gives no period of actual detention [S111] |
| `trespass on the case for words spoken`, `words defamatory spoken`, `words spoken` | An action for slander. Trespass on the case covered indirect wrongs, including slander and libel [S114], and Roberta Estes reads "words spoken" as the early phrasing for slander [S111] | Entry onto land or into a house | A bare `trespass on the case` does not name the wrong: the action also covered indirect or accidental injury, intangible property and wrongly performed contracts [S114] |
| `alias` and `pluries` writs | An alias writ is a second writ of the same kind, issued because the first had no effect; a pluries follows when the original and the alias have both failed [S116], [S117] | Another name used by the person | Read the return on the earlier writ for the reason it failed. Track an alias followed by a pluries under the same defendant and cause [S115] |
| `scire facias` on a forfeited recognizance | A writ calling a party to show why a judgment of record should not be executed against him [S118]. On a forfeited recognizance it ran against the principal and the sureties named in it, after a judgment of forfeiture [S119] | A new criminal charge, or a sign that the surety took part in the offence | After a forfeiture, check whom the court ordered to show cause. In the 1819 Lawrence County docket, Benjamin Weatherford’s failure to appear also brought in his bondsman, John Gilbreath [S115]. Follow the named surety as an associate |
| `nulla bona` ("nothing found") and `non est inventus` (`n. e.`) | Nulla bona: the sheriff's return to a writ of execution when he found no goods of the defendant within his jurisdiction to levy on [S120]. Non est inventus: his return that the defendant himself was not found there [S121] | Proof that the debtor was destitute or had left the county | Check the sheriff’s search and territorial jurisdiction before inferring poverty or departure [S111]. In 1847 the North Carolina Supreme Court faulted a sheriff who made no inquiry at the debtor’s residence [S122]. Compare local deeds and tax rolls |
| `sperate` and `desperate` debts | In an inventory or account of debts due to an estate: sperate, a debt that one may hope to recover; desperate, a debt considered hopeless of collection [S154], [S155] | A misspelling of "separate", or a separate list | Keep the estate inventory’s spelling, including `sperate` in a 1762 Baltimore County account [S156]. Search the exact term in a period law dictionary before accepting an automatic correction |
| `adr c t a` | Administrator *cum testamento annexo*: a will existed, but it named no executor, named incapable persons, or the named executors refused to act or had died. Look for the will [S18], [S46], [S47] | Proof the person died intestate | |
| `heir`, `heir-at-law` | At common law, a person who succeeds by law to an intestate's land by descent and relationship [S137]. Who qualifies, and in what order, is set by the descent and distribution acts in force at the death [S80], [S139] | A legatee, devisee or anyone else who received property under a will or other document [S138] | In civil-law places the word also covers a testamentary heir [S137]. A grandchild is an heir only in place of a parent who died before the decedent: under Virginia's 1785 act the issue of a dead child took that child's share [S80], [S138]. Read every listed heir, not only your ancestor [S139]. A parent named as heir-at-law points to no surviving descendants, but do not assume the siblings shared: the same act gave the land to the father, and to the mother, brothers and sisters only when there was no father [S80], [S140]. For heir lists as evidence of children, see [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md) |
| `heirs and assigns` in a deed or grant | Words marking a fee simple: the estate goes to the grantee, and the phrase gives no rights to any particular heirs or assigns [S141], [S142] | A list of the grantee's family, or proof that he had children | Heirs inherit by law when no will or other instrument disposes of the property [S143]. Assigns are those to whom an interest is or may be transferred, by conveyance, will, descent and distribution, or statute [S142] |
| `commissioner` in Virginia records, 1777–1783 | One of several offices that overlapped. Commissioners of the tax: three elected each year by the freeholders and housekeepers under the October 1777 act, who had the county's property assessed [S177]. Two commissioners appointed each year by the county court under the May 1779 act for a tax payable in wheat, corn, hemp, tobacco and other commodities, the specific tax; that act sends their accounts to the sheriff and "to the commissioners of the tax for the county", so it treats them as separate offices [S178], [S182]. Provision commissioners appointed by the governor and council under the May 1780 act, who bought or seized supplies and gave certificates [S179], [S180]. The public claims commissioners in Richmond, who approved the county courts' claims in 1783 [S180] | One office, or the later `commissioner of the revenue` | Before naming a man's office from a public-claims booklet or certificate, match the record's date to the act and the appointing body: freeholders, county court, or governor and council. A Just Genealogy live stream of 15 September 2022 warns against taking a specific-tax commissioner for a public-claims commissioner [S176]. It also counts a commissioner of revenue among the offices of that time. The wartime assessors were the commissioners of the tax [S177], [S178]; the heading "Commissioners of the revenue" is in the margin of the October 1786 act, whose text still calls its county-court appointees commissioners or commissioners of the tax [S181]. Check the act in force before using that title |
| `curator` (Missouri) | Guardian of a ward's estate, as distinct from guardian of the person [S18], [S89] | A vague custodian | Treat the 1876 docket abbreviation `g & c m h` as unresolved. The suggested expansion as guardian and curator of minor heirs needs confirmation from a spelled-out entry in that court [S18] |
| `guardian` of a minor | Usually guardian of the child's estate, who managed the inherited property. Guardianship had little to do with custody, and courts assumed that a fatherless child lived with the mother if she was alive and able [S148], [S149] | Proof that the child lived in the guardian's household | Read the order to identify whose person or property it covered [S151]. A grandfather administered property in one family while a maternal uncle’s obituary names the child he and his wife raised [S150]. Check censuses and family records for actual residence |
| `orphan` | In older guardian and probate records, a child whose father had died, whether or not the mother was living [S148], [S149], [S152] | A child with no living parent | A fatherless child with no estate, and no one to take him or her in, could be bound out as an apprentice by the court, and a guardian could bind a ward with the court's approval [S148], [S149]. When such a child drops out of the record after the father's death, search the county or orphans' court minutes and the indentures for the binding [S153]. Read an age in an indenture as evidence of the birth date, not as a birth record |
| `-ix`, `-trix` endings | Spelled out in full (administratrix, executrix, testatrix, creditrix, tutrix, relatrix), a female role-holder, even with an ambiguous given name [S13], [S30], [S31], [S32], [S33], [S40], [S44] | Something to leave undetermined | The dictionaries define the words; they do not rule out a clerk's error in one record |
| `ultimate facts` | In court cases from the mid-1800s to the 1930s, the bottom-line facts in issue, in the pleadings or a court's findings, as distinct from evidentiary facts [S2], [S25], [S90], [S91] | Emphasis meaning "the true facts" | The author quotes that courts found the distinction hard to apply consistently [S2] |
| `late of [place]`, `formerly of [place]` | Former residence: the person used to live there [S144], [S146], [S147] | Proof of death, or of death in that place | Black's gives `late` both senses, dead and formerly [S145]. Conclude a death only from a separate word such as `deceased` or from another record. Without one, look for the person in a new place [S146]. Neill reads `late widow of` a named man as usually meaning that she had married again [S147] |
| `indenture` (a form of deed) | Any reciprocal sealed agreement between two or more parties [S8], [S37], [S42], [S41] | Only servitude, apprenticeship or land | Read the operative promises. Premarital agreements in 1760 Boston and 1857 Vermont were also called indentures [S107], [S8] |

## 4. Infer only what the law actually required

- **A young bride is not proof of an unlawful marriage.** Find the statute in force at the exact time and place. Where the common-law rule applied, a male at fourteen and a female at twelve could consent to marry, and a marriage below those ages was inchoate and voidable, not simply impossible [S14], [S48]. Do not assume which rule applied. The Ohio Supreme Court said in 1851 that the common-law rule prevailed in some states while others set the matter by statute, and Ohio's own ages were eighteen and fourteen [S83].
- **Silence before the age of consent did not settle an underage marriage.** At common law the right to disagree arose on reaching that age, and assent or continuing together afterward confirmed the marriage [S48]. Authority varied: the same Ohio decision treated such a marriage as void absent confirmation after age [S83].
- **Test the claimed age at marriage.** Use the proposed marriage date and the age minimum then in force to calculate the latest qualifying birth date. Compare dated age evidence. A child’s birth date is a separate benchmark if the marriage date is unknown [S14]. Record conflicting ages as a range. Under the Alabama law applied in *Beggs v. State*, an underage marriage was voidable. Check the applicable statute and any court action before inferring its status [S108], [S197].
- **A juror list shows eligibility under that year's act.** On a 1748 Virginia county-court juror list, first identify the jury. Under the 1705 act a county-court trial jury needed a visible estate, real or personal, of 50 pounds sterling, not land ownership. A county grand jury was summoned from freeholders, and a jury trying a crime or title to land needed a freeholder with a visible estate of 100 pounds sterling [S99]. These are legal requirements, not proof of what a man held. A 1748 juror list cannot rule out tavern keepers or mill owners on the basis of the October 1748 act [S23]. That exclusion concerned county grand-jury summonses and came into force only after 10 June 1751 [S98], [S99].
- **A December 25 court date is not an error by itself.** Check when that jurisdiction made Christmas a legal holiday. The 1870 federal act reached only the federal workforce in the District of Columbia, and states adopted the holiday at different dates [S19], [S92], [S93]. No source documented a particular court sitting that day, so treat this as a reason not to correct the date, not as proof that court sat.
- **A will witness was probably not a legatee under that will, and may still be an heir or relative.** Statutes put the witness's own gift at risk rather than barring the witness. Virginia's 1785 act voided a bequest to a witness only if the will could not otherwise be proved, kept the witness compellable to testify, and saved to a witness who would have inherited without the will a share up to the value of the legacy [S132]. The Illinois Wills Act that its Supreme Court quoted in 1909 voided a gift to a subscribing witness unless enough other witnesses attested the will [S133]. So read a witness as probably not a beneficiary of that will, never as proof that he was no heir or kin, and check the statute for the place and date [S134], [S135], [S136].
- **A late or out-of-state remarriage after a divorce can come from the divorce law.** Check the act in force at the decree for a waiting period or a bar [S160]. New York's 1829 Revised Statutes barred a defendant divorced for adultery from marrying again while the former spouse lived; from 1879 a court could lift the bar after five years, if the former spouse had remarried and the defendant's conduct had been uniformly good [S157]. Tennessee's code barred a defendant guilty of adultery from marrying the other party to it during the former spouse's life, and in 1937 its Supreme Court applied the bar only to a defendant adjudged guilty of adultery [S158]. Illinois required divorced people to wait one year before remarrying, and the party decreed guilty of adultery two years, from 1905 to 1923 [S159], [S175]. People evaded such laws by marrying in another state, so search neighbouring states for the remarriage; in 1912 the Illinois Supreme Court held such a marriage by Illinois residents invalid [S157], [S159].
- **Marriage into the Cherokee Nation did not end U.S. citizenship.** For a U.S.-citizen woman adopted into the Cherokee Nation in Indian Territory by intermarriage, search Cherokee court records for her civil suits with tribal members. Do not read later naturalization papers as proof she had lost citizenship. The decision rests on Cherokee treaties and does not establish this for recognized tribes generally [S7], [S71].
- **Compare the Dawes roll, enrollment card and application.** The card records Commission and Interior actions, while the application can preserve testimony and correspondence [S129]. A reported change from Cherokee by blood to citizen by intermarriage followed by removal makes each dated action worth checking [S127]. For an allotment claim based on intermarriage, compare the marriage date and governing decision. *Red Bird v. United States* (5 November 1906) addressed rights in common tribal property for whites marrying Cherokee citizens after 1 November 1875 [S128]. A transcribed Commission decision of 10 January 1907 applied it in denying enrollment, with Interior’s affirmance dated 26 January [S130]. Record the actual ground in the applicant’s file.

### War of 1812 pension envelopes

**Read each bounty-land warrant under the act named on the envelope.** Record the act, acreage and warrant number together. The Just Genealogy video suggests reading the acreage by act and considering that a veteran may have skipped an earlier application, as reported in the supplied transcript excerpts [S191†].

**Use the 1850 acreage as a clue to service.** For a veteran’s award, the act of 28 September 1850 ordinarily paired 80 acres with a six-month engagement and at least four months served, and 160 acres with an engagement for twelve months or the duration of the war and at least nine months served. The 40-acre tier required at least one month served on a fixed or indefinite engagement [S192]. Check for an honorable discharge for a disability incurred in service, credited captivity [S192], and travel credit allowed by the 1852 act [S193]. Establish actual service from the application and service records [S195].

**Read a second 80 acres under the 1855 act as the balance.** If the same veteran’s envelope lists 80 acres under each act, the second warrant brings the total to 160 acres. The act of 3 March 1855 gave eligible claimants the balance after crediting earlier warrants and generally required fourteen days of service or participation in a battle [S194].

**Treat a lone 1855 warrant as an incomplete record of earlier claims.** If a veteran’s envelope shows an approved survivor pension and only a 160-acre warrant under the 1855 act, keep both 1850 eligibility and whether the veteran applied under that act unresolved. The pension had separate eligibility rules, and the file can contain bounty-land applications omitted from the envelope [S195]. Test the documented service against the 1850 thresholds and check statutory bars, including entitlement to an earlier military land bounty [S192], [S195].

## 5. Check inheritance and widows' rights against the date

- **North Carolina land.** Before the April 1784 act, an intestate father's land passed to the oldest son. Do not apply that rule after it. The act gave land to all sons equally, and to daughters only if there were no sons [S5], [S67]. One secondary source says daughters shared with sons from 1795 [S68].
- **Virginia land passing by intestacy.** Check the death date against 1 January 1787. Earlier, the oldest son took the land, with daughters taking together only when there were no sons. From that date sons and daughters inherited jointly, with no preference by age or sex among children. The act kept a male preference among lineal ancestors [S12], [S80], [S82].
- **Pennsylvania intestate estates before 19 April 1794.** Under the act of 12 January 1705/6, the widow took one third of the clear personal estate and the children took the rest in equal portions, the eldest son taking two shares. Land the court did not order sold was divided among the widow and children, again with a double share to the eldest son, and the widow held her part only for life [S124]. Check the beneficiary’s identity, the death date and the law in force before using a double share to identify an eldest son. Childs reports both Jacob Fisher’s intestate death and a double-share distribution in 1796 (Berks County estate file no. 1766) [S123]. The reported dates fall after the change in the law [S125]. Check the original packet for the terms and dates before explaining that share. Read a smaller share as a possible advancement: the act counted what a child had received in the father's lifetime against that child's portion [S124]. The double share was still argued before the state's Supreme Court in 1793 [S126]. The act of 19 April 1794 gave the children equal parts, with the widow's third of the personal estate absolute and of the land for life, and it saved rights acquired under the former laws [S125]. Test the death date, not the distribution date, against 19 April 1794.
- **An omitted daughter needs independent kinship evidence.** Look for a marriage portion, settlement or receipt. Some testators named a previously provided-for child with a token bequest [S187]. In *Terry v. Foster* (1804) one justice presumed the daughters had received portions at marriage [S185]. The hypothetical 1668 Boston will and 1666 marriage in Jacobus’s exercise show why omission alone cannot settle the relationship [S183], [S184]. Carry the surviving evidence to [proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).
- **Check for an omitted-child statute in force at the death.** In Massachusetts the provincial act of 1700–01 (12 Will. III c. 4) let a child left out of a parent's will claim a proportion of the estate as though the parent had died intestate [S185], [S186]. In 1804 one justice called it a new law cutting into the power to devise, and another read it as reaching entire omission only, so a mention in the will defeated the claim [S185]. Its 1783 revision gave a child left no legacy a proportion "if not advanced" [S186]. If the law applied at death, look in the Massachusetts probate file for the omitted child’s claim. A 1668 will predates the 1700–01 act. Elsewhere, find the statute for the place and date before reading an omission.
- **Read a father's book charges against a child by their form.** Check surviving family account books for sums advanced to adult children [S183]. If such a book survives, an entry against a child can show a portion and name the child, but not every entry is an advancement. In 1861 the Pennsylvania Supreme Court read a charge in the father's general day-book, with the son credited for repayments, as a debt, and said advanced portions were usually entered in a "family book" [S188]. In 1865 the New Hampshire court took charges kept apart from the regular book of accounts, entered for a later adjustment, as evidence of advancements [S189]. In Massachusetts a charge against a child in the ordinary form of an account book had been held no advancement [S190]. Do not assume that a father kept such a book or that it survives.
- **Escheat.** Read `escheat` in American records as property passing to the state for want of anyone competent to inherit [S161]. Who was competent is the statute's question. Under Virginia's 1785 descent act, in force from 1 January 1787, a child born outside marriage inherited on the mother's side, and was legitimated only if the father later married the mother and recognized the child [S80]. In October 1786 the General Assembly, at their request, vested escheatable land in a man's natural son and an intestate natural son's land in his mother [S162]. When such land stayed in the family, look for a petition and private act, as in [state legislative private acts](state-legislative-private-act-petitions.md) [S163].
- **Illinois dower not assigned within a month of the husband's death.** Look in that county's circuit court chancery records for a petition for assignment of dower (Revised Statutes of 1845, ch. 34, sections 18-19). Check the text in force for your date. Read the bill for named minor heirs, taxes and claimed improvements, as in the reported 1880 petition [S20]. Treat them as allegations until checked against the decree. Compare the statute’s procedure [S94].
- **Broad powers in an Alabama will.** When a will gives a spouse everything with full power to sell and convey in fee simple, look for limiting words such as "during her life" and a gift over at death. If present, she may dispose of the property while living, and the remainder takes only what she did not dispose of. Without a limited remainder she took an absolute fee. This is confirmed for Alabama under the Code sections quoted in *Hood v. Bramlett* (1894); check other states [S21], [S100].
- **Look for a premarital agreement.** For propertied people entering a second or later marriage, one may set out what the widow receives instead of dower [S8], [S107], [S109]. Bouvier (1856) recognizes a jointure made before marriage in satisfaction of dower [S43]. For Jewish families, the ketubah is the marriage contract [S8], [S110]. Search family papers and manuscript collections as well as recorded instruments. The Vermont agreement described by Russell was donated [S8].
- **Look beyond estates in a Georgia Court of Ordinary.** FamilySearch county pages list twelve months' support, poor school funds, free persons of color, apprenticeship indentures, lunacy, Confederate pension rolls and estray sales, each for particular counties and years [S3], [S60], [S61], [S62], [S63], [S64], [S65], [S66]. Confirm the series for your county.

## 6. Read a bond as a promise with named people behind it

- **The penal sum was promised, not paid.** When an official bond names a penal sum and sureties, read the sum as a secured promise to pay on default, not money deposited or spent by the officer or sureties. The author says "in most cases", at least until corporate sureties became the norm [S9], [S35], [S36].
- **A marriage bond's sum was never a licence fee.** The groom and his bondsman promised to pay it only if a legal bar to the marriage came to light, and the condition voids the obligation if there is no lawful cause to obstruct the marriage [S164], [S165]. Do not read the sum as money paid or as a measure of the groom's wealth.
- **An executor's or administrator's penal sum is a multiple of an estimate, not the estate's value.** Virginia's 1785 act required an executor's bond in a penalty at least equal to the full value of the estate, with security approved by the court [S132]. In 1847 the Court of Appeals called the general practice of setting it at double the estimated value a proper exercise of that discretion, allowing for unexpected assets and for sureties who might fail [S166]. Read the sum as a rough upper bound on the estate as estimated when the executor qualified, find the multiple used for that place and date before dividing, and compare the inventory [S167], [S168]. The sum shows neither cash deposited nor the executor's own wealth: the sureties, at least together, were expected to answer for it [S166], [S169]. Search each surety, a woman included, in deeds and tax lists [S167].
- **A surety is a lead.** On probate, court, marriage and bastardy bonds, treat a named surety as a lead to a relative or close associate of the principal, and research that relationship [S72], [S73], [S74]. A surety was not necessarily related. The author treats sureties on official bonds the same way [S9]; no independent source was found for that.
- **An enforced bond is a claim of breach, not a finding of theft.** Read enforcement of an official bond as a claimed breach of official duty, as a 1912 Wisconsin case required. A shortage or failure to pay over does not by itself show theft or bribery. Sureties could also answer for a shortage found after the officer's death, as in an Arkansas case [S75], [S76].

## 7. Read cause-of-death terms by their line on the form

These readings come from a 2003 standard form and from current county and federal guidance. Check the form used in your certificate's year.

- **Part I runs backward in time.** On the standard certification, read the first line of Part I as the direct cause and the lowest completed line of Part I as the underlying cause the certifier entered. The lowest line is not always the selected underlying cause: the NCHS manual has further selection rules [S17], [S87], [S86].
- **An earlier injury or illness counts as underlying only through an unbroken sequence** to death, with no intervening cause [S17], [S85].
- **`Proximate` is not the nearest cause in time.** Read `proximate` and `underlying` as the same thing, as Harris County's medical examiner glossary does: the disease or injury that started the chain to death. Read `immediate` as the final event in the sequence, closest to death [S17], [S85], [S88]. The author also treats `ultimate` and `proximal` as equivalents; no independent source was found for those two.
- **Decode a cause-of-death number with the classification used that year.** Copy the number and suffix separately from the certifier’s written cause. Codes can be added later, and some certificates have none [S170], [S171], [S172]. On a reported 1925 New York certificate the bare number was 188 [S170]. The Census Bureau’s 1925 tables distinguish primary automobile accidents (188c) from collisions with trains or streetcars (parts of 188a and 188b) [S198]. Use the written cause to identify the event.

## 8. Write the reading with its rule

For each legal reading you rely on, keep one row:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Words as written | The term or abbreviation, unchanged |
| Place and date | The jurisdiction on the event date |
| Rule and source | Statute, case or dictionary, with edition and year |
| Source level | Primary text read, secondary summary, or rule not found |
| What it supports | What the law required or allowed |
| What it does not show | What the person actually did, owned or was |

A rule shows what was required or allowed. It does not show what happened in one family. Use the row as a dated hypothesis and take identity and kinship rulings to [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).
