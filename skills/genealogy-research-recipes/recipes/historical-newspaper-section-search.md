## Find people in lists and social columns

Read list and table entries as well as long notices for engagements, weddings and milestone anniversaries. For a wedding, capture named attendants or witnesses and the place as separate newspaper observations to compare with the marriage record. [S1], [S2]

When no photograph survives, check local wedding coverage for descriptions of what the bride or attendants wore. Record the clothing as described for that occasion. [S8], [S9]

For a missing associate, browse society columns for meetings, club events and private gatherings. Copy the guest and organizer names with their roles and the event’s date. Party attendance gives a social link to investigate. [S1], [S2]

For a deceased person’s travels and civic ties, browse society columns for visits, parties they hosted and named group memberships, according to Rhonda Lauritzen’s guidance. [S17†], [S18] Check the destination paper using the reported place and date of a visit. Keep a host or guest role distinct from an explicitly stated membership. [S18]

When a deceased person is missing from longer articles, browse letters to the editor for their signature and check brief sick-list notices and local sports results, according to the podcast’s suggestions. [S19†], [S20]

If the first profile ties the person to a conference or award, search for the event itself in the host city and in publications serving the sponsoring religious or civic community. Coverage may be filed under the event or organization. [S1]

For a deceased twentieth-century immigrant, browse local reports of school sports, academic prizes and amateur tournaments, along with legal notices of sales or marriages. [S5]

When U.S. obituaries and community announcements leave a famine-era immigrant’s Irish origin unresolved, look for family names and Irish hometowns in reports on Irish organizations and St. Patrick’s Day parades. [S7]

When an index hit points to a photograph, transcribe the caption’s identifications and record each pictured person separately from people named only in the text. Keep facial resemblance out of the identification evidence. Treat any later recognition as the recognizer’s attributed claim. The 1924 *Cincinnati Enquirer* item indexed under a mother’s name pictured her daughters. [S27]

## Use the classifieds to recover the right kind of lead

Trace a family business through repeated advertisements. Compare dated address and opening-hour entries across issues so a single ad supplies one dated observation. [S1], [S2]

For a suspected move, compare advertisements in the old and new towns. Record each advertised address with its issue date and leave the actual move date open. [S4]

After a proprietor’s death, check later issues for stock offered in the estate’s name and declarations that someone will continue the firm. Record who placed each notice. When a move announcement is followed by an order notice, assign each address its stated role, including a shop at the old address that still took orders. [S39], [S40]

To trace a vanished building, search by historical street address or lot number in auction notices, demolition orders and demolition reports. Record the advertised or reported sale, what was sold, the removal deadline and any planned replacement as separate observations. If the sale covers buildings for removal, attach its price to those buildings and leave the lot’s ownership to separate evidence. Check subsequent issues for completion or corrections. [S36], [S37], [S38]

For a market gardener, compare relocation notices and dated seed price lists, including winter issues when tracking fresh produce. Keep the goods advertised and selling location with each notice and record a residence only when the wording identifies it as such. [S55]

When advertisements place a product in local shops and you need to identify its maker, search exhibition prize lists for an exhibitor expressly credited with producing it. Record the retail offer and the maker attribution separately. [S57]

When an immigrant disappears from the expected destination, browse the classifieds for *Information Wanted* and *Missing Friends*. Search for both the advertiser and the missing person. The Boston *Pilot* used this genre for Irish immigrant notices. Use stated origins and migration details as leads to the records for each person. [S1], [S3]

For an indexed route to the *Pilot* ads, consult *Boston Pilot: Irish Immigrant Advertisements (Search for Missing Friends), 1831–1920* at American Ancestors. [S54 @2026-11-22] Look for the compiled advertisements by title, according to the presenter’s guidance. [S28† @2026-11-22]

For *Boston Pilot* Missing Friends hits, compare parish and county, last-known American locality and named associates to distinguish candidates. Keep birthplace, marriage place, last-known whereabouts and the address for replies in their stated roles. [S29], [S30] According to the presenter, use the issue date to interpret elapsed-time phrases, verify them under *Information Wanted* in that issue and check matching ads in later issues for repeat insertions. [S28†], [S30] Record each printing date while counting a repeated ad as one inquiry. [S30]

Check Boston College Libraries’ digitized newspaper guide for coverage of the dated *Pilot* issue under the title used that year. [S31 @2026-11-22]

Where the issue survives, browse it for reports of a death in Ireland or of travel to or from Ireland, according to the presenter’s guidance. [S28†]

## Find a correspondent in letters and editorial replies

For a locality or writer in Finnish-language newspapers, search Translocalis by place or author metadata and open the relevant issue. Its selection covers local reader letters published in the Finnish-language press from 1775 through 1885. Search the paper itself for other kinds of contributions or dates outside that range before weighing a missing hit. [S44 @2026-11-22], [S45 @2026-11-22], [S46 @2026-11-22]

If a contribution is missing, browse published replies to correspondents by initials and locality. Record a rejection that identifies the submission as evidence of sending it. Follow later replies if a letter started an exchange. When several papers give the same unexpected spelling or job title, follow the credited source and read later issues for a signed correction before treating the printings as independent. [S42], [S43]

## Recover missing articles and installments

If the issue containing a disputed report is missing, search surviving issues of the same title for a published reply. Cite the reply for the topic it addresses and leave the missing report’s wording and treatment unresolved. [S58]

Follow a serial’s title or continuation heading through later issues even when the next issue has no installment. Check each installment’s page afresh and cite it with its own issue date and page. [S59]

If a serial is absent from issue scans, check press histories for how the installments were distributed. According to the author’s reading of a contemporary description, installments that accompanied a paper can be a lead to separately issued sheets. [S60†] Search for the supplement as its own catalogue item and check physical holdings for separate sheets. [S61], [S62]

For a striking report in a nineteenth-century Finnish issue around April 1, search subsequent issues for an explanation or reveal before entering the event. Check whether other titles credited the same story. On April 1, 1899, *Tampereen Uutiset* ran both an advertisement and a news item for the same fictitious animal show. [S63], [S64]

## Trace travelers through shipping and arrival columns

When a traveler is missing from other records, browse arrival and departure columns in ports on the route. Record stated companions and relationships, where the party came from, whom they stayed with and the lodging address. Compare repeat visits for fuller names or addresses and keep temporary lodging separate from evidence of residence. [S40], [S41], [S49]

In a published travel letter, use the dateline for the writer’s stated place and date of writing and keep the issue date with the citation. Read through any description of a typical journey to the writer’s own account of boarding before extracting an embarkation place. [S47]

## Trace people in enslavement coverage

For an enslaved person, browse sale and escape advertisements, newspaper court reports and abolitionist newspapers for names, descriptions and places. If the person’s name is missing, search by the named enslaver, estate or locality. [S21], [S22], [S23] When a report identifies a freedom suit, use the parties and court to look for the underlying petition. Keep details from the report and the petition attributed to their respective sources. [S21], [S23], [S24]

## Find notices outside the newspaper

If a historical Finnish auction or magistrate’s notice is missing from the paper, check whether the local title was publishing at the time. Search retrospective articles by town, notice type and terms such as *rumpali* (drummer) or *kuulutus* (proclamation) for accounts of street-corner announcements. Look for wording preserved in the later account. Date the notice separately from the account and record who drummed and who read the notice when the account distinguishes them. A 1919 recollection describes Kuopio auctions announced by a drummer and recalls that city notices did not appear in *Tapio*. [S65]

For a wanted notice in historical Finland, look for printed provincial proclamations alongside newspaper notices. Use the issuing province and date to locate the proclamation. Use any stated birthplace and approximate age to narrow candidates. Record build, hair and clothing at escape separately from earlier service details such as rank and regiment. [S66], [S67]

## Follow probate notices to the estate

When a will is hard to find, check the local paper’s legal notices for probate or estate notices. Use the named court and any hearing date to guide the search for the will or other estate papers. [S6], [S10]

If the estate inventory notice leads to no will notice, search retrospective profiles for reported bequests and burial instructions. Use the details as leads to the will and attribute them to the profile until you have the document. [S56]

If the death-notice search turns up a summons about the deceased’s debts, take the court and parties from that notice and browse later issues for the reported bankruptcy decision, as in the *Posttidningar* case. Record the summons and decision as distinct stages. [S41]

For a missing heir or spouse, search notice columns as well as news of the disappearance. Read the notice for whom it seeks and why. Establish any obligation to publish from separate evidence for that place and period. [S48]

## Follow divorce and annulment notices to the case

For a suspected historical divorce with a missing case record, search the legal notices for both parties and headings such as *Notice of Summons*, according to Judy Russell’s guidance on published service. [S50†], [S32], [S33] Record the court, county and any complaint filing date for the case search. Keep the summons, publication order and final decree as separate events. [S33]

Also search for a spouse’s notice disclaiming responsibility for debts after the other spouse left the household, according to Judy Russell’s guidance. [S50†], [S51] Record its place, date and signer’s account as a separation lead. Look for a case record before recording a divorce. [S51]

For a suspected historical annulment, search local legal notices and follow-up news for the parties and the reported stage of proceedings. [S52]

## Reconstruct an unusual death away from home

Search the news columns in towns near the incident as well as the deceased person’s home paper. Keep the initial account and follow-up reports as dated observations. Record their witnesses, sequence and conflicting details separately. [S1]

For someone missing after a disaster, check papers in the affected area and the person’s former hometown for casualty and missing-person lists, hospital admissions, relief reports, funerals and accounts of survivors. Pair a surname with a period address, employer, neighborhood or relative. Browse with event and status terms such as *reported missing* and *among the injured*, then track subsequent reports before recording a death. [S34], [S35]

For a historical Finnish missing-person notice, record the reported disappearance and any date it gives, according to the author’s distinction between a disappearance and a declaration of death. [S68†] Look for the declaration in the court records for the person’s last Finnish domicile. Record the date of the decision and the death date it assigns separately from the disappearance. [S69]

When an out-of-town death report describes a letter found on the deceased, look for the postmark even if the writer is unnamed, as reported in the podcast’s newspaper segment. [S19†], [S25] Record its locality and date as printed and look for the candidate or their associates in records from that locality. Keep the mailing place, the address on the letter and any evidence of residence separate. [S26]

To test an oral account about a deceased ancestor, search the name as it would appear in print, including any title, with an incident keyword from the story. Read each promising article for the event it describes. In the reported example, a trolley-accident search turned up a refugee-arrival article. [S4]
