# Recover a Quaker ancestor from monthly-meeting minutes and removal certificates

Use this recipe when a Hinshaw or Ancestry index entry points to a Quaker meeting or when you need to identify candidate meetings for a family's place and dates. Before relying on Hinshaw, check that it covers the meeting. Decode the entry's meeting before you assign it to a place. Then read the original men's and women's minutes and both removal-certificate series as images. Keep each numbered date exactly as written.

Some steps belong to other recipes. To test a disputed year against the documented calendar and follow record versions, use [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md) § Follow record versions and test disputed dates. For general Ancestry zero-result diagnosis, use [Ancestry record-search recovery](ancestry-record-search-recovery.md).

## Identify the meeting and reported record holder

Use Thomas C. Hill’s Monthly Meetings in North America index at QuakerMeetings.com to identify candidate meetings, former names, parent bodies and reported record locations. Resolve the meeting for the date before selecting its books. The maintainers warn that not all website and record-location data can be kept current, so a listed repository is a custody lead to confirm, not a current holding proved by the index. [S19 @2026-11-16], [S20 @2026-11-16]

For a colonial Mid-Atlantic family, include the minutes of monthly meetings that served its locality even if the family’s affiliation is unknown. Start with the best-supported candidate and search nearby monthly meetings that also fit the family’s dates and places. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter suggests both searches [S34†]. If a preparative meeting was set off as a monthly meeting during the period, check the former parent and the new monthly meeting on either side of the change, even if the family stayed put. For Goose Creek in Loudoun County, for example, check Fairfax Monthly Meeting’s minutes during Goose Creek’s preparative years from 1774 to its set-off in 1785 [S35].

For Waterford families in Loudoun County, Virginia, check both spouses’ death notices in Patricia B. Duncan’s *Genealogical Abstracts from the Telephone, Loudoun County, Virginia* (1881–1896) [S41]. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter reads a statement of Society of Friends membership in one spouse’s notice and a Friends meetinghouse funeral in the other [S40†]. Attribute each clue to its own notice. Use a Waterford Friends meetinghouse reference to test Fairfax Monthly Meeting as a candidate [S42]. Keep the funeral venue separate from any claim about either spouse’s membership.

For a Hopewell Monthly Meeting family in Frederick County, Virginia, with a gap during the French and Indian War, look for Jesse Pugh and Isaac Hollingsworth’s November 1757 relief list [S49]. Search the miscellaneous papers of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting’s Meeting for Sufferings [S51] and check its *Minutes, 1756–1775*, HC.PhY.040.01.001, for related entries [S50]. The 1757 list describes families already back home and families still away [S49]. Follow any documented destination into the meeting records for that place and check surviving Hopewell minutes for the family during and after the war. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter describes wartime departures and later returns [S48†].

## Check compiled-abstract coverage before trusting silence

1. Record whether the target meeting falls within Hinshaw's *Encyclopedia of American Quaker Genealogy*. This is a six-volume work compiled by William Wade Hinshaw and published 1936–1950. A separate index volume followed in 1999. It covers mainly meetings east of the Mississippi River, not the whole United States [S1]. The printed volumes are North Carolina (v. 1), Pennsylvania and New Jersey (v. 2), New York (v. 3), Ohio (v. 4–5) and Virginia (v. 6). Indiana is not in the printed set; Willard Heiss abstracted Indiana separately.
2. Keep the printed Encyclopedia and Ancestry's *Hinshaw Index to Selected Quaker Records, 1680–1940* apart. The reported figure of 197 meetings in 17 states was not verified against Ancestry's current collection description; confirm coverage there before relying on that count. The presenter demonstrates why the distinction matters: Walnut Ridge Monthly Meeting, Indiana appears on an index card even though Indiana is absent from the printed volumes [S1]. Record which of the two you checked.
3. If the meeting is outside the work you checked, or you cannot confirm it is inside, do not read an empty Hinshaw result as a negative. Record the result as "not covered" or "coverage unconfirmed," not "no record."
4. Even inside its coverage, treat a Hinshaw abstract as a pointer back to the monthly-meeting books. It is not a substitute for them [S1].
5. For Ancestry's Quaker minutes (titled U.S. Quaker meeting records, 1681–1935), read the database description before searching. The 2017 presenter reports that the description gives date-style tips and notes the 1827 split that affected record-keeping. In 2017 she gave the coverage as 20 states plus the District of Columbia, with nearly 6.5 million names indexed and many more people in the images left unindexed [S2]. Record the current description's coverage and name counts in your packet, and use those figures.

## Decode the meeting code, then collect surname leads

1. Read the meeting code at the top of each Hinshaw index entry. Translate it with the index's own key before you assign the entry to a meeting. If the entry itself does not explain the code, inspect the first card in the sequence: its key identified Walnut Ridge Monthly Meeting, Indiana [S1]. Do not guess a meeting from the surname's state distribution or from a nearby entry.
2. On Ancestry, find the Hinshaw items through the Card Catalog keyword search. Several Hinshaw-related titles turn up, including the *Hinshaw Index to Selected Quaker Records, 1680–1940*, a cross-index to it, and the Encyclopedia itself [S1]. Note which one you actually searched.
3. Once the meeting is decoded, search the meeting name together with the surname to gather every entry for that family in that meeting [S1]. Keep these entries as leads. A shared surname within one meeting does not establish one household or kinship.
4. Keep the literal code, the decoded meeting name, and the key you used side by side, so that another researcher can check a misread code.
5. Before you read an event from an entry, decode its abbreviations with the list at the front of the Hinshaw volume (Volume I, page xv) [S7], [S8]. Common ones: rocf = received on certificate from; gct = granted certificate to; rq = requested; rqct = requested certificate to; dis = disowned (for); mou = married out of unity (one partner not a Friend, or of another branch); mcd = married contrary to discipline; con = condemned (the member acknowledged his or her own misconduct); rmt = reported married to; d = died; dec = deceased; dt = daughter. "dis mou" records a disownment for marrying out of unity, not a death or a move. Copy the abbreviation literally and put your expansion beside it.

In the Hinshaw collections, expand `exms` as “excused from military service” [S30]. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter suggests using the abbreviation as a cue for military-related entries [S29†]. Follow the cue to the meeting entry and record the excuse in the terms the entry uses.

## Read both sexes' minutes and certificate series

1. Men and women met separately, and each kept its own minutes. Search both the men's minutes and the women's minutes for every meeting. Search the removal certificates for both men and women, in every series the custodian's finding aid sets out, whether arranged by sex, by branch or both [S1]. List each series you inspected, with its date span. A search of only one sex's series is partial, and so is a search of only one branch's where both branches existed for the target place and period.
2. Expect marriages and removal certificates to be the main records. The presenter says births and deaths are often incomplete and birthplaces were usually not recorded [S1]. A missing birth, death or birthplace is not negative evidence.
3. The presenter says that when a new meeting was set off, no membership list was made: Friends already living within its boundary became its members automatically. For someone who later moved, a removal certificate to the next meeting is the clearest proof of membership in the earlier meeting. For someone who never moved, she points to marriage records and disciplinary minutes as the places the person appears [S1]. Search for the outgoing certificate in the earlier meeting and the matching receipt in the receiving meeting. Some meetings did keep membership lists in later periods (for example, Goshen from 1828), so check whether a list survives for your meeting and years. Never assign membership from residence within the boundary alone.
4. Read the full image of each marriage minute or certificate for the witness list. The 2017 presenter reports that witnesses appear in the records but were not indexed [S2]. Transcribe the witnesses and other associates from the image. Never infer them from the name index.
5. For any entry dated after the Hicksite/Orthodox separation in the meeting's own yearly meeting, record which branch kept it. The 2017 presenter notes that the split affected record-keeping [S2]. New York Yearly Meeting's historical statement dates the separation to 1827 for Philadelphia, and to 1828 for New York, Ohio, Indiana and Baltimore. It also records later Wilburite/Conservative separations of yearly meetings, in New England in 1845 and New York in 1853 [S5]. In New York the Orthodox quarterly meetings began to divide earlier: Scipio and several others split in 1847, and the Wilburite yearly meeting at Poplar Ridge was founded in 1853. The Wilburites divided again in 1859, into Otisite and Kingite branches [S6]. So a New York meeting can have a Wilburite branch from 1847. Take the keeping meeting's affiliation and dates from the custodian's finding aid, not from the date alone. Where both branches had a meeting in the area, search both sets of books. A gap in one branch's books is not absence from the Society.

6. For an emigrant from Britain or Ireland, start from the removal certificate received in the American meeting: it normally names the issuing meeting and its date. It may be copied into a separate book of recorded certificates, or kept as an original, rather than noted in the minutes. Early minutes seldom record a receipt [S9]. Then search that meeting's records:
   - **England and Wales:** the Quaker registers surrendered to the Registrar General (TNA RG 6, 1578–1841), searchable on TheGenealogist, Ancestry and Findmypast. Friends House Library in London holds the digest registers that Friends compiled from them by surname, on microfilm, and a later digest sequence from 1837. Most meeting minutes are in local record offices, not at Friends House. [S10], [S11]
   - **Ireland:** Friends Historical Library, Dublin, holds the archives of Quakers in Ireland. Documents from 1670 to 1914, including migration records naming the origin and destination meetings, are on Findmypast. [S12]

   A matching name in a British or Irish register is a candidate until the certificate's meeting and dates agree with it.

7. For a meeting in Maryland, find which of its books were filmed in Phebe R. Jacobsen's *Quaker Records in Maryland* (Maryland Hall of Records publications, no. 14, 1966) [S15]. It lists the Quaker records that the Hall of Records in Annapolis, now the Maryland State Archives, had microfilmed, with their reel numbers. It covers Baltimore Yearly Meeting and the Maryland meetings of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting. It also traces Virginia Yearly Meeting's meetings and the books filmed before their transfer to Haverford, according to Hill's Quaker index, which was only partially read [S14]. The filming took in both branches of Baltimore Yearly Meeting: the Hicksite records then kept at Stony Run and the Orthodox records kept at Homewood [S17].

   A community video recommends a Maryland guide of this kind without naming it; only a transcript excerpt of that video was seen, and the video itself was not inspected [S13†]. Treat Jacobsen's reel numbers and locations as 1966 values. A book missing from her lists is a retrieval gap to check, not evidence that the book is lost.

   Take the current holder and call number from today's catalogues, not from Jacobsen. The Hicksite branch deposited its records at Friends Historical Library, Swarthmore, in 1967–1969. The Orthodox branch deposited its own and Virginia Yearly Meeting's records at Haverford's Quaker Collection in the 1960s, according to Hill's Quaker index, which was only partially read [S14 @2026-11-16]. Search the Quaker Meeting Catalog that Swarthmore and Haverford share [S16 @2026-11-16]. Also search the Maryland State Archives' Guide to Special Collections for Maryland churches, whose Friends entries give the Archives' collection numbers (only the first page of results was read) [S18 @2026-11-16]. Record Jacobsen's reel number and the current catalogue entry side by side.

8. A birth recorded by a monthly meeting is not thereby a birth in that meeting’s locality. New-member entries can repeat birth and marriage information for the family; distinguish the stated birthplace, residence and receiving meeting, and retain an unstated entry date as unknown. Do not move a child’s birthplace to every meeting that later recorded the family. [S21]

9. Read the intention preamble of a marriage certificate as well as its vows and witness list. The published 1769 preamble identifies Azariah Lewis as Nathan’s son of Newtown Township and Hannah Scott as James’s daughter of Easttown Township, both in Chester County. Assign each place to the person and role actually named; a witness’s place or surname does not supply a missing parent or birthplace. [S22]

10. Keep a removal certificate’s issue and receipt dates separate from the physical move. Some families obtained the certificate after migrating. Bracket the physical move only if evidence establishes the person’s presence at both endpoints; do not assign the certificate date as an exact departure or arrival date. [S23], [S24]

11. Browse the selected meeting’s books in addition to searching its name index. Guilford College’s Ancestry guide describes the state → county → meeting → book route and warns that the viewer image number can differ from the record page. List the record types and books actually inspected; an indexed surname miss does not search every image or a neighbouring meeting. [S25 @2026-11-16], [S26 @2026-11-16]

For a deceased ancestor’s Irish vital event, search the *Ireland, Society of Friends (Quaker)* births, marriages or deaths collection on Findmypast [S43 @2026-11-22], [S44 @2026-11-22], [S45 @2026-11-22], [S46 @2026-11-22]. If the name search misses, use *Ireland, Society of Friends (Quaker) Browse* to inspect volumes that fit the target meeting and years [S47 @2026-11-22].

## Read disciplinary labels and emancipation instruments

1. When a Revolutionary-era source labels a Friend a Loyalist, read the person’s entries in the monthly-meeting minutes for the act behind the label. Religious refusal to swear an allegiance oath, pay war taxes or support military action can differ from active support for the Crown. Keep the source’s label and the documented conduct separate; neither pacifism nor a label alone establishes the individual’s political allegiance. [S23], [S27]

2. In North Carolina Quaker minutes, a statement that someone was freed “by instrument” is a lead to a separate emancipation paper. Search the meeting papers and relevant local court or deed records for the instrument itself. Hilty describes both court-issued deeds and private papers made without court approval; the phrase alone does not prove a recorded deed, immediate freedom or legal recognition. [S25], [S28]

For a woman’s disciplinary case, start in the women’s minutes and compare the men’s entry for the same case. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter prefers to read the women’s minutes first [S34†]. In Dartmouth, the women’s minutes give the grounds for Katurah Peckham’s disownment, which the men’s concurrence entry omits [S38], [S39]. Record the stated conduct and each meeting’s action under their own citations.

When a case was appealed, identify the quarterly and yearly meetings for the monthly meeting’s date and branch. Follow the appeal into the quarterly minutes and, if it went further, into the yearly minutes. Look for separately filed appeal papers and copies of the lower meetings’ minutes [S36], [S37]. Record the decision at each level you can document. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter suggests looking beyond the monthly meeting to both higher levels [S34†].

## Trace military-related discipline and verify service

Browse the monthly meeting’s minutes on both sides of a military-related disciplinary entry. Put the first complaint or report, committee visits and reports, and the meeting’s decision in date order wherever they survive [S31]. According to the supplied transcript excerpt, the presenter suggests tracing the case across entries and checking later minutes for a return to membership [S29†]. After a disownment, look for an acknowledgment and the meeting’s separate decision restoring membership before recording a return [S32].

For a Revolutionary-era disownment, record the stated cause and distinguish enlistment from other support for the war effort [S31], [S32]. If the minutes say enlistment was reported, retain it as a report received by the meeting and identify the informant if named [S31]. Check the military records that fit the conflict and place for evidence of the service itself, including relevant compiled service records, pension applications and bounty-land applications [S33].

If early U.S. military records are silent about religion, leave Quaker affiliation open and look for membership or disciplinary entries in the candidate meeting for the period of service [S31]. If a pension affidavit mentions affiliation, record who made the statement [S33].

## Check meeting-related court proceedings when minutes are unavailable

If early minutes for the target meeting are unavailable, use its locality and period to look for court proceedings concerning Quaker gatherings, then inspect whom they name as attending. For a Salem gathering on 29 June 1658, Ruane used a list of attendees in court records [S3]. Record the named attendance and its source. Membership requires separate evidence from the meeting.

## Preserve numbered dates literally

1. Copy each date exactly as written: day, numbered month (Roman or Arabic numerals), and year. Put the converted modern date in square brackets after it, for example "27 IV 1731 [June 1731]" [S1]. Never replace the literal value with the conversion.
2. Consult Swarthmore’s [Quaker Calendar](https://www.swarthmore.edu/friends-historical-library/quaker-calendar) and its worked examples [S4]. In the old English calendar the year began on 25 March and First Month was March, so “iv” is June. From 1752 First Month was January. Check this change before converting a numbered month [S4].
3. Flag any single-dated entry from January to 24 March before 1752 as year-ambiguous. The presenter says such a writer was usually, but not always, anticipating the new year [S1], and Swarthmore's examples treat it the same way. Record both candidate years, and send the choice to the evidence/proof recipe linked above.
4. Search online indexes with the modern form, which the presenter says most systems expect. Keep the literal form in your notes. Do not add an "O.S." suffix inside search fields, because the presenter warns it can confuse search algorithms [S1].

## Close with a meeting-scoped packet

Return a packet with these items:

- the decoded meeting, with its literal code and the key used, plus the Hill-index lookup and check of its listed record holder;
- the other candidate monthly meetings searched and any change in which meeting kept the records for the locality;
- the Hinshaw coverage check (printed Encyclopedia or the Ancestry index) and its result;
- each minute series inspected (men's and women's, and the branch after the yearly meeting's separation), with its span, books and record types actually browsed, and record pages kept apart from viewer image numbers;
- each removal-certificate series inspected, with outgoing and receiving pairs, issue and receipt dates as membership-transfer observations, and any physical-move bounds with their supporting presence evidence;
- for an emigrant from Britain or Ireland, the received certificate's issuing meeting and the registers searched there;
- for a Maryland meeting, Jacobsen's 1966 reel number beside the current holder and call number;
- witness and associate lists transcribed from images, and any parents and residence places extracted from a marriage preamble in their named roles;
- each stated birthplace and residence kept apart from the recording meeting, with an unstated entry date left unknown;
- any Loyalist label beside the conduct recorded in minutes, and any “by instrument” reference beside the paper retrieved or its retrieval gap;
- for an appealed disciplinary case, the decisions documented in quarterly and yearly minutes or separate appeal papers;
- for a military-related disciplinary case, the dated meeting actions and stated cause, any recorded restoration, and the evidence for affiliation and service;
- every date as a literal value with a bracketed conversion, plus any year-ambiguity flag.

Name any series you did not inspect as a retrieval gap. Index silence remains "not found in the searched index." It is never a finding that the person was not a member.
