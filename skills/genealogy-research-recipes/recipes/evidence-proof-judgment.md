# Recipe: Correlating evidence & proving identity — the judgment AI gets wrong

## Goal

How an AI assistant (or a genealogist using one) can correlate documentary + DNA evidence and rule on **identity and proof** without inheriting the model's biases — what the human must decide, where the model reliably fails, and the techniques that actually separate same-named people and reconcile conflicts.

## The mental model (proof is one correlated argument, not a pile of facts)

One lens explains most of the modes below — call it **narrative gravity** (this recipe's own shorthand, consistent with known LLM coherence/mode-collapse bias, *not* a term the genealogy sources use). A language model is trained to produce the single tidiest coherent story — and genealogical proof is the *opposite* discipline. Proof is direction-neutral — it splits or unifies identities as the evidence dictates (two men named David, or one Henry assembled from two record sets), keeps conflicts **open** until reconciled, and refuses the neat link. So the model's instincts are systematically inverted from what proof requires: it merges where you must separate, votes where you must weigh, and states an answer where you must build an argument. (Two modes below — skipped impossibility checks and dismissed negative evidence — are as much plain omission as coherence-seeking; the lens is a mnemonic for the pattern, not a claim of one literal cause.)

Two consequences follow, and they are the whole recipe: (1) treat the model as a fast assistant that **assembles the dossier and drafts the prose**, never as the authority that **rules** on identity or proof (the sibling's "every AI output is a hypothesis to verify" frame applies wholesale here). (2) Proof is **one correlated argument** — DNA and documents converging on a single hypothesis, conflicts explained, negatives ruled out — not a stack of facts the model can total.

## Hard rules — apply these before you rule (the load-bearing directives)

1. **Stop extending a questionable link as established ancestry and gather its sources** — before any conclusion, merge, or DNA attribution through that link. You can investigate beyond it in an explicitly conditional scenario; keep the premise and every dependent claim visible using the working-hypothesis procedure below.
2. **No identity claim until the descriptor dossier exists — and sort the table BY descriptor, not by name.** A distinct verified signature (or any strong descriptor) can support a split, a matching one a union — but only corroborated by the rest of the dossier, never as a lone identity test.
3. **Any unresolved biological or chronological impossibility BLOCKS the merge** — however smooth the prose reads. A genuine impossibility (childbearing window, post-mortem fatherhood) separates; a softer anomaly (a duplicate census enumeration, a household member who appears then vanishes) is a CONFLICT to investigate, not an automatic separator.
4. **Keep same-name candidates in SEPARATE research projects** so their records never cross-contaminate.
5. **Weigh sources, don't count them.** Five online trees copying one claim aren't five witnesses — they're one unverified claim repeated. Classify each *statement* by whether its informant had firsthand knowledge of THAT fact (a death certificate's informant usually knew the death firsthand, the birth only secondhand) — never stamp a whole record "primary" or "secondary."
6. **A record's mere mention of a person doesn't prove a relationship it never asserts** — but an unstated relationship can still be proved by an explicit, written correlation of indirect evidence (never by assertion). A parent's signature on a standard-form marriage bond does **not** prove a minor bride.
7. **"No record found" is a search result, not evidence** — and absence of a death record is not evidence of life. **Log every search with its date** (online coverage changes over time).
8. **An indirect conclusion must be WRITTEN as an explicit correlation argument**, never asserted as if direct.
9. **Document each DNA test-taker's parent-child descent to the common ancestor BEFORE the match counts** — then require paper descent and genetic sharing to converge on **one** hypothesis, and judge match **quality**, not count.
10. **On a collaborative tree, read the change log bottom-up** to recover the profile's original intended identity before untangling.
11. **You rule on identity and proof. The model assembles and drafts — it never concludes.**

## What the HUMAN must own (never delegate to an AI conclusion)

- **Framing the question and the standard of proof.** Decide upfront exactly what you're proving (an identity? a specific parent-child link? an event?) and what would COUNT as proof — a reasoned probabilistic argument, not certainty. A wrong or vague frame upstream invalidates everything below it, and the model will happily "prove" a question you never meant to ask.
- **The merge-vs-separate ruling on same-named people.** The AI can build the descriptor dossier; *you* decide whether the records describe one person or two. A wrong merge cascades — false relationships, false record hints, combined families across generations — and reshapes the whole tree above it. Separating is the harder call and runs against the model's reduce-entities instinct. (Kathryn Grant; Jan Joyce, RLP 399)
- **The reconciliation of conflicting evidence.** Assign weight by informant proximity and source *independence*, per **statement** (records are hybrid), and articulate *why* the sources disagree. Correlation is reasoning about reliability, not tallying — the model's "pick the popular value" default is exactly the error to override. (Power of Proof; Slagle)
- **The sufficiency judgment on negative evidence.** Decide when a search was exhaustive enough — record loss, incomplete coverage, indexing error all ruled out — that an absence now carries evidentiary weight. It hinges on jurisdiction and collection coverage the model can gather and compare, but only the human can verify it and make the sufficiency judgment; both over- and under-claiming are cheap for a model to state confidently. (RLP 409)
- **The correlation of DNA and documents into a single argument.** When a proof relies on DNA it must be integrated INTO the documentary argument, not run beside it — the model instead hands you two independent verdicts ("DNA suggests X; records suggest Y"). An undocumented descent path means a match can't yet be attributed to the proposed common ancestor; and a sufficiently documented conclusion can meet the standard without DNA at all. (Why Document DNA Links; Coverage)
- **The final evidentiary ruling.** "Can I explain, source, and defend this in the real historical record?" The model drafts the proof summary; the human rules on whether the standard is actually met. Proof validation is professional responsibility, not a delegable output. (Denyse Allen, Lesson 5; How to Know if You've Met the GPS)
- **The discipline to stop and verify *before* asserting ancestry.** Check whether each source belongs to the correct person and what it actually contributes to the kinship claim. Stop extending a questionable link as fact. Conditional exploration is useful when the unproved premise stays explicit; the human owns whether the eventual argument supports the link. (Wrong Until Proven Right; Jan Joyce, RLP 399; Using Hypotheses)

## Where AI reliably goes wrong (the named failure modes — never trust)

- **Same-name collapse (entity-merging bias).** Given two record sets sharing a name and rough place/era, the model defaults to ONE person and stitches a single coherent timeline. It treats a shared name as identity evidence when a name is the **weakest** discriminator (a FamilySearch example counts **hundreds of thousands of "John Smith"** profiles). Tell: it fuses "Maryland Henry" with "Ohio Henry," or absorbs two same-named wives into one profile, without ever demanding the discriminating descriptors. Corrective: no identity claim until the descriptor dossier exists. (Kathryn Grant, Same Name Trap; Jan Joyce, RLP 399; Dewitt case; One Man, Two Same-Named Wives)
- **Skips the reality-check filters.** It won't run the biological/chronological impossibility tests that expose a bad merge — a woman born in the 1800s "bearing" children recorded in the 1600s, a man "fathering" a child ten years after his death, or a **duplicate enumeration** in one census year it never pauses to explain. It accepts an impossible merged timeline as long as the prose reads smoothly. (Kathryn Grant, Same Name Trap & Recognizing Problems; Seventeen Rules)
- **Conflict-flattening (vote-counting instead of reconciliation).** Faced with conflicting evidence, the model picks the majority / most-recent / most-official-**sounding** value and states one answer, instead of weighting each **statement** by informant proximity and explaining *why* they differ. It counts sources rather than judging independence — **five online trees copying one claim aren't five witnesses; they're one unverified claim repeated** — and treats a derivative/secondary statement as equal to a primary one. (Power of Proof; Slagle; Ann Tanner)
- **Over-reading a document beyond what it states.** It infers a relationship or status the record never asserts — reading a father's signature on a **standard-form marriage bond** as proof of an underage bride, or treating any record that merely *mentions* a person as proof of the claimed parentage. Rule: **a record that merely mentions a person doesn't prove a parentage it never states** — though that parentage may still be provable by correlating indirect evidence. (Wrong Until Proven Right — Josiah How; Slagle)
- **Negative-evidence errors in BOTH directions.** It conflates a negative *search result* with negative *evidence*. It over-claims — "no death record found, therefore he was still alive" (**absence of a death record is not evidence of life**) — or dismisses a meaningful absence outright. It won't do the ruling-out work that converts absence into evidence: corroborating searches, excluding record loss / incomplete coverage / indexing error, and confirming the record **should** exist. (Negative evidence is the hardest type to apply.) (RLP 409; Writing Up Negative Findings; Seventeen Rules; Lesson 5)
- **Indirect-evidence over-confidence.** It asserts an indirect conclusion as if it were direct, skipping the explicit correlation argument indirect evidence *requires* — chaining multiple clues into a reasoned case. It states the answer instead of building the argument that would support it. (RLP 409; Lesson 5; Wrong Until Proven Right)
- **DNA/documentary siloing (parallel-track fallacy).** Asked to weigh genetic and paper evidence, the model returns **two separate verdicts** ("DNA suggests X; records suggest Y") instead of one proof argument. It uses matches whose parent-child descent to the common ancestor is **undocumented** (so they can't yet function as evidence), ignores that an unphased autosomal match doesn't by itself reveal which **side** (that needs phasing, tested relatives, or documentary framing — routed to ai-for-dna-research), and equates "more matches" with "stronger proof" when match **quality** plus the strength of the **non-DNA** evidence is what resolves the question. (Why Document DNA Links; How Many Matches; Using DNA Source Citations; Coverage)
- **Narrative-gravity / crowd-tree credulity (the meta-failure).** Trained for the tidiest story, the model resolves ambiguity toward **fewer entities** and accepts other users' trees, record hints, or a same-named **"gateway to royalty"** link as fact — the very errors that propagate across online trees and DNA matches (an incorrect merge then *generates* false record hints). It won't adopt "wrong until proven right" or "stop expanding the branch and gather every source first" unless explicitly told. (Jan Joyce, RLP 399; Gateway Ancestors; Wrong Until Proven Right; RLP 391; New Merge Experience)

- **De-novo fabrication (inventing the bridging fact).** Distinct from crowd-tree credulity (which propagates *others'* errors), this is the model **inventing** a record, citation, date, or relationship that never existed to complete the coherent story — the sharpest form of narrative gravity and the most dangerous mode in proof work, because a fabricated source *is* fake evidence. Never accept a record or citation the model produced without confirming it exists in the actual repository. (Verifying any AI-surfaced record/citation is detailed in **ai-for-documentary-records**.)
- **Sycophancy / anchoring on the answer you WANT.** Shown which ancestor you hope to prove — the famous, royal, or brick-wall-breaking one — the model bends the argument toward it, over-weighting confirming evidence and skipping the disconfirming search. Distinct from crowd-tree credulity: it defers to the *operator's* wish, not other users' trees. Frame the question neutrally and demand the disconfirming case ("argue this is the WRONG person") before accepting a link.

## Techniques that work

For a compiled genealogy or local history, inspect its preface/editorial notes for a named
contributor to the relevant family or section. Retain that attribution and locator, then assess
the contributor's possible knowledge of each statement rather than assigning every fact to
the editor. The [family-specific informant example](https://familylocket.com/rlp-185-revisiting-timelines-and-analysis-again/)
motivates this retrieval step; an older relative's contribution does not make the whole book
firsthand, reliable or independent.

Before you rely on any entry in a compiled genealogy, read the work's apparatus: the family and
locality it covers, its numbering scheme, its inclusion criteria, its transcription conventions and
any doubts the compiler admits. Keep hedges such as 'probably' attached and record those statements
as speculation, separate from passages that cite wills, deeds or registers. Weigh each generation by
whether it lived in the author's own time. Do not treat a progenitor named in the title as
identified unless the text actually concludes who he was. List the records each statement cites as
retrieval targets. Search later journal volumes and fraud discussions for reviews, rebuttals or
revisions. A tree birthplace written as 'of [place]' may be a residence entered as a presumed
birthplace. Read a same-name hit within its own family and place. Motivating examples:
[books](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PYK389_glSI),
[surname books](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QBLRdzk88po),
[descendancy book](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lqYLQJICzDY),
[family book](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lb58Xkl8czk),
[journal chains](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iUHlYfkHtb8),
[Horn Papers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=73Cnkvh3tmk),
[compiled histories](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d7IaUGH2hPI),
[Great Migration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AG1TzZ79buQ),
[preface](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cjzdXl19ewA),
['of' places](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZOBFGCHCKy8). The apparatus and any review guide how
you read a claim; neither verifies it. If no response to a work turns up, that neither confirms nor
refutes it.

Treat an accepted lineage-society application, a patriot-database entry or a society's compiled
study as a dated compiled conclusion, not as proof. Record its acceptance or edition date. Identify
which child of the qualifying ancestor your line passes through. Examine the documentation cited for
each generation, which may be hearsay, books or later copies, and assess it against current
standards. Look for the society's later flags, and for a later revision by the same author or body,
then retrieve what the revision relies on. Copied trees that repeat the earlier claim do not outweigh
a revision. Flag tree parents that go beyond a peer-reviewed study as unsupported, for example
parents given for Mayflower passengers whose origins the study leaves unknown. Motivating
discussions: [Geni](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e9DQLOq0x_o),
[Mayflower applications](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UyinOYMxTOI),
[DAR papers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcwF-SvHe5Y),
[ranking review](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zn1CxFAWFq0),
[SAR](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uVlhnNVudY0),
[colonial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AG1TzZ79buQ),
[DAR registrar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CSB6bd-Agcs),
[Mayflower study](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uetstsga33c). An application without a flag is not
thereby verified. These sources give no rate at which approvals are later revised.

When a compiled book gives a relationship or event without an item citation, retain the exact
passage and inspect its bibliography for the missing source. If the compiler cannot clarify
it, use the book's named publisher or local historical society to prepare a bounded inquiry
about that statement and its underlying record. Obtain a record reference and inspect the
available item, distinguishing an image, transcription or later compilation; retain any gap
to the original. Cite the book and recovered item separately before reassessing the claim.
The [Carding Jackson case](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UUcoAC0pd_g) follows this route from an
uncited compiled parentage to a society's baptismal record; it does not establish that every
publisher holds the source or that a retrieved web item is the archival original.

- **Trace a family tradition to its teller.** Record who first wrote or told the story, their generation, and whether they knew the subject or could have witnessed each episode. A first-person account may describe events from the writer's early childhood, or may have been written as fiction. Ask older relatives whether they heard the story and from whom. Flag a detail that recurs in unrelated families or contradicts the documented procedure. Motivating examples: [grandson's account](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qG2YD76a3gk), [novelized memoir](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CoxXz-C754c), [recent legend](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ekd8yqtdMUE), [shared motif](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V6PN8hTtv6c). A contemporaneous record may confirm the event but not the story's extra detail. If elders don't know the story, or the motif turns up elsewhere, the story is weaker but not disproved.
- **Identity dossier / one-row-per-record descriptor table — and sort it BY descriptor.** Stop expanding the branch, then capture **every** descriptor per record in its own column — the Dewitt case used **40+ columns**: dates, places, occupations, spouses, children, neighbors, signatures, and behavioral **"psychographics"** (the case label "Bad Barney"). The core move is the **sort:** a distinct signature (or cluster of descriptors) surfaces separate people, a matching one unifies records a name alone couldn't — corroborated across the dossier, since a signature can be clerk-written or vary. Have the AI *build* the table; you *read the sort*. (Jan Joyce, RLP 399; From Chaos to Clarity)
- **Three-bucket identity triage.** Same-name+same-place / same-name+different-place / different-names — each needs a different separation tactic. **Parental sets** and the **FAN** network (Friends, Associates, Neighbors — Elizabeth Shown Mills' term) discriminate same-named people far better than name-spelling variants; for common names, keep candidates in **separate research projects** so records don't cross-contaminate. (Jan Joyce, RLP 399; One Man, Two Same-Named Wives; RLP 288; Seventeen Rules)
- **Different names are a bucket to test, not a separator.** Records may give one father different given names, or list a man by a place-of-origin byname in one census and by a surname in another. Where peasant surnames were fixed late, one paternal line may appear under several surnames, hiding a shared ancestor and a cousin marriage. Before splitting or merging, compare what does not depend on the name: the wife's name and patronymic, the godparent and surety circle, the recruiting group or settlement, an associate listed beside him in both sources, and paternal lines rebuilt from patronymics and earlier listings. Keep each form verbatim. Motivating examples: [alternating given names](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001505-000-0-0-1775639167=), [byname and surname](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001620-000-0-0-1781866477=), [late surnames](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-25-0-00000220-000-0-0-1573821172=). None explains why the names differ or firmly dates surname fixation. A match remains a reconstruction, a name unique in one listing is unique only there, and a shared surname proves no kinship.
- **Measure the namesake pool before attributing.** Run staged searches in a large index: surname, then given name within a date window, then locality. For a couple, search the surname with both parents' given names across the whole childbearing window and the index's full jurisdiction. Record each count with its collection, scope and search date, and list every candidate or couple returned. Attribute a record only when one candidate fits. Read each attached child's own source, and flag a different parent name or parish for investigation; detach a child only when correlated evidence establishes a different family. In a small community, extract every resident to see how much names and ages normally drift, then link people by recurring spouse and child combinations. Motivating examples: [mixed wives](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_ij26t85WY8), [Pryor](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tii0ig0_FuM), [Talbot](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=19dWr--gyB4), [Findmypast counts](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3axBF8ZZJtI), [Beaumont](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5frrXOx0Y2E), [John Thomas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7fhBUIK9qkA), [Irish index](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AS_MMnXesfg), [spreadsheet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rA6aOQwct2o). Counts measure index entries, not people. A sole candidate is unique only within that index's coverage, a rare name implies no kinship, and uniqueness never proves parentage.
- **Use house numbers as a household descriptor.** Where a parish register gives a bare number beside a place name, check whether it is a house number before matching same-named candidates. Compare the number in the marriage entry with the number in each candidate's baptism: a different house is a reason to doubt that candidate, and the same house is one descriptor among others. Follow the number, and any house or vulgo name, to find other household members. A [house-number reply](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/2977841/) motivates this step. Houses could be renumbered or shared by several families, so a matching number does not prove kinship.
- **Jr./Sr. separates namesakes; it does not prove descent.** In early records, Senior/Junior (or Elder/Younger) may only tell apart an older and a younger man of the same name in one place on that record's date. The two may be uncle and nephew, cousins or unrelated, and a Junior can become Senior when the elder dies. Record each suffix verbatim with its record date as one descriptor among others (for example a tax entry's land section). Before linking two 'Sr.' entries, work out who was alive and eldest at each date, and do not invent a missing counterpart. Keep other period terms verbatim too ('nephew', 'natural child', 'reputed father', ignotus, or 'orphan', which in one court usage meant fatherless) and check them in a dictionary from the record's period. Motivating discussions: [1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8NPz1yDXe-A), [2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vVWewJI3NgM), [3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MDSb0XgbEJg), [4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=llK0cgm8Is0), [5](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7yVDky7MvO0), [6](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xOWgEolt7-g), [7](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y9LHaORmIUE), [8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3vjuk_Pcvk), [9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YsYAGYqYxzg), [10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DiqiKRy-YAs), [11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6HOBIqyzNo4), [12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Miqeo11X4LI). Any kinship needs its own evidence. None of these sources gives firm period or regional bounds or a frequency, and a dictionary gives possible meanings, not this writer's meaning.
- **Eliminate same-name candidates only on positive evidence.** List every candidate with parents and residence. Eliminate a candidate only on sufficient correlated evidence; a matched burial before marriageable age, or an independently established concurrent marriage and children elsewhere, are examples, not an exhaustive list. A candidate with no later record, including no burial found, stays open under the negative-evidence gate. Do not exclude a candidate because of a changed wife, children or state (test remarriage and migration first). Do not exclude one over a later self-reported birthplace either: check for an early-childhood move or institutional removal. When you link a common-named person in a new place, search the former place for a namesake who stayed. If two first marriages cannot be told apart, the widow's later remarriage may name her father; then check the rejected candidate's profile for children it has absorbed. Motivating examples: [German parish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8pltErjQpU), [Lithuanian](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=49kPDjLKLtg), [brick wall](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1pVpwjsp7l4) (also [brick wall, second video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=33NcRbkm-eQ)), [Brady](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8rMB2nCIZz8), [Hamp](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L_69HP2yP3s), [Parkinson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sxEZVAooC58), [Parkinson 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M-gAadppRAY). The candidate who survives is a hypothesis that still needs independent linking evidence, not the proven ancestor.
- **Calibrate a register's stated ages before choosing a candidate by age.** When an age at death is the deciding clue among same-name baptism candidates, take other burial entries by the same recorder whose baptisms you can find. Compare stated with actual ages, and record the error pattern for that register and period instead of applying a generic tolerance. A [German register discussion](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/215886/) motivates this step. The error sizes it reports are individual experiences, not norms. An accurate recorder narrows the window, but it does not identify the person, and the other candidates stay open under the elimination rule above.
- **Test a same-named man's marriage series for consistency.** Tabulate every marriage attributed to him with the marital status the register gives (bachelor, widower), his stated age, the witnesses, and the death of each previous wife. A bachelor status after an earlier marriage, or ages that do not progress, flags a possible second man; a widower status needs a matching death of the previous wife, which may be entered under her married surname. Take parents and origin from whichever entry states them (in the example, only the first marriage gave the parents). The [marriage-series comparison](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/3014148/) leaves one marriage unassigned: an inconsistency is a conflict to investigate, not a separator.
- **When two people carry one birth identity, test each claimant against the records.** If marriage entries for two different women give the same name, birth date, parents and birthplace, first list every child of that surname in the origin parish's baptisms for the surrounding years, with parents, so twins or a same-named relative are tested rather than assumed. Then, for each claimant, take from her children's baptisms the mother's stated age and birth order, any sponsors from her natal family, and names echoing her natal siblings. A [duplicated-identity discussion](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/3014856/) shows these checks. There the sponsor test was negative for both families and the age gap was small, so neither woman was identified. Weak or absent signals leave both claimants open.
- **Reality-check filters on any merged or proposed-merge timeline.** A genuine biological/chronological impossibility (childbearing window, post-mortem fatherhood) **blocks the merge**; softer anomalies (a duplicate census enumeration, spouses/children that appear then vanish) are **conflicts to investigate** — resolve them, don't merge over them, but don't treat them as automatic separators either.** On a collaborative tree, read the **change log bottom-up** to recover the profile's *original intended identity* before untangling. (Kathryn Grant, Same Name Trap & Recognizing Problems; New Merge Experience)
- **Screen a compiled child list for two merged couples.** Look for these signs: an unexplained multi-year gap or an over-long childbearing span; children overlapping under two different spouses; two differently named children born in one year; more attached children than a census child count reports (confirm the field's definition first); grandparents that differ. To split the families, use marriages near each first birth, occupations in the baptism entries, census households, and the exact form of each parent's name that the clerk wrote (Johann vs Johannes). Test each child separately against the father's will (allowing for children who died before it), the siblings' baptism places and the birth spacing. Detach a child only when several independent signals agree, and write the reasons down. Motivating examples: [Makins](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p-ImaWJYGMA), [same-name class](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D-PJUgIDY-M), [later version](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dlFJ4BKQyOg), [merge review](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HMoPsznHP1g), [Sutton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jfWVACjBbbM), [20 children](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GbTECnZXn88). Each sign is a conflict to investigate, not a separator: gaps, omissions and census answers can have innocent explanations. Children you cannot assign stay uncertain.
- **Check a stated parent's household roster.** When a record names someone as the child of a named father, test the claim against an enumeration of that father's household that lists daughters as well as sons. A child of the expected age who is missing, together with other gaps (a mother outside the childbearing window, sponsors or relatives who never appear in that family), suggests a same-named father. The [revision-list check](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001487-000-0-0-1776019117=) shows this combination. Absence alone counts only under the negative-evidence gate: the child may have lived elsewhere or been missed.
- **Geographic reality check.** Map every event place attached to one identity or family, and trace each place to the record that supplied it: a place taken from a child's record does not put the parent there. Confirm whether each date is a birth or a christening. Then ask whether the mother could plausibly have been present at each birth, given the distance and travel in that period, and flag baptisms that alternate between distant communities. Use a dated boundary source or gazetteer to check whether each place existed then, whether one place lay inside another, whether a county label changed, and how many places shared the name. An event placed somewhere that did not yet exist, or a stated age that conflicts with when the birthplace existed, is unlocated or in conflict; do not quietly modernise it. Motivating examples: [Sanderson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gssWfSl8JwY), [Morgan](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oNYa_u3v_c8), [Sutton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tii0ig0_FuM), [non-event](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9YdOl_wgvb4), [mother present](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aSwzD902OUE), [English](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yLKR7PCqFrc) and [Irish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YWpZYZVqHfw) jurisdictions, [Stepney](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NCIZoX2VV04), [Kent](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=19dWr--gyB4), [hint cluster](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-op5VOfN0M), [places first](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cxdv-_iOfH4), [distance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHpQnoG9PS0), [railroad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eG5ZcWAjiLg), [settlement dates](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FDmZ8YGxldo), [alternating counties](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uzd9VGvE1yc), [Tipton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3rpwjGBRnG0), [same-named towns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXde-Z4rocM). A spread, an anachronism or a clash is a conflict to investigate, not a separator. A tight cluster proves nothing, and the presenters' mileages and dates are not rules.
- **Same-named siblings and apparent twins need a test, not a merge or a split.** Two baptisms with one given name to the same parents years apart may be a child who died and a later namesake. Read both originals, and search that parish's burials for the elder child before the later christening, with an age that fits. Two entries close together may be a provisional baptism, noted in the margin, followed by a later one, not two children. One baptism date can cover children born on different dates. An unexpected index entry sharing the subject's birth date, place and mother's maiden name may have been copied from a neighbouring entry: get the certificate or a no-record statement. Motivating examples: [British Isles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hEZSzJRVXYE), [Scottish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tlfJga5MZ_M), [necessity baptism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_YP7JKXjthk), [Blackburn](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e3wVkWt581Y), [Welsh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BAoVsb8RzSQ), [shared baptism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-4MobTXiuw), [phantom entry](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D3EnR_-ZUhA). Neither a reused name nor a baptism 'of necessity' proves a death. A missing burial counts only under the negative-evidence gate, and marginal practice varied by parish.
- **Test a one-off name in a series before creating a person.** When one entry gives a known man a different given name, patronymic or spouse, lay his household out across consecutive confession lists, revision lists and parish entries, comparing names and ages year by year. A name that breaks an otherwise continuous series, such as a son listed under a brother's name while that brother heads his own household, is a probable scribal substitution: annotate it beside the verbatim entry rather than correcting it. Before calling it an error, search the nearest revision list for a second same-named man of that generation. Motivating examples: [confession-list series](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001893-000-0-0-1781670413=), [conflicting baptism entry](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00000862-000-0-0-1778497763=). Your emendation is an inference; a lone entry stays a conflict until the series or namesake search settles it.
- **Conflict resolution by reliability, not by vote.** Judge each **statement** by its informant's basis of knowledge (a death certificate's informant usually knew the death firsthand but the birth secondhand — classify the statement, not the record), weight by informant proximity, count only **independent** information streams (copies aren't corroboration), correct for document conventions (a standard-form bond signed by a parent doesn't prove minority), and **explain the conflict's cause before choosing a value.** (Power of Proof; Ann Tanner; Slagle)
- **Test independence before counting agreement.** Open any cited website or database page before counting it. Shared distinctive errors, shared placeholder entries, content matching the Family Tree, or a common descendancy book all mark one copied lineage: record it as one derivative claim and verify it against the original records. Agreement between Ancestral File or Pedigree Resource File and a Tree profile is not corroboration; per the presenter's account, their data were loaded into the Tree. Records that all agree on a father but were each supplied by the subject are one stream as well. Consider a stepparent, and test that by searching for the mother's remarriage and the siblings' or church records. Motivating examples: [shared mistakes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-AqGC6qa2rU), [Bryant book](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9Q4qYeK5DTg), [MyHeritage citation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2yVk3RoVGcA), [smart-match artefacts](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-op5VOfN0M), [file history](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7KAPMSXHu9M), [stepfather case](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dY9EV5R_6ZA). Shared artefacts suggest copying, but they do not identify the original or the direction of copying. Trees without shared errors are not thereby independent.
- **Negative-evidence gate.** A negative finding carries weight only when the source system and circumstances create a **justified expectation** that the missing information would appear — after ruling out record loss, incomplete coverage, and indexing error, and confirming the record **should** exist. Multiple failed searches alone never convert it (that is just vote-counting). **Log every search's date** — online coverage changes over time. (RLP 409; Writing Up Negative Findings)
- **An archive's 'no records' reply is someone else's search result.** Log it with its date, the issuing body and the years, record types and churches it covered. Separate parishes searched without result from any whose registers the reply says never reached the archive, and work out which parish actually served the village. Then check the fund inventory and any filmed copies yourself, and ask where a second copy of the register was kept: a volume may combine births and deaths over several years, or survive for only one event type in a year. Motivating examples: [negative certificate](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=20&t=5084), [unreceived parish books](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00000636-000-0-0-1779081499=). Until coverage is verified, the reply does not meet the negative-evidence gate. 'Not received' does not show the books were destroyed, and a volume the inventory lists does not prove the entry you need survives.
- **A missing marriage record proves neither no marriage nor a marriage.** Before inferring illegitimacy or a legal marriage, establish from a dated legal authority whether the jurisdiction recognised informal marriage at that place and time. Gather holding-out evidence: census marital status, children, land or probate records naming the wife, and any court ruling. When children and long co-residence have no marriage record, keep 'never legally married' as a live alternative. Do not read the woman's surname as either her married or her maiden name, because it may come from an earlier marriage. Test the alternatives with Bible entries, relatives' wills, estate and court records, and presentments (read the outcome too, since an indictment is only an allegation). Motivating examples: [common-law talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8IyZ2AdcCCo), [Talbott](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CgcnOlNO9bA). A census 'married' does not prove a legal marriage, the gate above still applies to the absent entry, and present-day recognition is not historical law.
- **Indirect-evidence discipline.** Never let "a record mentions X" stand in for "a record proves the relationship." An indirect conclusion must be **written as an explicit correlation argument** chaining the clues, not asserted. (Wrong Until Proven Right; RLP 409; Lesson 5)
- **A stated status is a statement, not an event.** A census 'widowed' neither proves nor dates a spouse's death: divorce, desertion or separation can lie behind it. Likewise, 'married' does not show that an absent husband was alive. Before recording a death, look for the spouse alive or remarried later, and for death, burial or probate evidence. Where family lore says a spouse vanished, search court calendars and newspaper notices for a divorce, linking them by case number. A licence's 'single and unmarried' gives the status on that date, not 'never married'. A guardianship does not show that both parents were dead (the guardian may be the mother). An orphanage listing does not show orphanhood or adoption: trace the children back to earlier censuses and follow each parent forward. Motivating examples: [time grid](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8kc1qA0IKBo), [Wellborne](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X4MD-Bitis8), [grass widow](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jeshnSv9xQ0), [1900 case](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YPKGBX2zZOs), [Holtz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZbTw-Ba4tBs), [1880](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CjjD9mI_dY8), [divorce records](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9XPYpWcaU1o), [marriage affidavit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7iLTGhthWKI), [probate](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PzZ4fwSbjq8), [orphanage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z9IHEinKGzs). These are single cases. They set no frequency, they do not make every widow suspect, and guardianship rules depend on jurisdiction and period.
- **A named father or legitimacy label is a registration decision, not a finding of paternity.** A baptism's father may be the mother's legal husband even when he had been absent for years (in the army, exiled, whereabouts unknown). Some registers marked a widow's child illegitimate with a note citing the husband's death entry by date and number: follow that reference and record the note as the clerk's reasoning. A participant reports that soldiers' wives' children were also often marked illegitimate while the husband was alive. List the mother's entries across neighbouring parishes in date order, with her exact status label, each child's legitimacy and the godparents. Then establish the husband's service, return and death, and search marriages for candidate fathers. Differing labels may reflect different priests. On an illegitimate entry, a sponsor described as the father's brother is an indirect clue to correlate. Motivating discussions: [absent husband](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001736-000-0-0-1775021161=), [legitimacy notes](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000221-000-0-0-1737654759=). These are participants' accounts without a cited legal rule. Practice varied by priest and parish, and no label, named father or sponsor establishes biological paternity.
- **Alive-or-dead wording on a later entry is a dated statement too.** A 'deceased' qualifier on a bride's or groom's father says he died before that date, so search the death registers before it. Later entries that still give his rank or occupation without the qualifier do not prove he was alive, and the qualifier does not automatically outweigh them. Likewise, a periodically compiled household list may carry a person forward after a separate dated death record. Record the list's reference, date and the person's position (head or relative of the head), and ask whether that entry was updated or copied forward. First establish that the entries describe one person, and keep the conflict open until a death record or other evidence resolves it. Motivating examples: [deceased qualifier](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001487-000-0-0-1776019117=), [family list](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=4&t=4667). In the first, no death entry was found and a register year is missing; the second is a forum heuristic, not a rule for any list series.
- **Date a household list from its contents, not its label.** A family list's catalogue or file year may not be the year its entries were written. Before reading a child's absence as death, marriage or omission, take the members whose birth dates are known from other records and add their stated ages to their birth years. Note which compilation window the ages imply. If that window falls before the missing child's birth, the absence says nothing. A [family-list dating example](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=4&t=4667) motivates this step. The result depends on the stated ages and the independent birth dates, and it gives a window, not a date. Entries added at different times may imply different windows.
- **Separation wording is not a divorce.** If a personal or service file says a man 'parted from' his wife, it records that they lived apart. Before recording a divorce, find a dissolution note beside the marriage entry, a later marriage entry saying the first marriage was dissolved, or the consistory's divorce file. If a note gives a ground such as the wife's fault, record it as the court's stated finding, not as a fact about anyone's conduct. The [divorce discussion](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000224-000-0-0-1716470420=) reports a case file where a spouse would agree to divorce only on a named ground. That is one file and sets no rate. A missing note in one copy of the register does not show there was no divorce.
- **Read a divorce decree's remarriage terms.** When a dissolution note survives, record what it allows each spouse: remarriage at once, remarriage only after a church penance (one transcribed note sets seven years for the guilty wife), or a lifelong bar. Keep the decree's reference and date, and use its terms to judge when, or whether, a later marriage entry you are attributing to that person could have happened. The [divorce-note examples](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000224-000-0-0-1716470420=) motivate this step. They do not show how the restrictions were enforced or evaded, so a marriage that seems to break them is a conflict to investigate, not proof of a different person.
- **Heir lists are not child lists.** A will can leave out children already provided for. An heirs' deed lists the participants and may miss a child who died earlier without heirs. 'Heir' does not mean child. In an intestate estate, record each share's stated chain (heir, deceased intermediate parent, relation to the decedent) as its own observation. Several heirs splitting one share is a lead to a deceased child's descendants. A distribution to siblings raises a question about surviving entitled descendants at that date; resolve entitlement under the descent law in force for that place and date before drawing a survival conclusion, and do not read it as proof that the decedent was childless. Before using relatives from a will or probate index, confirm each name in the testator's own text, because an index can carry names over from the adjacent entry. Motivating examples: [McClure](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q9dHUILop_A), [intestacy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CjjD9mI_dY8), [Jerusha Abbott](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=880wgUzrmhQ), [will index](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z0wwMYchf2I). None of these sources states that law. A fractional share is a lead, not proof of descent.
- **Use a death entry's family summary as a control, within its limits.** Some registers list a deceased man's successive marriages or count his children. Before searching for a spouse claimed only by an unsourced tree, compare her with the marriages that entry lists; her absence is a reason to doubt the claim, not proof that no such marriage occurred. A child count may cover only the children living at the death, so search baptisms after each marriage for children who died earlier. Motivating examples: [marriage list](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/3013930/), [surviving children](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/2927558/). Check the entry's own wording; neither thread shows how common either practice was.
- **An age threshold needs the law of that place and date, and even then it is not a hard bound.** Before using an age of majority or a minimum sponsor or marriage age to bound a birth, read a guardianship or consent, or call someone a minor, identify and cite the legal system in force for that territory and date. Do not assume 21 or 18. A [majority compilation](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/134872/) shows one state using different ages by district and changing them on different dates. Keep majority, marriage with consent, and capacity to dispose of land or take over a holding as separate thresholds; a marriage does not prove majority, and an age to inherit or dispose of land may differ from majority. A [sponsor-age discussion](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000141-000-0-0-1701453218=) quotes rules setting minimum sponsor ages yet reports much younger sponsors, so do not use a statutory sponsor age as a hard bound on a sponsor's birth. Verify each quoted figure against the statute; these observations set no frequency.
- **DNA + paper as one proof.** Document each test-taker's parent-child chain to the common ancestor **before** using the match as evidence; require the documented descent path and the genetic sharing to **converge on the same hypothesis** before the match counts as proof. (Match selection, clustering, coverage across independent descendant lines, and triangulation belong to **unknown-parentage-wato** — route them there, don't re-derive them here.) (Why Document DNA Links; How Many Matches; Coverage; Using DNA Source Citations; RLP 409)
- **Test a famous or gateway link from the documented end.** First find where the documentation starts. If sources appear only from the noble or gateway generation upward, the connection is unproven: verify each generation from the researcher upward. Watch for same-name ocean crossings, overlapping marriages and geographic leaps, and record a published pedigree's provenance. Check that the famous person left surviving children, and join a line only at a person a documented descendancy actually names. Convert a claimed cousin degree into the shared ancestors it implies and use them as a search guide; rule out slots by dates, then recompute the degree from the joined tree. When trees attach an ancestor to a progenitor because of a rare given name, list the progenitor's same-named descendants and compare their children with men documented locally. Motivating examples: [gateway](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mbDqe9rXWx4), [famous ancestor](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qG2YD76a3gk), [Truman](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gdyo3ORfPk4), [Tecumseh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jjknGUtMs7s), [rare name](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v3x23zLGi04). A compiled descendancy is a source to evaluate. A claim that has not been excluded is still unproved, and a shared rare name proves no link.

## Worked mini-examples (the reasoning logic)

- **Two-way descriptor sort splits and unifies at once (Dewitt).** Sorting a 40+-column spreadsheet of "David Dewitt" *and* "Henry Dewitt" records simultaneously **split** two men named David (two distinct signatures on an 1833 petition) **and unified** "Maryland Henry" with "Ohio Henry" — Maryland Henry vanished from Maryland tax records in 1815–1816, Ohio Henry bought Holmes County land on 26 May 1815 — as one man, David's father. The name carried zero discriminating power; the descriptor pattern carried all of it. (From Chaos to Clarity)
- **Test overlapping tax locations against separate family timelines (Scandinavian).** Same-name tax entries in different parishes raised a **two-family hypothesis**, not an automatic identity split. Check what each entry actually records before treating a taxable interest as residence or physical presence. In the episode's account, one father's death in 1730 was compared with the other father's child born in 1732 and separate death in 1771. Correlate the identities and event dates behind that chronology; tax geography alone does not settle it. The later choice between their same-named sons remained a separate indirect argument. (RLP 286)
- **Reconcile, don't average (Slagle birth date).** Census enumerations implying birth years scattered across **1830–1835** (1850 → 1835; 1860/1880/1900 → 1834), plus a marriage bond, death certificate, and headstone (**4 May 1834**), were reconciled to **4 May 1834** by weighting informant proximity and geographic evidence — and the father's signature on the **$200 standard-form marriage bond was correctly *not* read as proof the bride was a minor.** Pre-1940 census ages carry an unknown informant; **1940 is the first U.S. census to mark who reported.** (When Birthdates Don't Agree)
- **Resolve a same-name fork by tracing each candidate *forward* (457-person tree).** An entire 457-person tree rested on **one unverified parent-child link**; two same-name census candidates were eliminated by following each one's *later* residences, marriages, and families until only one stayed consistent. One bad link invalidated everything above it. (Genealogical Proof Standard | Ancestry)
- **Absence into evidence (Henderson Weatherford / Eliza Poling).** Repeated failed searches became negative *evidence* only after corroboration and ruling out record loss / coverage / indexing; a **missing 1888 death registration** counted because the person was known to be in the area and should have been recorded. (RLP 409; Writing Up Negative Findings)
- **Undocumented descent blocks the DNA argument.** Two starting test-takers lacked documented lines of descent, so their matches couldn't serve as strong evidence until the **paper descent was built** — proof came from correlating the DNA cluster *with* a documented line to the common ancestor, not from the match list alone. (Why Document DNA Links; RLP 409)
- **"Wrong until proven right" (Josiah How).** Census, military, and pension records *about* Josiah didn't prove the family story until each was checked for belonging to the correct man **and** actually proving kinship — which overturned the story (he survived the Civil War). (Wrong Until Proven Right)
- **Immigrant identity across two countries (Ane Jensen).** Connecting the right person in the US with the right person in the home country was itself an indirect-evidence correlation problem — resolved by correlating census and newspaper conflicts, *not* by assuming the matching name was the match. (How to Know if You've Met the GPS)

## Use a naming pattern to choose a search

For competing families, compare their documented children in birth order with independently
known relatives. Attribute any proposed naming custom to its locality and period; record
missing order, repeated names and exceptions. Use a plausible expected name to choose the
next parent-record search, then compare dates, places and the other families. An apparent
pattern supplies neither parentage nor a death, religious or ethnic identity.

This worksheet applies the [2024 UK naming-pattern demonstration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0G_xOogKkTI)
as a search aid within the conditional-premise procedure below, without imposing a universal
British naming order.

Verify a name element before using it as a search term or identifier. A middle name, initial or
suffix found only on a tree profile or in an inherited compilation may be a compiler's label. Check
whether records or signatures from the person's lifetime show it, and note where it first appears.
In the illustrated U.S. Army records, 'NMN' is read as 'no middle name'; confirm the abbreviation against the record series. Do not turn an unverified middle
name into the mother's maiden surname. Where a second given name or a surname-like given name
appears, test it both as a namesake (relative, friend or minister) and as a maiden surname in the
mother's or grandmother's generation. Motivating examples:
[Wales](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AExYnNLU_LU),
[Scotland](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4cVRQxlR_jY),
[maiden names](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CAuwye17wbQ),
[Tefft](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W-FMXSswYAY),
[Morgan](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xOWgEolt7-g),
[entry guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y4vkJheOo80). Not finding a name in the records you
inspected does not prove it was never used. A custom of passing on maternal surnames must be shown
from the records, and any such clue is a search lead, not parentage.

## Compare the creation stages within a marriage record

When a loose license/return and a book entry survive, compare the license, ceremony return
and filing/recording endorsements separately. Retain both locators and each component's
creator or handwriting, action date and copying relationship. Issue, ceremony and recording
dates answer different questions.

The [RLP 371 discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wxj2UzRtJ98) qualifies the author's
earlier [article](https://familylocket.com/comparing-marriage-records-original-licence-and-return-vs-marriage-book-entry/):
in the demonstrated 1888 Texas case, a book can combine a contemporaneous license entry
with a deputy's copied return. Examine the volume and surrounding entries to establish
the actual process; give components their own source classifications. Copied wording is
dependent evidence. A blank return leaves both an unperformed ceremony and a missing
returned/recorded certificate open. The article and episode belong to one authorial chain;
establish another jurisdiction's process from its records.

Before treating a bond witness as a family associate, compare the name/hand and stated role
on available contemporaneous bonds or court records. Retain actual evidence of routine clerk
or other official service; repetition alone proves neither office nor kinship. Keep witness,
bondsman and consenting-person roles distinct. The [marriage-bond example](https://familylocket.com/back-to-the-basics-with-marriage-records-part-1-marriage-bonds/)
adds this comparison to the existing form-convention and signature controls.

If an actual index exposes a bondsman/surety field, search the identified actor in that
role and carry each couple, date, jurisdiction and index locator to the original bond.
Inspect separately available consent and return material; an index's omitted associate
or consent is not absence from the packet. The [bondsmen example](https://familylocket.com/rlp-233-marriage-bonds-and-bondsmen/)
motivates this reverse lookup without promising a current field, image route or legal rule.

A diocese label on a marriage-licence record may not give the place of marriage. Record the licence, its issuing authority and the ceremony place as separate items. Find the marriage entry itself before placing the couple in a parish or county. In a [licence misreading](https://genarchivist.net/showthread.php?tid=837), a poster read a 'Dioceses of Canterbury' label as the marriage place for couples whose licensed marriages were in fact in Middlesex. The thread does not say what the label denotes or how the Middlesex place was found, which licensing series were involved, or what the licence copies contain. A licence alone does not prove that the ceremony took place.

A banns entry records an intention to marry, not a marriage, and an index may record banns readings
as marriage dates. Several dates a few weeks apart for one couple are a sign of this. Inspect the
full entry and the facing page for a note of solemnization or objection, and search for the marriage
entry itself. Banns with no recorded outcome leave the marriage open. In the other direction, a
second ceremony or registration for the same couple is a separate event, not the start of the union
or a second couple. Examples include a ceremony repeated for an administrative scheme, a later
religious sealing, or a civil registration made years after the children were born. Keep each entry
with its locator and role, and do not redate the children's legitimacy from the later event.
Motivating examples: [banns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HX-vIeT3nuo),
[indexed banns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZOBFGCHCKy8),
[subsidy remarriage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8IyZ2AdcCCo),
[sealing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vwx-RUk8sbU),
[late civil registration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uks4xjQvig8). These sources establish no
indexing practice, period or legal requirement; take those from dated local authority.

## Follow record versions and test disputed dates

For a struck-out index or later-filed entry, retain both references and distinguish the
event, registration and change dates. Follow supplied amendment/court references to the
available original and amended records. Read a later marginal identity or married-name
note separately from the original statement, retaining its date/author when known; compare
all live namesake candidates. A later filing alone establishes neither adoption nor biological
parentage. These are retrieval checks illustrated by the
[Edna Estes](https://dna-explained.com/2022/06/12/edna-estes-miller-1920-1990-sister-once-found-twice-lost-52-ancestors-361/)
and [Gockeler](https://dna-explained.com/2022/08/18/wolfgangius-gockeler-his-wife-barbara-born-c-1585-i-baptize-thee-52-ancestors-370-371/)
cases; their original records remain to be inspected in any new investigation.

An issued certificate, extract, archive statement or compiled ancestry form is derived from a register entry. A dash or blank on it (religion, nationality, birthplace, the mother's surname, a whole paternal side) means 'not stated' there (for a blank paternal side, at most that the father was not known), not 'none' or a hidden origin. Find where the original register is held and request the entry's full text, ideally an image, including marginal notes, because issued statements may abbreviate it. Treat an uncertified handwritten addition, especially in a field that year's form lacked, as an unattributed later addition. Record differing surnames as a conflict. Look beside the act for dated notes of a name change, conversion, court decision or later acknowledgment of paternity; clerks could also write variant forms without an official change. Motivating discussions: [register extract](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=95&t=5107), [certificate addition](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=20&t=5084), [act annotations](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=4&t=4667), [blank paternal side](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/7419/). None establishes period rules on forms, amendments or registering fathers, or what an omitted field contained.

A bilingual seal may reflect the issuing office rather than its holder. On a Soviet-era certificate, a Yiddish stamp next to Slavic text is not a rabbinate seal and does not show the person's Jewish identity: offices in Jewish national districts and village soviets could use bilingual seals. First identify the issuing district. Then look for nationality fields, such as the parents' nationality, which one participant says was usually given on 1937 civil records, or the person's later identity documents. A [bilingual-seal discussion](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=95&t=5107) motivates this step. It shows no stamp image, and one participant's remark on 1937 nationality fields is a generalisation, not a rule for any form.

When an entry corrects itself, transcribe both readings. Record the informant the note blames for the struck reading and the corrector's attestation, and treat each reading as a separate statement. A correction that matches an earlier register entry may mean the clergy aligned it with that entry (one participant's reading of this case); it does not independently confirm the reading. The struck reading may preserve the name the family actually used, for example a stepfather's patronymic, which later civil documents may repeat. Record notes of certificates issued later from the entry as dated later uses of it. A [metrical-book correction example](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001736-000-0-0-1775021161=) motivates this step. The thread quotes transcriptions only, and neither reading establishes biological parentage.

When the image of a birth entry is available, look for papers inserted between the leaves and for later margin notes that an index or transcription may not carry. A receipt for a birth certificate issued for an adoption, naming the recipient, issue date and number, is its own dated document. Record it with its locator, and use the named recipient to search his parish for the child under his surname or a new patronymic. A later court legitimization note names a legitimizing husband. Neither the receipt nor the note proves that an adoption was completed or who the biological father was. An [adoption-records discussion](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000146-000-0-0-1759035209=) shows both forms; its link to a later family is only a suggestion.

If a disputed year comes with a month/day and liturgical observance, preserve the literal
forms and test candidate years against the documented calendar/tradition. Compare independent
chronology, considering a later-added signature only where the document supports it.
Report compatibility or an unresolved calendar basis; compatibility does not prove the year.
The [calendar-and-signatory example](https://www.roots-branches.com/examine-assumptions-use-local-expertise/)
supplies this extra constraint, not a verified date for the present case.

Old Style dates can also explain a gap of days. If a pre-1918 date from the former Russian Empire differs from a later Soviet-era document by about 13 days, or by nearly a year across a December–January boundary, test for a conversion that shifted the day without changing the year. If a remembered or gravestone birthday differs by days from the metrical birth date, test whether it is the name-day of the child's saint, converted to New Style. Keep each literal date with its calendar and role, and look in the register for dual dates or later issue notes that add 13 days. Motivating discussions: [calendar conversion](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=75&t=7576), [name-day](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00000862-000-0-0-1778497763=). They give no rule for how any office converted dates and show no general practice. A fitting explanation does not prove which date is correct.

Do not add 13 days to every Old Style date. The gap between the Julian and Gregorian calendars was 10 days in the seventeenth century, 11 in the eighteenth, 12 in the nineteenth and 13 from 1900. Each increase falls around the end of February in 1700, 1800 and 1900. Use the offset for the date's own century, check any date near those boundaries with a calendar converter, and keep the literal Old Style date beside the conversion. A [calendar-offset table](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000172-000-0-0-1772397970=), credited to a secondary article, motivates this step. It does not say which calendar its boundary dates use, and it does not cover registers already kept in New Style.

A Hebrew date one day later than a converter's value for the civil date may record an evening event, since the Hebrew day begins at the previous evening; do not move the entry to another year just because the converter matches both dates exactly in that other year, and check the book's title year and neighbouring entries first. The [dual-date discussion](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=4&t=4667) motivates this; it explains only a one-day offset in that direction, not an earlier Hebrew date or a larger gap.

A record made long after the event is a late claim. For a delayed birth certificate or affidavit,
record the application or issue date separately from the birth date it asserts. Note each informant
or affiant with their stated relationship and years known, research them as associates, and ask what
each could actually have known. A precise day may have been fixed from a remembered feast or season,
so check how the date was established before weighing it, and compare it with earlier records such
as sibling order across censuses. For a church certificate issued years after the event, check
whether it identifies an underlying register and classify its information from that evidence. A different given name on it may be a copyist's rendering of an
original-language form; test that against the register entry. Motivating examples:
[delayed affidavit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jeshnSv9xQ0),
[delayed certificate](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TW-sRiN0mYM)
(also [2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fwjzc6KsI4o)),
[late baptismal certificate](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7vUOB1x6QR0). These are single cases.
They show neither how common the practice was nor what form was used, and the affiants' stated
relationships still need independent proof.

A document reissued after a loss is also a late claim. If the family says papers were lost and replaced, for example during wartime evacuation, treat the birth year on the replacement, and on later records copied from it, as possibly changed. When contact is authorized, ask the archive to search the original birth register over several earlier years, not only the stated one. Also look on surviving certificates for altered numerals, such as an extended Roman month numeral. A [reissued-document discussion](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=64&t=561) motivates this step. The family story is unconfirmed and no earlier entry had been found, so a wider search window does not show which year is correct.

When an index names only each entry's principals, a person's later roles as sponsor or
witness stay hidden. To bound a life window, inspect documented relatives' baptisms backward
from the latest relevant date and retain the latest entry in which the person actually acts
as sponsor or witness. A mere mention in someone else's record, such as a spouse named in a
burial entry without participation or living-status wording, does not by itself establish
survival. The [Port Royal godmother example](https://dna-explained.com/2024/06/16/francoise-dugas-1679-after-1751-goodbye-port-royal-52-ancestors-426/)
motivates this search; a latest appearance is not a death date.

Read a baptism's whole sponsor section, including any note in the entry, before you use a sponsorship. A named sponsor, such as a high-ranking one, may have accepted without attending or been represented by a stand-in. Record each sponsor's role as written: named, absent, represented by someone, present. Only then use the entry to place someone in the parish, to bound a life window as above, or as evidence of a close tie to the family. A [baptism with absent royal sponsors](https://forum.ahnenforschung.net/forum/thread/928/) motivates this step. It is one unusual entry. Most entries carry no such note, and a missing note does not show that a sponsor was present.

## Resolve a relationship label across successive unions

Retain an in-law term verbatim with its reference person and record date. Compare earlier
and later household entries and independently supported unions before calling the term a
scribal error or reconciling different fathers through a double name. Show each candidate
path through former/current spouses and subsequent marriages; one person can hold two
affinal roles. Preserve supported paths and unresolved alternatives. The [revision-list discussion](https://forum.j-roots.info/viewtopic.php?f=4&t=13676)
motivates this dated comparison without establishing its narrated family solution or a
universal kinship dictionary.

Surnames shift across unions too. A bride's surname on a marriage entry is not her maiden name
unless the record says so: a widow may marry under an earlier married surname without the record
mentioning a previous marriage. Check for children carrying that surname, for an earlier marriage,
for the mother's maiden name in the children's birth registrations, and for a census taken before
the marriage. Before attaching household members as the head's children, check the relationship
column, their ages and any earlier marriage of the wife. A census child with no birth registration
under the household surname may be registered under the mother's maiden or earlier married surname.
Children of another surname may be a remarried mother's: test this with their later answers about
her birthplace and with land or probate records she made under both names. Motivating examples:
[1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DG2eJWmekcY), [2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DncJDtUmqeE),
[3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EwBoSeXHnX8), [4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dtl6pnXMiEY),
[Caroline](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gsbQIuilOYk)
(also [Caroline, second video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LtVWWY1u5OI) and
[Caroline, third video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gp9OE5-gtG4)),
[Gertrude](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9g14sFJ7ZjY),
[Patton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=svND4WwnX54),
[Gunton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QOxgU5AZjEo),
[Dove](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pNF5B1jZ8gM). Agreement supports a surname but does not prove
it. A matching birthplace or a registration under the maiden name identifies no father.

When a woman's marriage entry is lost, search the parish baptisms for her as a sponsor. First look for her under her married name at christenings in a candidate family, and note whether those parents share her patronymic. Then look for an unmarried woman with the same given name and patronymic under that family's surname before the estimated marriage. Keep both sets of entries with their locators as indirect evidence. The [sponsor-entry example](https://forum.molgen.org/index.php/topic,1298.0.html) motivates this search. The entries state no kinship, and an unmarried namesake is not proven to be the later married woman, so the maiden surname still needs a written correlation argument.

When a mother is recorded only by given name and patronymic, look at the godparents at her children's baptisms and the sureties at their marriages for someone who shares her patronymic. Treat that person as a lead to a possible natal family, not as a brother. Then reconstruct the candidate family from a compiled family table of the registers: look for one father with both a son of the sponsor's name and a daughter of the mother's name, at compatible ages. Seek her own baptism or marriage entry to confirm the link; neither thread cited below does. Motivating examples: a [godparent-patronymic test](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000238-000-0-0-1721744472=) and a [surety-patronymic lead](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-2-0-00001505-000-0-0-1775639167=). Sponsors need not be kin and patronymics are common, so a fit is a reconstruction until her own record states it.

If one person has a different patronymic or surname in revision lists than in metrical books, keep both forms. Test upbringing in another household in both directions between the two candidate families, A and B: a child born to B raised in A, or a child born to A, raised by B, who later took back the birth name. Check three things. Does the candidate family's birth spacing leave room in the years whose registers are missing? Do the two households stand godparent for each other before and after the gap? What do dated confession lists say about when each wife died? Record which lists you could not consult. Motivating sources: a [patronymic-mismatch case study](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000238-000-0-0-1721744472=) and a [rough-notes remark](https://rodgersforum.borda.ru/?1-0-0-00000230-000-0-0-1775396698=). The case's author left it a working hypothesis; a good fit proves neither direction nor identity.

## Discover same-name rivals before accepting a census hint

Start from the candidate entry and independently supported name, approximate birth interval
and locality. Run a separate search in that census year and collection, using only supported
constraints; include the relevant county and adjoining counties when available. A surname-only
pass can expose variants where the result set remains reviewable. Record the actual query,
coverage, result count and candidate record IDs. On Ancestry, check whether active filters
hide saved or previously viewed results using the [search-recovery recipe](ancestry-record-search-recovery.md)
before treating the displayed list as the available candidates. (Sources 161–163)

Carry the recovered rivals into the descriptor dossier above, with their images and identifying
details. Keep initial-only and otherwise ambiguous entries open until checked: the demonstrated
three-result search still included two unexamined E.-initial alternatives. A low result count
does not establish uniqueness, and the bounded survey is not an exhaustive search of all people.
Stop the pass at its stated collection, dates and places; record missing coverage and the next
discriminating record instead of attaching the preferred entry by elimination. (Sources 161–163)

## Using working hypotheses without inheriting their assumptions

**A provisional link can choose the next search; it cannot supply the answer to that search.** The specific failure is a premise disappearing between steps: the researcher assumes Candidate A is the father, finds A's parents, and the assistant reports those people as the subject's grandparents. The new record can establish A's family while leaving A's relationship to the subject unresolved. Keep those two claims separate. This procedure organizes the hypothesis-testing practices in *Using Hypotheses*, *RLP 187* and Mills's *QuickLesson 16*; the premise IDs, dependency register and statuses below are this recipe's practical worksheet design.

1. **Write the claim and name the premises.** Give a working version an ID, such as H1 v1. Separate source observations with citations from assumptions P1, P2, and so on. A will listing children is an observation; oldest-first order, regular birth spacing and one mother for all children are separate assumptions. Estimates may set a search window; keep them labeled as estimates and broaden the window when their premises are weak. (*Using Hypotheses*; *RLP 187*)
2. **Keep the live alternatives, including an unknown answer.** Record the strongest plausible rival to the preferred explanation and what information would distinguish them. Do not invent a named father merely to fill H2: an unnamed candidate with a place/date profile is a legitimate working hypothesis. A hypothesis must grow from actual clues; an attractive story with no evidentiary foothold is not a reason to build several generations. (*RLP 187*; *QuickLesson 16*)
3. **Record dependencies before following a tentative branch.** For each proposed conclusion, list the premises it needs. If P1 is "Candidate A is the subject's father", a record naming A's parents supports the observation about A, while "these are the subject's grandparents" remains conditional on P1. Keep candidate records and conditional placements in a separate worksheet or clearly labeled research scenario. A source found because of a hypothesis can still provide independent evidence: assess its information and identity, rather than rejecting it just because of how it was found. (*Using Hypotheses*; *QuickLesson 27*; worksheet synthesis)
4. **Choose a search that can change the choice between explanations.** Write the expected observation under each live version, the actual collection/repository, locality/date coverage and access, and the result that would force a revision. If both explanations predict the same surname nearby, that observation does little to distinguish them; look for identifying detail or another relevant record instead. Include a search that challenges the preferred version. Keep the next phase short and executable; revise it after results. A missing record changes the argument only after the negative-evidence gate above is met. (*RLP 187*; *QuickLesson 16*; test-comparison synthesis)
5. **Record the result before assigning it to the family.** Preserve the source's information, citation and search outcome separately from the interpretation. Then state which premise it supports, challenges or leaves untouched. Mark H1 as open, supported so far, contradicted or rejected, with the reason and date. These are working labels, not probability estimates or proof grades. (*Using Hypotheses*; BCG *Ethics and Standards*; worksheet synthesis)
6. **When a premise changes, review every conclusion that depends on it.** Keep the old version and the reason for revision; mark dependent conclusions for rechecking and withdraw unsupported attribution to the subject. Preserve independently supported observations about the candidate and any surviving conclusions with a sound alternative basis. Discovery of a second marriage reopens the one-mother assumption; it does not by itself assign each child to a different mother. Once a premise actually fails, recheck its estimated dates and search windows rather than silently retaining the old reconstruction. Correct dependent aliases, pedigree diagrams and affected totals too: a caveat in prose does not repair a chart that still asserts the disputed link. Date the amendment in the current conclusion and identify superseded reports as earlier conclusions. (*Using Hypotheses*; *QuickLesson 27*; Kinprove dependency-review synthesis)
7. **Close the phase with the remaining uncertainty visible.** State the observations established, assumptions still open, versions rejected and next discriminating search. Supporting H1 or eliminating the alternatives you happened to list does not automatically prove H1: assess the correlated argument and remaining possibilities using this recipe's proof rules. A phase can end unresolved. (*RLP 187*; *QuickLesson 16*)

Use this small register alongside the ordinary research log:

| Item | Information and basis | Depends on | Effect of a revised premise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | Candidate A is the subject's father; hypothesis from the existing clues | H1's stated observations | Reassess after identifying records |
| O1 | Record R names Candidate A's parents; cite and verify R's identity | R's information, independent of P1 | Keep the supported observation about A |
| C1 | R's named people are the subject's grandparents | O1 and P1 | Withdraw that attribution if P1 fails; do not delete O1 |

When testing a parent-child link with a generated timeline, mark or temporarily exclude
parent-presence events derived solely from that attached child's birth. Compare independently
sourced dated observations instead, retaining gaps as unknown. The [timeline-view example](https://familylocket.com/rlp-218-goldie-may-subway-map-with-richard-miller/)
motivates this dependency check without assuming a particular tool offers a switch. A plotted
overlap or absence does not establish or exclude parentage.

Apply the same dependency check to inherited tree dates before they enter a reality check or a
search window. Ask whether each date is an observation, a derivation or a bound. An unsourced
'about' year may be a marriage minus an assumed age. A birth date identical to the christening date
may be a copied placeholder. A 'before' death date may only mean the person was dead by a later
record, and a death date may have been inferred from 'died as infant'. A baptism supports the
baptism, the name and the listed parents. It does not support a birth date or sex that the entry
does not record; without a stated birth date or infant status, treat it only as a born-before limit.
Distinguish age-derived approximate years from assumption-based search estimates, and do not file a
conventional marriage age as an observed fact. A record hint that matches your own estimate may only be echoing it. Motivating examples:
[Parkinson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=goYE2J6rp3Y),
[christening date](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mzIbF22wUKM),
[Levenia](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lJSDH-L86oE),
[Tree Sweeper](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tg7wVQyTvAM),
[source tagging](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QuCBcmiFGFg),
[timeline grid](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yYtExm8oYxE),
[placeholder hint](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XZp1tyYAJc4),
[adult baptism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v_tpU77sOn4). Label derived values with their basis
and uncertainty, use them only within that uncertainty, and never count them as independent
corroboration of the observations they were derived from. Late and adult christenings occur, so treat one as a conflict to explain, not an impossibility.

**Two source examples explain why the register matters.** In Ancestry's *Using Hypotheses*, estimated sibling dates from will order and assumed spacing guide further research; the presenter explains how a newly discovered marriage could require a different family reconstruction. Those assumptions are search aids, not general demographic rules. In Roberta Estes's proof-table discussion, evidence establishing Mary Younger as John Y. Estes's grandmother still does not establish what his middle initial means. Success in proving one relationship does not retrospectively verify every clue that suggested it. (*Using Hypotheses*; *Creating Genealogy Proof Tables*)

**Prompt to use:** "Work through H1 conditionally. List its source observations and unverified premises; retain plausible alternatives and an unknown answer. For each proposed conclusion, name the premises it depends on. Propose the next accessible record search that could distinguish the versions, including a challenge to H1. Report source information separately from your interpretation. If a premise changes, list affected conclusions and preserve observations that remain independently supported. Do not convert a provisional parentage, estimated date, or unresolved identity into fact in later steps."

## Pitfalls worth internalizing (the least-obvious, most-LLM-usable part)

- It will merge on a name match and present the fused timeline as fact — even when the timeline is biologically impossible.
- It counts sources instead of weighing them; copied trees inflate a single error into false "corroboration."
- It reads more into a document than the document says (a bond, a mention → a proven relationship).
- It flips "no record found" into either "so the fact is true" or "so it doesn't matter" — skipping the exhaustiveness work.
- It states indirect conclusions as direct ones, with no written correlation argument.
- It hands you DNA and paper as two verdicts and calls "more matches" stronger proof.
- It trusts crowd trees, record hints, and gateway-to-royalty links — and a merge it accepts *spawns* new false hints.

## Verification discipline

- **Run the loop, in order: stop → dossier → reality-check → reconcile → converge → rule.** Don't present ancestry through an unresolved link as established. To investigate beyond it, use the conditional procedure above and keep each dependent attribution provisional.
- **Make the model show its work, then audit it.** Require the descriptor sort, the per-statement reliability weighting, the negative-search log (with dates), and the written indirect-evidence argument — a conclusion with none of these is a hypothesis, not proof.
- **Rule on identity and proof yourself.** The AI drafts the summary; you decide whether it can be explained, sourced, and defended in the historical record. (Lesson 5; How to Know if You've Met the GPS)
- **Scope US/Anglo-specific facts before applying them.** "Absence of a death record is not evidence of life," "1940 is the first U.S. census to mark the informant," and marriage-bond conventions are jurisdiction-specific — the *reasoning* transfers, but the concrete record facts must be re-verified against the relevant country's record system.
- **Cross-recipe boundaries:** route cM→relationship odds, Shared cM Tool/WATO, Leeds, clustering, and endogamy-as-cM-inflation to **ai-for-dna-research**; route AI transcription/hint verification and citation format to **ai-for-documentary-records**. Endogamy appears here only as a reason DNA can mislead, so corroborate with documents. De-identify living individuals and get consent before pasting DNA/match data into any cloud tool (per the sibling — not re-derived here).
