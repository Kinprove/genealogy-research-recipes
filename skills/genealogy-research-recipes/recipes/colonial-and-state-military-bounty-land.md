# Identify the issuer of a colonial or state military bounty grant

The grant's location alone leaves the place of enlistment, service and residence open.

## Separate the issuing programs

According to the presenter, check the authority and period before identifying a military tract on a map [S1†]. Copy the authority from the warrant or grant. Use that authority to decide whether to follow a colony, state or federal series. NARA microfilm M829 contains federal Revolutionary War warrants [S3].

According to the presenter, check federal patents and state awards separately for the same veteran [S8† @2026-11-21]. Search the Bureau of Land Management’s General Land Office index for completed federal entries and use the federal warrant series when a patent search is empty [S11 @2026-11-21]. For state awards, use Lloyd Dewitt Bockstruck’s *Revolutionary War Bounty Land Grants: Awarded by State Governments* as a finding aid [S7 @2026-11-21] [S12 @2026-11-21].

Run the federal search if the state award is missing and the service could qualify under federal rules [S7] [S3]. Check the terms of a cession before switching issuing authorities. Virginia reserved land in what is now Ohio for its own Revolutionary War veterans [S13].

For a bounty under the Continental Congress’s resolution of 16 September 1776, compare the warrant acreage with the schedule for the soldier’s documented rank [S7] [S3]. Confirm the issuing program before using acreage to infer rank.

## Follow a state Revolutionary War award

According to the presenter, search Kentucky for a Virginia Revolutionary War military tract [S1† @2026-11-20]. Use the Kentucky Land Office warrant index by veteran, warrant number or immediate assignee. Follow the authorized patent references separately and distinguish the person awarded the warrant from the eventual patentee [S4 @2026-11-20].

According to the presenter, search Pennsylvania under the label *Donation Land* [S1† @2026-11-20]. Use the Pennsylvania State Archives’ guide to find the Pennsylvania Line entitlement return and follow the named soldier into the land records. Keep Donation Land distinct from other military compensation [S5 @2026-11-20].

According to the presenter, search the Pennsylvania Donation Lands separately [S8† @2026-11-21]. Use the surname index in the Pennsylvania State Archives’ *Donation Land Register* (RG 17, series 17.174) to identify the lot and district, then follow them to the tract and patent listings [S21 @2026-11-21].

For a Massachusetts Revolutionary War award in the District of Maine, check the Massachusetts resolve of 5 March 1801 [S7 @2026-11-21] [S14 @2026-11-21]. Search FamilySearch’s *Massachusetts, Revolutionary War Bounty Land Applications, 1805–1845* by applicant [S15 @2026-11-21].

According to the presenter, look for the land from a North Carolina Revolutionary War warrant in present-day Tennessee [S8† @2026-11-21]. Follow the North Carolina military warrant into the grant books described by the Tennessee State Library and Archives. Keep the warrant number distinct from the grant number [S7 @2026-11-21] [S16 @2026-11-21].

According to the presenter, look in western Maryland for the state’s Revolutionary War bounty tracts [S8† @2026-11-21]. Find the soldier and lot number in *Bounty Land Soldiers* (MSA S162), then follow that number in *Lots Westward of Fort Cumberland* (SE1-1) [S18 @2026-11-21].

According to the presenter, check Georgia’s eligibility rules before inferring service from a grant [S8†]. Trace the grant to the bounty certificate to establish the basis of the claim. Georgia’s 1781 act allowed qualifying civilians to receive a bounty without military service [S19]. Include Georgia bounty grants in the search for a documented deserter from the British forces [S7] [S20].

## Trace a Virginia claim or award

For an Ohio tract based on a Virginia award, trace the warrant in the Virginia Military District entry and survey books in federal Record Group 49. Check the separate warrantee and patentee indexes and carry the Virginia issuer through to the patent [S7 @2026-11-21] [S17 @2026-11-21].

According to the presenter, identify the unit before choosing Virginia’s award provisions [S8†]. For general Virginia state or Continental service, start with the 1779 Virginia act [S22]. For George Rogers Clark’s Illinois Regiment, check Virginia’s 1781 resolution and 1783 act, then trace the allotment in the plat of Clark’s Grant in present-day Indiana [S23].

According to the presenter, look up Virginia soldiers and sailors in Samuel Mackay Wilson’s *Catalogue of Revolutionary Soldiers and Sailors of the Commonwealth of Virginia* [S8† @2026-11-21]. Follow a positive result to the Library of Virginia’s *Bounty Warrants* files [S12 @2026-11-21] [S22 @2026-11-21].

According to the presenter, check William Lindsay Hopkins’s *Virginia Revolutionary War Land Grant Claims, 1783–1850 (Rejected)* if the expected award is missing [S8† @2026-11-21]. Follow the claim to the Virginia Governor’s Office *Rejected Claims* (accession 41986) and record the disposition separately from the service evidence in the file [S12 @2026-11-21] [S22 @2026-11-21].

According to the presenter, look up 1852 Virginia warrant exchanges in Margie Brown’s *Genealogical Abstracts, Revolutionary War Veterans, Scrip Act 1852* [S8† @2026-11-21] [S12 @2026-11-21]. If a Virginia warrant remained wholly or partly unsatisfied, trace any exchange under the 1852 act into the federal scrip application and surrendered warrant at the National Archives. Keep the Virginia warrant and the federal scrip as separate records [S24 @2026-11-21] [S22 @2026-11-21].

## Read a colonial group grant

For a colonial New England town, start with its town history to identify the original grant name, granting colony and any service named in the settlement account. Use those clues to find the petition or grant, as with Suncook, also called Lovewell’s Town, in present-day Pembroke, New Hampshire [S7] [S9].

Keep a soldier who served on a single expedition in the colonial grant search when the grant names that expedition, as Massachusetts’s 1728 Suncook grant did for Lovewell’s Pigwacket company and the legal representatives of deceased men [S7] [S10].

According to the presenter, look for group petitions or grants and distinguish a Narragansett settler from the soldier whose service qualified the claimant [S2†]. For a Narragansett township associated with King Philip’s War, record the original qualifying veteran or heir, the proprietor and any later settler as separate roles. Trace any assignment or inheritance before attributing service [S6].
