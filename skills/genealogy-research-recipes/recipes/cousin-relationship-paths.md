# Classify cousin relationships along every documented pedigree path

Use this recipe when the family uses a broad kinship term, a tree shows several paths, or you need to enumerate cousins one generation above and below a focal person. Classify each distinct shared line on its own.

## Count both paths

For each distinct line, find the closest common ancestor on that line and count parent-child steps from each person. For counts of two or more on both sides, cousin degree is the smaller count minus one and removal is the absolute difference between the counts. A 3-step and a 4-step path make second cousins once removed. Keep a one-step path in the sibling or aunt/uncle part of the chart. [S1], [S2], [S3]

When a census household makes two cousins look like peers, check whom each relationship entry refers to. In the 1950 U.S. census, read **Cousin** in the relationship column as a tie to the household head. Count both documented paths before assigning degree or removal, even when the people lived together or were close in age. [S13], [S14]

## Check for additional and half relationships

When one person is called an uncle and the documented descent suggests cousins, trace both maternal and paternal paths. Identify each distinct shared line and record its own cousin degree and removal. If the same ancestor reappears in the pedigree, keep one identity with each documented path to it. [S1], [S2]

When first cousins share both sets of grandparents through two sibling pairs, record both lines as double first cousins. If the connecting siblings share only one parent, record the half relationship on that line. Follow each partner and their children separately, keeping step-relationships as research leads distinct from common descent. [S1]

## Test cousin paths in marriage and birth records

When a civil marriage form indicates that the applicants are related by blood, record it as reported kinship. Read any accompanying description and trace both pedigrees before assigning a cousin degree. [S15], [S16]

If two spouses' birth entries name their grandparents, compare the named parents of the two suspected siblings, even when the marriage entry is missing. Test whether the entries identify the same grandparent couple before recording a first-cousin path through those siblings. [S17], [S18], [S19]

When a Swedish-language marriage permission calls the couple *halfsyskonebarn* or *half-syskonebarn*, read it as half first cousins and test the path through two half siblings to their one shared parent. [S20], [S21]

## Interpret cross- and parallel-cousin paths

When a source uses this distinction, trace cross-cousin paths through the mother's brother or the father's sister, and parallel-cousin paths through the mother's sister or the father's brother. Preserve the source's kinship term alongside the documented path and calculate cousin degree and removal from the path. [S6], [S8]

## Test a third-cousin DNA estimate

When a DNA match is estimated to be a third cousin, start with the tester's great-great-grandparents and trace their other children's descendant lines toward the match. If the match's pedigree is available, compare the ancestors four steps up each tree. [S3], [S6] Keep other paths that fit the shared DNA in play, including half and once-removed relationships, and require evidence for each parent-child link before identifying a shared ancestor. [S7]

## Test a double-first-cousin DNA hypothesis

When the records support one first-cousin path but the shared autosomal DNA suggests a closer match, test the other parental sides for a second first-cousin path. With no additional shared ancestry and two ordinary sibling pairs, double first cousins have the same expected autosomal relatedness as half siblings, an aunt or uncle and their niece or nephew, or a grandparent and grandchild. Keep those alternatives open until the paths are documented. [S22], [S23]

Check whether either parental sibling pair consists of identical twins before interpreting the total. For double first cousins without additional shared ancestry, the expected autosomal relatedness is about 25% with ordinary sibling pairs, including fraternal twins, about 37.5% with one identical-twin pair, and about 50% with two. Use these as pedigree expectations. [S22], [S23]

If the available segment data distinguish sharing on both copies at the same location (IBD2), use verified IBD2 to test for a second parental line. An ordinary half-sibling path alone accounts for sharing through one parent. Inspect any additional shared paths before ruling out half siblings, and leave the question open when only the total or segment lengths are available. [S22], [S23]

Use birth and marriage records to document both sibling pairs and the two marriages between them. Use named children in wills and probate files to test which sibling group and parent a record identifies. When the two fathers are brothers with the same surname, assign each record to a specific brother using the named spouse, parents, or children, and leave ambiguous records unresolved. [S22]

## Enumerate third cousins once removed in both directions

For the lower generation, start four steps up at your great-great-grandparents and go five steps down a different child branch. For the upper generation, start five steps up at your third-great-grandparents and go four steps down a different child branch. Check each resulting pair against the 4/5 chart cell before labeling it. [S1], [S3]
