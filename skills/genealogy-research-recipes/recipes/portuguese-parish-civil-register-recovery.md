# Recover a Portuguese ancestor in the parish and civil registers of mainland Portugal, the Azores and Madeira

Use this recipe to find a baptism, marriage or death in mainland Portugal, the Azores or Madeira, in the parish books or the civil register. Use it before you treat Portuguese records like Spanish or Hispanic ones. That mistake leads to four failures: searching a civil register that did not cover the person, looking in the wrong archive, reading the first surname as the father's, and calling a book missing when another archive holds it. Brazil is out of scope. So are Portuguese-Jewish communities abroad; for Amsterdam, use [Amsterdam archival record recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/b89b086db98d5f1588e5c07b0b02854d14893708/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/amsterdam-archival-record-recovery.md).

It covers the choice of register, who holds the books, tombo.pt, the separate series, what each period recorded, name order and an emigrant's parish. Link out for the shared steps:

## Inputs to gather first

Gather these before searching:

- the event type and approximate year
- whether the person may not have been Catholic
- the district (or, in the Azores, the island), the municipality (concelho) and the parish (freguesia), each as named at the date of the event
- every name form seen so far, with the record it came from
- for an emigrant, the departure years and any destination record that names a place

## 1. Choose the register by date and religion

- **Before 1911, look for a Catholic's events in the parish books, not in a civil register.** The decree of 16 May 1832 began a civil registry and gave non-Catholics the right to register. The decree of 28 November 1878 then gave parish priests the registration of most of the population. It left municipal administrators only the non-Catholic minority's registrations, and this lasted until the end of the monarchy [S5], [S12]. Between 1878 and 1911, a Catholic's event therefore normally has no civil entry. Do not record its absence as a gap [S1], [S5]. The earlier civil registry, from 1832 to 1878, never worked well [S5]. Treat any civil book from those years as a bonus, not as a series you should expect.
- **Do not use Napoleonic or Latin American start dates.** One presenter ties Portuguese civil registration to Napoleon [S2]. Portuguese sources date it from the 1832 and 1878 decrees above [S5], [S12].
- **For a non-Catholic event before 1911, or an 1830s civil entry, search the municipal administration's books.** tombo.pt lists them under an *Administração do Concelho* fonds. Only some years may survive. Elvas, for example, shows births for 1834–1836 and 1910–1911, and marriages for 1834–1836 and 1898–1911 [S10].
- **From 1911, search the civil register first.** The Civil Registry Code of 18 February 1911 made civil registration compulsory for everyone and gave it priority over the church record [S5]. For an event in 1911 itself, search both registers. Parish books continued after 1911, but those later books are held by the diocesan archive or the parish, not by the state archives [S5]. Ask the diocese or the parish for a church entry after 1911.
- **From the 1940 Concordat, also search the transcription books.** A Catholic marriage was then copied from the parish record into the civil register of that parish's area [S5]. In the civil registry fonds these copies form their own series, for example "Transcrições de casamento" in Braga [S10]. Search that series as well as the ordinary marriage books.
- **Do not take 1563 as the first year of every register type.**
  - The Lisbon diocesan constitution of 25 August 1536 already required baptism entries in its area.
  - The Council of Trent session of 11 November 1563 made marriage and baptism registers general.
  - The Roman Ritual of 17 June 1614 added deaths [S5].

  One presenter and the FamilySearch Wiki give 1562 for Trent [S2], [S11]. Use the decree dates above. Take each parish's first year from its own list of books.

## 2. Find who holds the book before you search

- **Treat most pre-1911 parish books as state archive holdings.** In 1911 the parish books were handed over for the new civil registry offices. They now sit on district-archive shelves [S5], and some were lost in the transfer [S11]. They are spread over 26 archives, each with its own website: 1 national, 17 district, 4 regional, 3 municipal and 1 diocesan [S6].
- **Check these exceptions before you call a book missing** [S6]:
  - **Diocese of Lamego:** most of its parish books were never handed to the state and are in the Diocesan Museum and Archive of Lamego [S6]. The Viseu district archive holds some books for the same parishes; for Almacave, for example, the diocesan archive lists books for 1565–1911 and the Viseu archive books for 1837–1898 [S19]. Search both, book by book and year by year.
  - **Elvas:** one account places the books of the former Diocese of Elvas (abolished in 1881) in the Historic Archive of Elvas [S6]. The Portalegre district archive nevertheless lists parish fonds for the Elvas parishes and publishes the Diocese of Elvas marriage-process files [S20], [S21]. Search the Portalegre district archive and the Elvas municipal archive before you call an Elvas book missing.
  - **Lisbon:** the Lisbon District Archive became part of the Torre do Tombo National Archive again on 16 May 2012. Pre-1860 books for Beja, Bragança, Castelo Branco, Faro, Guarda, Santarém and Vila Real can be in the Lisbon collection, because those districts' archives never received them. For those seven districts, search both the district archive and the Torre do Tombo [S11].
  - **Coimbra:** the district archive is an annex of the University Archive.
  - **Books never handed over:** some books stayed with parish priests whose predecessors refused to hand them over in 1911. Others are in private hands.
- **Braga's books open in the University of Minho archive's own catalogue,** pesquisa.adb.uminho.pt [S10]. Do not assume that one national catalogue covers every district.
- **In the Azores, choose the archive by island.** There are three regional archives, one for each former island district [S6]:
  - **Ponta Delgada:** São Miguel and Santa Maria.
  - **Angra do Heroísmo:** Terceira, São Jorge and Graciosa.
  - **Horta:** Faial, Pico, Flores and Corvo.

  The three archives publish their parish registers on one site, the Centro de Conhecimento dos Açores [S6]. Passport registers follow the same split by island [S15], [S10].
- **In Madeira, search the name indexes first.** Madeira's regional archive is the Arquivo Regional e Biblioteca Pública da Madeira [S6]. A 2022 FamilySearch class reported name indexes for marriages 1539–1962, baptisms 1860–1911 and passports 1860–1975 [S16]. Most mainland books are browsed by parish instead [S11], [S8].
- **Expect civil books to move on a schedule.** Article 15 of Decree-Law 324/2007 sends a digitized register book to the national archives authority once its last entry is more than 30 years old for deaths, more than 50 years for marriages, and more than 100 years for all other books, births included [S5], [S12]. A 1950 marriage can therefore already be in an archive while a 1950 birth is still at the civil registry office (conservatória). For each civil entry, record whether its book has passed its threshold. Check both custodians before you call it missing.
- **Inside the threshold, order a certificate (certidão).** The Civil Online service says anyone may request a birth, marriage or death certificate, either their own or another person's [S17]. The FamilySearch Wiki warns that privacy rules may still require proof of relationship [S12]. Record any refusal as an access state, not as an absent record.

## 3. Use tombo.pt as a list of books, not as an archive

- **Do not cite tombo.pt as the national archive.** One author runs it privately, without any tie to the archives [S7]. It holds no images; every book opens on its holding archive's site [S8]. One presenter calls it the Torre do Tombo's website [S2], and a genealogy firm describes it as holding record sets [S1]. Both are wrong. Cite the holding archive and its reference code.
- **Look under the level that kept the record.** Church books are listed under the civil parish (freguesia), civil registration under the municipality (concelho), and passports under the district [S16]. Browse district, then municipality, then parish. In the Azores, choose the island first [S8].
- **Search the historical parish name.** tombo.pt lists parishes that no longer exist [S8]. A modern "União das Freguesias de X e Y" is a merger made under Law 11-A/2013 [S18]. Search each former parish under its own name.
- **Read a title without a link as "exists, not online".** Use the book's contact link to ask its archive [S8]. A book judged too damaged (rated red) is withheld from both reading-room use and digitization [S3]. Record it as not online, never as missing.
- **Read the holding archive on every row.** One parish page can mix books from several archives [S7]. For example, a Braga-district parish lists its 1593–1911 books at the Braga archive and a second set of yearly books for 1886–1911 at the Viana do Castelo archive [S10]. Record each set as its own holding with its own locator. Then use the Catholic holdings recipe to compare them.
- **Do not stop at FamilySearch.** Images appear first on the district archive's site and reach FamilySearch later, and tombo.pt links to books that FamilySearch does not have [S11]. The FamilySearch indexes (Portugal Baptisms 1570–1910, Marriages 1760–1910, Deaths 1640–1910) cover only some localities [S11]. A miss in them is a coverage gap.
- **If a book is on no online list,** check the national archive's 1993 two-volume *Inventário Colectivo dos Registos Paroquiais*. It names the holding archive for each parish's books (FamilySearch Library book 946.9 A3i) [S11].

## 4. Search the marriage-process file and other series separately

- **A marriage-process file (processo de casamento) is not the marriage entry.** Couples compiled it for the church authorities before the wedding. It can name the bride's and groom's parents and give their birth dates, birthplaces and any former marriage [S11]. A genealogy firm lists it among the parish record types [S1]. Files survive more often for the 19th and 20th centuries [S11]. Search the holding archive's catalogue for the series by name. Record the result as searched, found or not found.
- **Search the dispensation as a third record.** Dispensations covered impediments such as consanguinity, affinity or an earlier marriage, and most are not digitized [S11]. They sit in the diocese's own records, so look for the current holder of that diocesan fonds: the diocesan archive, or a district archive where the diocese's records passed to the state, as the Diocese of Elvas files did at Portalegre [S11], [S21]. Use the Catholic holdings recipe to read which impediment a dispensation names.
- **Use the confession rolls (róis de confessados) as a household list.** A priest listed each Catholic in his parish who had reached the "age of reason", family by family. The rolls often give age and marital status, and they are at district archives or the parish [S14].
- **Do not search for national census returns.** The returns from 1864 onward are believed destroyed once the statistics were compiled [S14].
- **When the vital records run out,** search passports, wills and de genere files (the investigation of a candidate for the priesthood) [S9]. In some municipalities, wills sit in the municipal administration's fonds [S10].

## 5. Expect only what each period recorded

- **Read entries by period** [S9]:
  - **16th century to the late 17th century:** a baptism names the child, parents, godparents and date. A marriage names the couple and witnesses. A death gives the name and, at most, widowhood.
  - **Late 17th century to 1860:** a baptism adds the parents' birthplaces. A marriage adds the couple's birthplaces and parents.
  - **1860 to 1911:** the decree of 19 August 1859 standardized the entries. A baptism names the grandparents. A marriage gives the couple's ages. A death names the parents and the birthplace.
- **Do not log a missing grandparent in a pre-1860 baptism as an omission.** The form did not ask for one.
- **Read the margins of a civil birth entry.** It can carry later notes of the person's marriage or death [S9]. Treat each note as its own event and as a lead to that record.
- **Search legitimations in their own series.** A later legitimation of a child born outside marriage sits in a separate civil series [S1], [S12]. In Braga it is filed with acknowledgements of paternity [S10].
- **Allow for baptism dates in emigrant records.** One presenter says Azorean and Madeiran Catholics sometimes gave the baptism or confirmation date as a birth date [S4]. For the date roles, use the Hispanic register recipe.

## 6. Build the name in Portuguese order

- **Do not read a Portuguese name in Spanish order.** When both parents' surnames appear, the mother's usually comes first and the father's last [S13]. Search the last surname first, then each other surname alone, then the full string. In a married woman's name, the final surnames may be her husband's [S13], [S2].
- **Do not reject a sibling for a different surname.** Portugal never had a standard way of passing on surnames [S13]. Daughters often took the mother's surname and sons the father's, and women usually kept their maiden surname [S13]. Test a candidate on the parents, the parish and the dates.
- **Compare every element of a long name.** A married woman could have two given names and six surnames, so a full name can run past twelve words [S2], [S13]. Two different people can differ in a single element [S2].
- **Keep Marian name pairs together as the given name.** Maria da Conceição, Maria das Dores and Maria de Fátima are double given names [S13]. Do not split off "da Conceição" as a surname without evidence.
- **Allow for drift in emigrant names.** One presenter says Azorean and Madeiran families switched surnames between records, for example to honour a favourite great-uncle or a godmother [S4]. Names were also anglicized in the U.S., for example Rodrigues to Rogers, Pereira to Perry, Silva to Silver or Sylvia, and Oliveira to Oliver [S15]. Search the Portuguese form in Portuguese records, and keep each form as a separate entry in the name register.

## 7. Find the parish of an emigrant

- **Search the passport registers.** District civil governments (Governo Civil) kept them, and they are now at the district archives. Examples: Porto 1834–1966, Lisbon 1839–1944, Braga 1868–1965 [S15], [S10]. Migration records can give the birthplace and former residence [S15]. In the Azores, the passports are held by the archive for the island. In Madeira, use the regional archive's passport index [S15], [S16].
- **Trace the relatives who emigrated.** Siblings, parents or other relatives who left Portugal, even on the same voyage, may name the hometown [S1].
- **Search the destination community's own records.** For Azoreans and Madeirans in the U.S., one presenter recommends the records of Portuguese lay societies in the Northeast and in California [S4]. For the U.S. arrival and naturalization search, use the U.S. immigration recipe.
- **Once you have a parish name, go back to step 2** and find who holds its books.

## Close with a register packet

Return:

- **Jurisdiction row:** district or island, municipality, and parish under its name at the event date, plus the diocese.
- **Register decision:** parish, civil or both for each event, with the reason (date, religion, the 1940 transcription rule).
- **Holdings rows:** one per book or set of books, with the holding archive's code, the years covered, whether it is online, its locator, and the level inspected (index, catalogue entry or image).
- **Civil-book rows:** whether each book has passed its transfer threshold, and the custodian checked.
- **Separate-series rows:** marriage-process file, dispensation, confession roll and passport register, each marked searched, found or not found.
- **Name register:** every form in Portuguese order, and which surname was searched where.
- **Coverage gaps:** books listed but not online, books withheld for condition, post-1911 parish books held by the church, and the Lamego, Elvas and Lisbon exceptions checked.

The model assembles this packet. The researcher decides identity and kinship.
