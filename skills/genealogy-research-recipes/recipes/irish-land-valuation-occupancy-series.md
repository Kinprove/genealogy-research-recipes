# Read Irish valuation, tithe and estate-rental lists as dated occupancy series

Use this recipe when an Irish surname, county and possibly a parish or townland lead you to Griffith's (Primary) Valuation, the Tithe Applotment books, the manuscript valuation revision books or an estate-court rental. Read each one as a dated list of occupiers and holdings. None of them is a household census. The common mistakes are:

- treating Griffith's as a list of households or neighbours;
- choosing among same-named occupiers because of family lore or apparent wealth;
- dropping the parenthetical marks after names;
- reading an occupier change as father-to-son inheritance;
- turning "only surviving" into an exact death date.

This recipe builds the holding register and the occupancy chain. Send every identity or kinship ruling to [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md). If the next step is a Catholic baptism or marriage register for the narrowed parish, use [Irish Catholic parish image recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/irish-catholic-parish-image-recovery.md).

## Open every same-name entry as a candidate row

1. Treat Griffith's as a tax list, not a census substitute that lists people [S3]. Each entry describes a holding and its valuation, not a household. Griffith's is free to search online at Ask About Ireland (askaboutireland.ie) [S1, S2].
2. Search the surname across the candidate county, and allow for spelling variation. Open the detail for **every** entry with the target given name. Do not stop at the first one that seems plausible. In [S1], one surname in one county gave several entries for the same given name, and they differed sharply. One was a house only, rated at a few shillings, under one lessor. Another was a house and land of about 15 acres under a different lessor. Another occupier with the same surname was rated far higher.
3. Make one row per entry. Record these fields separately:
   - townland
   - lot number, copied literally (for example a lot with a letter suffix)
   - occupier name, copied literally
   - immediate lessor, copied literally ("in fee" there means the occupier was also the legal owner)
   - holding type (house only, or house and land)
   - acreage in acres, roods and perches
   - rateable value
   - series, and the date printed on the volume you consulted
   Do not identify the family at this stage.
4. Do not rank or eliminate rows on the strength of family lore about wealth or land. In [S1] the presenter tested a "wealthy family" story against holding size and value and then named a "most likely candidate". Here, that kind of ranking is only a working note attached to the rows. It is not an identification. A small holding does not rule out a person, and a large one does not confirm the lore.
5. Note lessor and sub-lease relationships between occupiers who share a surname. [S1] shows one occupier leasing from another with the same name, marked junior and senior. Record the "Jr"/"Sr" literally and add no relationship.

## Keep distinguishers literal and allow for several lots

1. Copy any parenthetical word or name after an occupier's name exactly as written, for example *John Daly (James)*. It is a mark that separates same-named occupiers [S3]. It is often the father's first name, but valuers also used Jr/Sr, occupation and physical traits as distinguishers. Hold the father's-name reading as a hypothesis in a separate column, and do not use it to fill a parent field.
2. One person can lease several holdings and so appear more than once [S3]. Mark repeated same-name rows within a townland as *possibly one occupier holding several lots*. Do not merge them by default. The presenter in [S3] goes further and says that same-named entries without distinguishers in one townland are the same person. Treat that as the presenter's rule of thumb, not a finding. The same presenter describes the traditional naming pattern (eldest son after the paternal grandfather, second after the maternal grandfather, third after the father) producing several men of the same name and similar age in one area [S3]. The pattern was not followed everywhere, but where it was, that is the very situation in which a default merge fails.
3. Draft family groups by townland only as clusters of candidates. "Probably all related" because they lived in one area for generations [S3] is a reason to look further, not evidence of kinship.

## Do not read entry order as adjacency; narrow the townland with tithe clusters

1. Names that sit next to each other in Griffith's are not necessarily next-door neighbours. Do not read entry order the way you would read a census visitation order [S2]. Entries follow the reference numbers on the Ordnance Survey map, so nearby lot numbers often mean nearby plots, not adjoining houses. Use the lot reference to place each row on the map rather than inferring position from the page.
2. If the county and the family's network of names are known but the townland is not, search the Tithe Applotment books **by place** on the National Archives of Ireland site (titheapplotmentbooks.nationalarchives.ie), which can be browsed by county, parish and townland [S2]. Look for places where the known cluster of family surnames appears together, then compare that cluster with the valuation for the same area [S2]. Record:
   - each place searched
   - the surnames found in each place
   - places searched where the cluster was not found
   - the date on each tithe book consulted, so the gap to the valuation entry is explicit
3. A cluster that turns up in both series narrows the search area. It does not identify any one occupier. The presenter's explanation that arranged matches kept land together [S2] is context for why clusters recur, not a kinship link between any two rows.

## Follow the same lot through the revision books

1. Once you have a Griffith's holding, follow **that lot** forward in the manuscript valuation revision ("cancelled") books. Valuers revisited the books, crossed out the occupier, wrote in the new one and dated the change [S3]. The books are manuscript only and were never printed, and Republic holdings can be followed in them into the 1970s [S3]. For the six counties of Northern Ireland they are held by PRONI. For the Republic they are in the Tailte Éireann Valuation archive in Dublin (the former Valuation Office merged into Tailte Éireann in 2023). Access is by pre-booked appointment only; digitised records are viewed on customer terminals in the office, and books not yet digitised are produced as physical volumes (Tailte Éireann).
2. For each change, record:
   - book and page
   - lot
   - the crossed-out name, copied literally
   - the replacement name, copied literally
   - the date written beside the change
   - the colour or ink of the entry, if visible
3. Treat each change as a dated observation that the occupier changed. Do not record it as an inheritance. A change can be a correction of a misread name. In the example in [S3], a family's surname in Griffith's was crossed out and replaced with a different surname in a revision dated 1883. The presenter took this to be a father-to-son transfer that also revealed the earlier misreading. On the page itself, that is two hypotheses: a correction and a transfer.
4. Later changes on the same lot, including passing to a different surname, need outside evidence for any relationship. In [S3], the presenter knew from other sources that a later occupier was the brother-in-law of the previous occupier. The revision book did not show it.

## Separate lease-era and sale-year layers in estate-court rentals

1. An estate-court rental lists tenants at the time of the sale, with lease details. The presenter in [S4] says the court began in 1848. That is wrong: the Encumbered Estates Court was set up under an Act of 1849, and the Landed Estates Court succeeded it in 1858. Record which court issued the rental, and take the rental's sale or statement date from the rental itself, never from a court start year. Record that date separately from each tenant's lease date.
2. Record the lease date and every named life or occupant, with the relationship wording copied literally ("his brother", "son of the said..."). Record this as an observation dated to the lease, not to the sale year. In the [S4] example, the lease is dated 1 May 1800 and names a man, his brother and a son, across at least two generations.
3. Read "only surviving" (or similar wording) as a window. The other named lives died at some point between the lease date and the date of the rental's statement [S4]. Record the death as "after [lease date], before [rental date]". Never assign a year inside that window. The presenter in [S4] closed the window at 1848, her stated court start year; use the date on the rental instead.

## Close with a holding register

Return:

- **Holding register.** One row per series entry, keyed by county, parish, townland and lot. Each row has the literal occupier, the distinguisher, the lessor, the holding type, the acreage, the value, the series and its date, and the status of the image or transcription you saw.
- **Occupancy chain** for each followed lot. List the revision-book changes in date order, each with its literal before and after names, and mark which interpretation is open: correction, transfer or unknown.
- **Tithe and valuation cluster comparison.** List the places searched, the surnames found, the places where the cluster was absent, and the dates of both series.
- **Estate-rental layers.** Give the issuing court, the lease date and its lives separately from the rental date, plus any death windows.
- **Separate hypothesis list.** Include proposed merges of same-name rows, patronymic readings of distinguishers, kinship behind transfers, and the result of testing the lore. Route these to evidence/proof judgment, with the next record named for each.
