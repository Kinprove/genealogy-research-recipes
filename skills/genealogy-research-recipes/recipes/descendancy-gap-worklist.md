# Build a ranked descendancy gap worklist

Use this recipe when you are researching downward from an ancestor who is already verified, and you have a collaborative tree or descendancy view to work from. "Research the descendants" is too open to act on. Turn it into a ranked list of specific gaps. Tie each gap to the records the person's lifespan crosses, and sweep one generation at a time. An empty field leaves the event unresolved in this tree. It does not mean the event never happened. For the household-by-census grid, use [research documentation § Compare one family across named censuses](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/research-documentation.md). For finding a missing household, use [U.S. census practical workflows](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/us-census-practical-workflows.md). For identity and conflation rulings, use [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## Do not read a blank as a finding

The tree shows no spouse, no death or no children for someone. Record that as an **open question**. Do not write "never married", "died young" or "childless" unless a source says so. One unrecorded marriage can hide a whole branch. The presenter's example is Henry Woodson Morgan, who lived from 1830 to 1895 (65 years) with no marriage shown. The presenter estimates that if he did marry and have children, there could be two or three thousand descendants between 1830 and the present to account for [S1]. Only state an absence as a conclusion after a search, and give the source for it (see the closing section below).

## Flag the three gap patterns

Go through the whole descendancy view and flag every person who fits one of these patterns [S1], [S2]:

1. **Birth but no death.** The person has a birth date but no death date or age at death. They might have died as an infant, or lived to old age and married more than once. A missing death date also leaves the marriage question open [S1], [S2].
2. **Adult with no spouse.** The person lived long enough to marry, but no spouse is recorded [S1].
3. **Couple with no children or too few.** The couple's lifespans allowed time for children, but none are recorded, or very few are. The presenter says that in the 1800s having children was much more common than having none, and that smaller families of one or two children become more plausible in the late 1800s and early 1900s [S1], [S2]. Apply this rule: if the couple's childbearing years fell in the 1800s, flag zero children and also flag one or two children. If they fell in the late 1800s or early 1900s, flag zero children and rank one or two children lower. U.S. fertility fell steadily across the whole 19th century, and the fall started earlier in New England and the Mid-Atlantic, so switch to the late-period rule earlier for couples there. Check whether the line was compiled surname-only: someone took the direct ancestor in each generation and never added the other children, second wives or other relationships [S2]. A widow who was young enough to still have children at home may well have remarried, so put that on the list too [S2].

Names repeat often within one family, so keep same-named people apart while you flag [S1].

## Rank by source count before working

Check each flagged person's source count before you start research. Do the people with few or no sources first [S1], [S2]. FamilySearch Family Tree shows the number of attached sources on the person's page (Sources tab and Details page). The descendancy view shows icons for record hints, research suggestions and data problems, and a research suggestion can flag "no sources attached". It does not show the count itself, so open the person page to read it. Lower the rank of well-sourced people unless the gap itself is unsourced. The goal is to add what is missing, not to confirm work someone has already done [S2]. Use relatives who already have sources to decide where to search first [S1].

For each person on the list, record: person ID, gap type, source count, lifespan or birth-to-last-known span, the records that span crosses, and the first targets to check.

## Tie each gap to the records its span crosses

From each lifespan, list every census taken while the person was alive. Then mark each one as name-level, head-only, or largely lost. The name-level censuses that survive are the first targets [S1]. In the U.S., the 1820, 1830 and 1840 censuses name only heads of household, so anyone else appears only as an unnamed tally in the household where they were counted. The 1890 population schedule was mostly destroyed after the January 1921 Commerce Department fire. Check the surviving fragments, but expect a gap between 1880 and 1900.

The presenter's examples, with those coverage notes added:

- **Born 1819, died 1893 (U.S.).** The presenter names the 1850, 1860, 1870 and 1880 censuses, which are the four surviving censuses that list this person by name. By 1850 the person was an adult [S1]. The same lifespan also crosses 1820–1840, where only heads of household are named: in 1840, first search for the person by name as a head of household; if they are not found, find the household in which they were counted. It also crosses 1890, where you should check the fragments.
- **Born 1855.** The presenter places this person in the 1860, 1870 and 1880 U.S. censuses, and says that in England the equivalent would be 1861, 1871, 1881 and 1891 [S1]. The two lists cover different spans. If the person was alive in 1890, the U.S. 1890 census is largely lost. If they lived longer, add 1900 and later in the U.S., and 1901, 1911 and 1921 in England.

Work out the list from each person's own dates and jurisdiction. Do not reuse these lists as fixed defaults. The presenter calls people in this period comparatively accessible because these censuses exist, and treats that as a factor in choosing whom to research first [S1]. Rank them accordingly.

Censuses are not the only targets. Lineage-society applications can carry documented descendant lines. For example, Sons of the American Revolution membership applications require the applicant to prove direct descent from a Patriot [S3]. Use the documents an application cites as leads for each generation of the descendant line.

## Sweep one generation at a time

Do not follow whatever branch looks most interesting. Jumping around that way is how siblings get skipped, and the connection you needed may be in a skipped sibling's line [S3]. Instead:

- Within each family, go from the oldest child to the youngest. The presenter says most family software programs place the oldest child on the far left, so the sweep runs left to right [S3]. In a vertical list view such as FamilySearch's descendancy view, use birth order instead.
- Identify every child you can for that family, then move to the next family in the same generation [S3].
- Finish the whole generation before you go down to the next one. Record the order you used so a later session can pick up where you stopped [S3].
- Use the oldest children to confirm the family. If the same child appears with the other children in two censuses in a row (the presenter's example is 1910 and 1920), that agreement is a quick check that you are following the right household [S3].

## Close, open or correct each family

Give every family in the sweep log one status:

- **Open:** gaps remain. Name the next records to check.
- **Closed:** state the reason and the source. For example, a "never married/no children" tag tells collaborators this absence was researched [S3].
- **Corrected:** research may show that the people in a family are not actually related to each other, or that the family was made up. If so, stop going down. Go back up the line and correct it until you reach the right ancestors, then continue [S1]. Send merge and separation decisions to the human through [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).

Return three things: the ranked worklist, the sweep log with each family's status, and a list of families flagged for correction. Leave out open-ended "research the descendants" suggestions.
