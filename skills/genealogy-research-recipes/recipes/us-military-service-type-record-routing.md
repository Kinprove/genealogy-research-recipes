# Route pre-1900 U.S. military service by service type before searching or ruling it out

Use this recipe before you search for, or rule out, pre-1900 U.S. military service. The usual failure is to search only federal compiled-service or pension indexes and then report "no service". A related mistake is reading an empty donation-land or bounty-land search as meaningful when the man was never eligible. This recipe works out the probable service type, sends each type to its own record series and custodian, labels what each record actually shows, and keeps same-named men apart.

To retrieve a pension or bounty-land file, use [pension-file recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/us-military-pension-file-recovery.md) (§1 for custodians, §4 for service the file does not declare). For merge or separate rulings, use [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## 1. Fix residence and enlistment place, then classify the service

Before opening any index, record where the man lived and where and when he probably enlisted. Without that, a militia man cannot be told apart from a Continental soldier, and either kind is hard to find [S1].

Classify each service clue into exactly one working type, and record the basis for that choice:

- **Continental or volunteer.** Search the compiled military service records (CMSRs) at the National Archives. CMSRs cover the volunteer army (NARA gives 1775–1902) and do **not** cover regular-army enlistments [S2]. CMSRs also exist for volunteers who served in the 1784–1811 "old war" period (NARA M905) [S4]. Revolutionary CMSRs are reconstructions. A War Department fire on 8 November 1800 destroyed most of the original records, and more were lost in 1814. NARA Record Group 93 was later put together as a substitute, from records transferred by the State, Interior and Treasury Departments (1893–1913) and from purchased and copied material [S2]. An incomplete Revolutionary CMSR therefore does not show that a man did not serve.
- **Militia, state line or ranger.** For state-only service, look in state (and sometimes county) records; NARA does not hold state militia records [S1], [S7]. Militia called into federal service also has CMSRs at NARA (for the War of 1812, federalized militia are in RG 94, index M602), so search both. State archives hold state militia, National Guard and Confederate pension records. Confederate pensions are not at NARA [S7]. Ranger companies are a separate in-between category (see §2).
- **Regular army.** Not in the volunteer CMSRs [S2]. For enlisted men, search the Register of Enlistments in the U.S. Army at NARA. For officers, start with Heitman's *Historical Register* and, for service from 1863, the Commission Branch and ACP files in RG 94 (NARA, *Prologue*, Fall 2002).
- **Loyalist.** Treat this as its own route (§5). Patriot service indexes are the wrong place to look.

If the type is unknown, keep two or more routes open. Do not choose one by default. Expect a man to have service of more than one type. An empty or incomplete CMSR search does not end the search. Check pension and state records for service the CMSR leaves out (pension recipe §4).

## 2. Write each series' eligibility rule before reading a miss

Build a routing table with one row per series: custodian, coverage, eligibility rule, and whether a miss is meaningful. A miss counts only when the man met the eligibility rule **and** the series covers his period and place.

- **Pennsylvania donation land.** This went only to Pennsylvania Continental Line veterans who served to the end of the war, so militia never qualified. A Line soldier who served from 1775 until the 1781 mutiny did not qualify either [S3]. The land was laid out in ten districts in northwestern Pennsylvania, in parts of Butler, Clarion, Crawford, Erie, Lawrence, Mercer, Venango and Warren counties. The index is on the State Archives' land-record indices page [S3]. The archivist interviewed adds one exception: some men from the special ranger companies, who were neither Line nor militia, did receive donation land [S3]. If the man was militia, or left the Line before the end of the war, a miss tells you nothing.
- **State bounty land.** This is a separate route from federal bounty land. Bockstruck's book (below) covers the nine states that granted land: Connecticut, Georgia, Maryland, Massachusetts, New York, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, South Carolina and Virginia. One presenter's spoken list named New Jersey in place of North Carolina; the publisher's description of the book does not [S4]. The same presenter says Massachusetts made veterans choose between federal and state land [S4]. If that holds, a Massachusetts man missing from the federal records may simply have taken the state grant.
  - Search Lloyd Bockstruck's *Revolutionary War Bounty Land Grants Awarded by State Governments* first. The presenter says it is alphabetical regardless of state. Then go to that state's archive for the record itself [S4].
  - Virginia warrants could lie in present-day Kentucky and Ohio (the Virginia Military District). The presenter adds West Virginia [S4]. The Kentucky Secretary of State website has 4,748 Virginia Revolutionary War warrants digitized [S4]. Search where the land lay, not only where the soldier came from.

## 3. Label what kind of evidence each record is

Attach exactly one evidence-kind label to every record: **service proof**, **payment**, **levy/fine**, **enumeration** or **claim lead**.

- **Militia fines and levies in tax lists are levy/fine, never service proof.** Pennsylvania militia fines were charged to men who were not serving. The archivist adds two details. Older men who could not serve were charged too. During the Revolution, some levies fell on a whole township, or on a group within it, depending on whether a volunteer could be found [S3]. Look for muster, class or account records instead.
- **A county-history "militia roster" is enumeration.** Read its heading and notes before treating it as a list of men who served. One presenter shows a Georgia county history whose militia roster stands in for lost early censuses. Its notes say some enumerators counted women and enslaved people and others did not [S5]. Record which rule the roster says it followed.
- **Pennsylvania militia-loan certificates.** These become service proof once you follow them to the underlying accounts. The archivist says they were issued from 1784 for wartime militia service, mostly frontier or guard duty. Cards with a certificate number that relate to militia loans say "militia loan accounts" in the lower right corner. The card itself gives little more than the name, the amount paid and perhaps the county. Following the certificate into the militia loan accounts can recover the date range, and often the place, of the tour. He calls these among the few records that prove when and where a man served [S3]. POWER Library hosts the Revolutionary War Military Abstract Card File (RG-13, Comptroller General's records). The archivist says the bulk of the militia records are there [S3], but not all of the collection is online.
- **State pay vouchers are payment records with service value.** The North Carolina State Archives holds soldier pay vouchers. The presenter showing them says each records the amount and when and where it was claimed, and that the DAR uses them to prove service [S6].
- **A state bounty-land warrant can carry service details.** The Virginia example names the soldier's Continental Line service and its length, as opposed to militia [S4]. Label it service proof only for what it actually states.

## 4. Read unit designations by roster status

Pennsylvania counties were organized into eight battalion districts under a County Lieutenant. For drafted militia, one presenter describes each drafted man being assigned a class from one to eight. The same presenter says one man could carry a completely different class, company and battalion number on the **inactive** roster than on the **active-duty** roster. His Lancaster County example moves from 2nd battalion, 7th company, 8th class (active) to 7th battalion, 1st company, 2nd class (inactive) [S4]. He says other colonies used broadly similar structures, though not identical ones [S4].

Record the roster status next to every designation. Two different number sets do not by themselves mean two men or two units. Do not apply the Pennsylvania renumbering to another colony without checking that colony's own rule.

## 5. Route a suspected Loyalist separately

Search period newspapers for notices of intent to leave the country and for public sales of confiscated property under the person's name [S4]. Then search Ancestry's "UK, American Loyalist Claims, 1776–1835" and, for New York, "New York, Sales of Loyalist Land, 1762–1830". The presenter also names an American Loyalist migration database, and says the claims are the largest of his three collections [S4]. Many claims were rejected or only partly paid, but even an unpaid claim can preserve sworn affidavits about residence and property lost [S4]. Treat a newspaper notice as a claim lead, not as an identification. German auxiliaries are a separate population and are not routed here.

## 6. Separate same-named men without choosing a name-only hit

The failure this section blocks is choosing one soldier from a name-only or name-and-state index result. Build a candidate-separation table with these columns: candidate, unit, where the unit was recruited, militia district, roster status, and voucher amount / date / place claimed. Fill it in as follows:

- Find where each candidate's company or regiment was raised, for example from FamilySearch Wiki regiment and county pages. Compare that with the man's census residence. Prioritize units recruited nearby; keep other candidates unless independent evidence excludes them [S7]. One presenter separated a Civil War soldier from dozens of same-named men by first fixing his birth date and Indiana residence, then listing the units raised from his area [S8].
- Use a county militia-district map to place same-named men in different districts [S5].
- Where vouchers survive, compare amount, date and place claimed, man by man [S6].
- Before splitting rows, check roster status (§4). Active and inactive designations that differ can belong to one man.

Each row is a narrowing clue, not an identification. Hand the merge or separate ruling to evidence/proof judgment.

## 7. Return the routing packet

Return:

- the service-type classification and its basis;
- the routing table (custodian, coverage, eligibility rule, whether a miss is meaningful, what was actually searched);
- an evidence-kind label for each record;
- the candidate-separation table;
- handoffs to pension recovery and proof judgment.

Mark every series you did not search as unsearched. Report "no service found" only for the series whose eligibility and coverage fit the man.
