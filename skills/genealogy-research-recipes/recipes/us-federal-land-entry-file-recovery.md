# Recover a U.S. federal land-entry case file behind (or without) a GLO patent

Use this recipe when a person may have acquired federal public-domain land, whether by military bounty-land warrant, cash entry or homestead. It also applies when your only clue is a BLM General Land Office (GLO) patent hit, the bounty-land number on a pension card, or a final-proof notice. The patent is only the last step of a federal land entry. Follow the patent, or the lack of one, through the legal description, the tract book and the bounty-land series to the land-entry case file in National Archives (NARA) Record Group 49. Keep veteran, heir, assignee, locator and patentee as separate roles.

Overlapping steps belong to sibling recipes. For the pension and bounty-land *application* packet and its custodian, use [military pension file recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/us-military-pension-file-recovery.md). For warrant/survey/patent stages and later county conveyances, use [land index and book-series recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/land-index-book-series-recovery.md). For packet-versus-selected-image boundaries, use [Ancestry record search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/ancestry-record-search-recovery.md). For identity and kinship rulings, use [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## What goes wrong

- Stopping at the patent image. The digitized GLO item is the final grant or warrant image, often a single page. It is not the entry file. [S1], [S4], [S5]
- Treating a failed GLO name search as proof that no claim existed. The GLO site indexes completed patents. Cancelled, relinquished or otherwise incomplete entries never became patents and do not appear there. [S3], [S14]
- Merging the bounty-land warrant *application* with the land *entry* file. They are separate series. The application files are in RG 15 and the entry files are in RG 49. [S2], [S1]
- Assuming that the person who located a warrant served, was related to the veteran or lived on the land. [S1], [S8], [S9], [S10], [S13]
- Reading a patent, or a neighbouring patentee, as evidence of residence. [S11]

## 1. Route the entry by authority

Start an entry-routing row for each GLO hit. Record the authority (military warrant, cash sale, homestead or another act), the act year, the acreage, the Document Nr. and the Accession Nr. as separate labelled fields, any warrant number, the land office and the legal description. The authority field classifies the entry. A military-warrant authority means the entry was not a cash sale or a homestead. In the Strauss example, the GLO detail showed a military warrant entry of 160 acres, file number 36643, under the bounty-land act of 1855. [S1]

If you have a pension or bounty-land index card, compare its BLWT number with the GLO hit. A BLWT number has three parts in this order: warrant/file number, acreage and act year (for example 36643-160-55). In Strauss's example all three matched the patent record. [S1] If any one part fails to match, treat the patent as belonging to a different warrant or person until you can explain the difference.

Read the acreage against the act. A veteran who received 40 acres under the 1850 act could receive another 120 acres under the 1855 act, for 160 acres in total. [S2] So one veteran can lie behind more than one warrant and more than one entry. The 1855 act was the last general bounty-land act, but warrants under the 1847–1855 acts kept being issued for decades. Acreage varied by rank and changed with each act. For example, the basic War of 1812 grant of 160 acres was raised to 320 by an 1814 act. [S9], [S12] State bounty-land programmes had their own rules and fall outside this federal route. [S12], [S1]

## 2. Keep the two bounty-land series apart

Record two locators, not one:

- **Warrant application file (RG 15):** the declaration by the veteran, widow or heir. It may contain service details, the marriage date and place, the veteran's death, residences and heirs. [S2], [S12] Retrieve it through the pension recipe.
- **Land-entry case file (RG 49):** the surrendered warrant, where it was located on a tract and, if it was assigned, the signed assignment. The issued warrant itself is in the entry file, not the application file. [S2], [S1]

Reading one series does not mean you have searched the other, in either direction. NARA holds the RG 49 land-entry case files, and the surrendered warrants are not online. [S1], [S4], [S5] The GLO site shows whether an entry exists and displays one page. [S1]

## 3. Build the party-role table before interpreting anyone

Keep one row per person, with the role that person plays: veteran; widow or heir who applied; assignee; locator/enterer; patentee. Record names exactly as each document gives them.

- **Same-name patentees:** separate them by the spouse listed with each entry. In Strauss's search, three entries under one name split into two couples with different wives, and only one couple matched the veteran's widow. [S1]
- **Assignment:** the Act of 1852 made warrants assignable. Brokers and speculators traded them, and newspapers quoted their prices. [S9], [S10], [S8] In the Strauss example, the widow assigned the warrant to a man whose relationship to the family is unknown. His name appears on the GLO page, and her signed assignment is on the back of the original warrant. [S1] Another presenter found that a War of 1812 veteran's bounty land went to someone else several years after the award. [S13]
- **Long delay:** a warrant could be entered long after it was issued, so the locator's entry date dates neither the veteran's service nor the assignment. Tanner gives an extreme case from his own experience: a 1930s entry in Davis County, Utah, made with a War of 1812 warrant. This anecdote has not been independently confirmed. [S8], [S10]

A warrant-based entry does not show that the locator served, was related to the veteran, or lived in the area when the warrant was issued. Treat any assignee as a separate identity question, and send kinship questions to evidence/proof judgment.

## 4. If GLO has no name hit, check NARA's cancelled-entry name indexes, then get a legal description

A missing name in the GLO index reflects what the index covers. It is not a negative finding. The site indexes completed title records, and BLM states that it does not yet hold every federal title record. [S3], [S14]

First check whether NARA publishes a name index to cancelled, rejected or relinquished entries for the land office and period. The National Archives at Kansas City publishes two: the Dodge City and Topeka, Kansas offices (ca. 1905–1937) and the Alliance, Nebraska office (ca. 1869–1950). They list names and dates and need no legal description. To get a hit, request the file from the Kansas City reference staff, as those index pages direct.

If no index covers the office and period, or it has no hit, get a section-township-range description from one of these:

- **Newspaper legal notices:** final-proof (intent-to-prove) notices, contests and patent-ready notices, usually in the county's official paper. Final-proof notices ran weekly for several weeks so that adverse claimants could come forward. [S7], [S3] Search for them with the [newspaper recovery recipe](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/newspapers-com-search-recovery.md). A contest notice points to a disputed entry. It does not show that the claim failed. [S7]
- **A GLO location search** on that description. In Smith's Kansas example, the claimant was missing from the patentees in his section, yet his entry fit "with no conflict". The gap was itself the clue. [S3] The GLO site also has survey plats, field notes and land status records (Master Title Plats) for that location. [S4]
- **A census civil township**, converted to township and range with a historical county atlas. [S3]

## 5. Read the tract book, not just the target line

Tract books record every transaction on a tract until the government transfers title, including unsuccessful entries. Later transfers are in county deed records. [S3]

1. Use the tract-book coverage table on the FamilySearch wiki to choose the state or territory and the volume. If the table lists several candidate volumes, browse all of them and expect to search. In Smith's Dakota example, the volume the table gave and the volume where the entry turned up were different (as narrated). [S3]
2. Log every entry on the tract in order, with its file number and any cancellation or relinquishment notation. In Smith's first example the person was the fourth claimant on one homestead. [S3]
3. Read several pages before and after the hit. Smith found a second, cancelled claim by the same man this way, under a different file number. That is a second case file to request. [S3]
4. Note relatives and associates on nearby tracts. Smith found two brothers-in-law close by. Their application file numbers were in the tract book even though they later left. [S3]

## 6. Decide whether a no-description search is worth running

Check any applicable NARA cancelled-entry name index (§4) first. Without a legal description, Smith calls this search time-consuming and usually unsuccessful. Run it only if all of the following hold [S3]:

- One county is established as the residence by a census, marriage or newspaper record.
- The date fits the entry sought: after 1862 (the Homestead Act) for an incomplete homestead application; for a credit entry, before about 1820; for a cash entry, from 1820 on (NARA's periods).
- The county was being settled at that time.
- The person (for example an immigrant or brick-wall ancestor) justifies the cost.

If you go ahead, convert the census township to township and range, choose the volume from the coverage table and read it page by page. Record every page you inspect, so that an unsuccessful run states exactly what it covered. [S3] Do not search a whole territory.

## 7. Request and inventory the RG 49 case file

Copy the identifier the NARA request type needs, exactly as GLO or the tract book gives it. For a pre-1908 file, give the state, land office, authority and patent number (GLO's Document Nr.). For a serial file (issue date July 1908 or later), give the serial patent number (GLO's Accession Nr.). For a warrant entry, the request needs the act, the acreage and the warrant number. Then request the case file from NARA. These files cover all 30 federal public-land states. [S4] Tanner points to NARA General Information Leaflet 67 (*Research in the Land Entry Files of the General Land Office*). [S4] NARA has replaced that leaflet with Reference Information Paper 114, and its "Accessing Land Entry Files" page explains current access.

Most case files are not digitized, but some are. Before ordering, check the homestead sets that NARA lists as online: Ancestry (Homestead Records, 1863–1908), Fold3 (Homestead Records, Nebraska) and FamilySearch (Homestead Final Certificates, 1863–1909). Serial patent files from the early 1960s onward are often at NARA regional facilities rather than in Washington. [S4], [S5]

Inventory the file one document at a time instead of summarizing it. Contents vary. Some files hold only a receipt and the patent, and others are much fuller. [S5] Look for:

- Application and proof-up papers, which may give an immigrant's original name, place of origin or family arrangements. [S4]
- Naturalization papers. The 1862 Homestead Act let citizens and people who had filed a declaration of intention make an entry, but citizenship was required by final proof. That makes a declaration or a citizenship certificate a likely item in the packet. [S6] If it names a court, look for the declaration or petition in that court. [S6] Take the court lead to [U.S. immigration search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/usa-immigration-search-recovery.md).
- For warrant entries: the surrendered warrant and any signed assignment on its back. [S1]

Each item is a lead with its own locator. A name or place of origin given in a land file still has to be correlated with independent records.

## 8. Optional: neighbouring patentees as FAN candidates

Run a GLO location search on the entry's township and range, and on the adjacent sections (each about one square mile), to list nearby patentees. [S11] Treat them as candidates for the friends, associates and neighbours (FAN) cluster, not as residents. The presenter's own ancestor owned land in Minnesota as a speculator and never lived there. [S11]

## Return packet

- An entry-routing row for each entry, including cancelled and relinquished ones.
- A party-role table.
- A tract-book observation log: volume, pages read, neighbouring entries and file numbers.
- Separate locators for the warrant application (RG 15) and the RG 49 entry file, with request status and whether an online copy was checked.
- A case-file inventory with leads (court, original name, origin).
- Explicit gaps: no legal description, volumes not yet browsed, file not yet ordered, assignee identity unresolved.
