# Read a memorial card or coffin plate kept by the family

Use this recipe when the user holds, or shows an image of, a funeral, mourning, memorial or prayer card, or a coffin (casket) plate, from a family collection. For an object made for a death, transcribe what it states, weigh each statement by when and by whom it was supplied, and turn its names and places into leads to the records of that death. The object points to those records; it does not replace them.

Neighbouring steps belong to sibling recipes. Capture both faces and the item's place in the collection with [family collection intake](family-collection-intake-and-capture.md). Read a funeral guest book with [family papers and Bible register dating](family-papers-and-bible-register-dating.md#address-books-and-card-lists) and [burial evidence packet](burial-evidence-packet.md#4-carry-the-hit-to-the-plot-and-cemetery-records). The burial evidence packet also takes an undertaker, church or cemetery lead to its records, and any request to a funeral home, church or cemetery goes through its privacy checkpoint. Send identity rulings and conflicting dates to [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## What goes wrong

- Entering a birth date or birthplace from a card or plate, or a birth date computed from its age, as if it came from a birth record. A card or plate was made at a death. Its birth details were supplied then, by someone other than the person born, and are not a birth record.
- Reading a coffin plate as evidence of where, or whether, the person was buried. A coffin plate kept by the family is not burial evidence.
- Reading a missing spouse or child on a card as evidence that none existed. Gorr finds family names on cards only occasionally. [S1]
- Assigning a plate that names nobody to a particular child or parent from family tradition alone.
- Copying the survivors named on a recent card into a shared tree. Treat them as living and keep their names in the private research log.

## 1. Read a memorial or funeral card

**Recognize the card.** Use a black border as a clue when sorting Victorian family papers. Check small single cards, open folded cards to read the inside, and inspect larger pieces mounted for display [S14] [S16]. Use the text to identify the card's purpose.

**Invitation or keepsake.** Use the wording to tell an invitation from a memorial keepsake, the two uses Robbie Gorr describes within the available excerpt [S12]. On an invitation, log the advertised service date, time and venue and any burial arrangements as plans [S13] [S16]. On a memorial keepsake, extract the decedent's birth and death dates and places and any named survivors [S16]. Read the wording around any service or burial details to tell whether the card announces or recalls the event [S13] [S14].

**Mass and prayer cards.** Check for wording dedicating the card to the deceased. Read a personalized Catholic Mass or prayer card for the decedent's dates and other particulars. A saint's name or image by itself gives no identity for the deceased [S15] [S16].

Transcribe both faces in the language on the card. Give each statement its own field, including the person’s name, dates, places, age, relatives, prayers and any printer’s or undertaker’s line [S1]. On the two French cards for Sister Louis-Bertrand and Emilia Perron in Fall River, the fronts carry portraits, ages and death details, while the backs carry prayers [S3]. On James Ingram’s card (London Museum 93.160/26), transcribe the death as 3 February 1862, age ten, and the stated burial place as Abney Park Cemetery. Look for his entry in the cemetery’s register. The undertaker’s line gives a separate lead to J. Dix senior at 3 Aylesbury Street, Clerkenwell Green [S4].

**Undertaker's line.** Copy the firm's name and address and the card's date exactly as printed. Undertakers used such cards to advertise and often supplied them free [S4], so take the named firm as the first candidate for the funeral home's file, and follow the funeral-home route in burial evidence packet.

**Burial place and church.** A cemetery or church named on the card leads to its burial or interment register [S1]. The card is not that entry: find the entry before treating the burial as established.

**Weight of each statement.** Log the death date and place as information supplied at or soon after the death. Log any birth date, birthplace or age as information supplied at the death about an earlier event, and look for a birth or baptism record before relying on it. A relative named with a relationship ("wife of …") is a lead to that relationship, to be tested in records made for it.

## 2. Read a coffin plate

**Identify the object.** Record the plate’s material, inscription and how it came into the collection. Andover’s account describes a custom particularly common in the northeastern United States from the 1840s, in which families took plates home after the funeral [S6]. For Frederick J. E. Tichborne’s Ontario plate of 1891 (Huron County Museum M972.0007.001), the museum describes removal before burial as likely [S2]. Keep that qualification when citing the object. To establish when a family acquired its own plate, look for a dated photograph, note or accession record that actually describes the transfer.

**Look beyond the boxes.** Check framed pieces and objects kept on mantelpieces and tables as well as stored keepsakes: Gorr notes that plates were displayed at home in a frame or on a mantelpiece or table [S1].

**Read the plate.** Copy the name, date of death and every unit of age. Frederick J. E. Tichborne’s Huron plate gives 27 February 1891 and an age of 6 months and 28 days [S2]. The Andover catalogue lists Betsey Jones as 80 years and 5 months (1951.002.2), and Eliza Hathaway Jones as 20 months and 19 days (1951.002.3) [S6]. Retain Betsey’s stated precision when estimating her birth. For Eliza, subtract 20 calendar months and 19 days from 28 April 1862 to get 9 August 1860. Record that date as calculated and test it against a birth or baptism entry.

**A plate that names nobody.** Gorr reports plates engraved only with an endearment such as "Our Darling" or "Baby" [S1]; no independent source opened for this recipe confirms it. Keep such a plate as an unidentified death in the family until a death, burial or cemetery record of a fitting date names the person, and do not assign it from family tradition alone.

**Burial and provenance.** Compare the inscription with the catalogue’s account of recovery. Catherine Mary Harriet Saunderson’s lead plate gives her death as 2 May 1828 at nine months. London Museum records that it was removed from a coffin buried at St Pancras Churchyard (NN3661) [S5]. The churchyard comes from that recovery history. If neither a recovery record nor a family account survives for your plate, leave the route into the collection open and search the burial register independently.

## Return packet

- One transcription per object: both faces and any inner panels, each statement in its own field as printed, and the object's reference and place in the collection.
- Advertised funeral and burial arrangements marked as planned.
- A lead list: undertaker (name, address and date as printed), church and cemetery, each with the object as its locator, routed to burial evidence packet.
- Derived values marked as derived, with the arithmetic.
- Open items: cards or plates that name nobody, plates of unknown provenance, and birth details without a birth or baptism record.
