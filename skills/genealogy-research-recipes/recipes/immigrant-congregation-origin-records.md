# Find an immigrant's hometown in U.S. congregation records

Use this recipe when U.S. civil, census, passenger or naturalization records name only a country or region ("Germany", "Prussia", "Bavaria") and the family's U.S. residence is known. The recipe has three parts. First, choose candidate congregations by language community, dates and address. Then work each candidate's registers past index misses to the original image. If the registers have not been filmed, trace the congregation's present-day successor or custodian.

For German-speaking Protestant immigrants, the main finding aid is Roger P. Minert's abstract series *German Immigrants in American Church Records* (GIACR). It is published in state volumes, such as the Indiana volume Everett uses. Each volume has a name index at the back and a table of contents arranged by place and congregation. The rest of this recipe calls it "the series". [S1], [S2], [S3]

## Mistakes this recipe prevents

- Searching only the church nearest the census address, then treating a miss there as absence.
- Ignoring ethnic or national parishes, and the dates when congregations were founded or split.
- Trusting a miss in an abstract's name index, or stopping at the abstract.
- Assuming the congregation's historical name, and its custodian, are still current.
- Expecting every register type and every confession to give a hometown.
- Filing one hometown statement as proven identity.

## 1. Fix residence and context before choosing a church

1. Date each U.S. residence from the census and city directories. Record the street address where you have it. The state tells you which volumes of the series to open. A state can span several volumes, so open every volume that covers the town. The town and address tell you which congregations to test in step 2. [S1]
2. Gather context from other sources and family records:
   - spelling variants
   - spouse, children, siblings and parents, with approximate birth years
   - religion
   [S1]
3. Keep this context in a list. In step 4 you will need it to recognise an entry that the index missed.

## 2. Build a candidate congregation table, not a nearest-church guess

Do not use distance to choose the congregation. Allen describes researchers who searched for hours in a Polish church a few doors from an Italian family's home. The family had worshipped about three miles away, in a church that used their language. [S5], [S6]

For each place and date, list every congregation that could have served the family. For each one, record:

- **Language or ethnic community served.** Ethnic or national parishes can be several miles from the household.
- **Founding date.** The congregation must have existed before the event. Castillo narrowed a Manhattan search to two German-serving parishes, then to the only one founded early enough. [S4]
- **Splits and offshoots, dated against the event.** Allen describes Polish members who attended a German church until a new congregation formed. The family could be in either set of registers, depending on the event date. [S5], [S6]
- **Address fit.** Castillo's choice also matched the family's neighbourhood in the city directory. Use the address only after the language and date filters have narrowed the list. [S4]
- **Custodian or successor**, filled in during step 5.

**Catholic cases:** list the diocese's parishes and other Catholic institutions from the printed historical volumes of the *Official Catholic Directory*. The series began in 1817 as the *Laity's Directory to the Church Service*. It did not appear every year at first (1817, 1822, then from 1833), and the *Official Catholic Directory* title dates from 1912. No volume exists for 1818–1821 or 1823–1832, so for those years build the parish list from other diocesan sources. In her 2017 talk, Castillo said the printed volumes carry historical parish information that the online version lacks. [S4]

**Places with no resident clergy:** visiting or circuit clergy may have kept the entries. Find out whom they reported to and where their journals or logs are now held. [S4]

Each candidate stays open until its relevant years have been searched. Otherwise it goes on the not-searched list with a reason.

## 3. Search every register type and every relative

In each candidate congregation, search the immigrant and their relatives. Also search neighbouring congregations, and places where siblings, children or parents settled. A relative's entry may be the one that names the hometown. [S1]

Search these register types:

- **Baptisms of U.S.-born children.** These can give each parent's birthplace. Minert estimates that about 20% of baptism entries do so. [S2], [S3]
- **Confirmations.** Minert's examples give the exact birth date and hometown. [S2], [S3]
- **Marriages.** Read each entry for the bride's and groom's stated origins. That is the main thing to take from a marriage entry. Minert's 1875 example names both home towns or districts, which is enough to go to the German parishes, but it gives no birth dates or ages. Where an entry gives no age, estimate birth years from the marriage date, as Minert does, and label them estimated. [S2], [S3]
- **Membership or soul registers.** [S1]
- **Deaths and burials.** Minert's 1882 example gives a birthplace and an age at death. Check each entry for a stated origin and age. Use any age only to calculate an estimated birth date, and label it as calculated. [S2], [S3]

Set expectations by confession and period:

- The series covers Protestant congregations. Minert says this means "any kind of Protestant", and the publisher's listings include Lutheran, Evangelical, Reformed and German Methodist congregations. [S3]
- Minert reports that German Catholics often joined existing Irish- or French-dominated parishes. Those registers rarely name a German hometown and usually give only a Latin country term. A miss in such a register says nothing about where the family came from. Go to the German-language congregations of relatives, other register types, or other jurisdictions. [S3]
- For pre-Revolutionary Pennsylvania Germans, Minert says records rarely give a hometown. He calls the usual "from the Palatinate" label usually wrong. Record that label as an unverified regional attribution, and keep looking for an entry that names a town. [S3]

## 4. Get past index misses to the original image

Minert says the series includes only people his team could show were born in Europe. A U.S.-born child is not indexed under their own name, so search the immigrant parents' names. [S3]

When the name index of a series volume misses, do not stop there:

1. Open the table of contents at the place and congregation of known residence.
2. Browse every entry, matching on the context from step 1 (given names, birth years, spouse, children).
3. Expect index errors. Everett estimates the series is about 99.9% accurate, yet in his case study an "n" was read as "u", so the family's surname was missing from the index. Browse even when the index shows nothing. [S1]

Next, find the heading of that congregation's section, which may be many pages earlier. Copy its U.S. place, county and state, and the source reference it gives for the original. Everett describes this reference as a FamilySearch film number or an Ancestry reference. At least one section instead cites a denominational archive microfilm (ELCA), so copy the reference as printed. [S1]

Then inspect the original image for three things:

- the correct spelling
- details the abstract leaves out (Everett's original death entry added widowhood, a birth date, birthplace and residence details)
- other entries for the same family [S1]

Keep three locators separate and linked: the abstract page, the film, archive or Ancestry reference, and the image locator. For catalogue and film navigation, use [FamilySearch workflows](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/familysearch-practical-record-workflows.md) or [Ancestry search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/ancestry-record-search-recovery.md).

## 5. If the registers are not filmed, trace the successor and custodian

Everett says that some sections were abstracted from books held by a local church or church archive. For those sections, the original can only be seen at that local church or archive. If the section heading gives no film or Ancestry reference, request the original from its custodian. [S1]

1. **Find the present-day successor.** Do not assume the historical name still exists. Minert describes an Illinois "German Evangelical Lutheran" church that is now Trinity Lutheran. He placed the renaming around World War I, possibly as early as 1900. [S3]
2. **Check denominational archives and their microfilm.** Minert says the LCMS and ELCA archives let him copy congregation records. [S3]
3. **Contact the congregation directly.** Ask whether its books are digitised and whether staff can search for a specific entry. [S3]
4. **Log each request:** the custodian asked, the date, what was requested, and the answer.

## Outputs and limits

Return three things:

1. **Candidate congregation table:** language community, founding and split dates, address fit, and custodian or successor.
2. **Entry log by register type:** abstract locator, film, archive or Ancestry reference, image locator, literal stated origin place, and the person whose entry it is.
3. **Not-searched or inaccessible list:** each congregation or register left unsearched, with the reason.

Limits:

- One hometown statement in one entry is a lead. Before filing identity, correlate it with the rest of the family's evidence and with origin-side records.
- Minert says the series does not confirm entries against church records in Germany. [S3]
