# Recover a U.S. federal court case file

Use when a newspaper item, family paper or other lead suggests an ancestor was in a federal
lawsuit, criminal case, bankruptcy or appeal. The task is to take that lead to the right federal
court for the period, then to the National Archives record group and its index, and to request
the loose case file and the related series. Naturalization has its own court table and index
route in [immigration search recovery § 9](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/usa-immigration-search-recovery.md),
so use that recipe for it. To build the newspaper lead, use [newspaper search recovery](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/newspapers-com-search-recovery.md).
To transcribe the papers with an AI tool, use [AI for documentary records](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/ai-for-documentary-records.md).
Identity rulings belong in [evidence/proof judgment](https://github.com/Kinprove/genealogy-research-recipes/blob/8b235c47acf62b78cd5a1ccf85f4eb974b2f966a/skills/genealogy-research-recipes/recipes/evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## Classify the matter before choosing where to look

Decide whether the matter is federal before you search a county courthouse or a large genealogy
database. Under current law, bankruptcy is a federal matter: James Tanner says the federal
government "has preempted all bankruptcy", so "now it's a federal action" that cannot be filed in
a local court [S1]. He also says these files generally are not in Ancestry, FamilySearch or other
large genealogy databases. In his account they are held by the National Archives or other
repositories and may be closed or hard to reach [S1].

Tanner describes the present position. Do not assume it held on every historical date. Before
you treat a county search as the wrong place, check whether a federal bankruptcy act was in
force on the date of the lead. The presenter's 1867 bankruptcy [S2] falls under the Bankruptcy
Act of 1867. If a federal act applied, a failed bankruptcy search in county probate, civil indexes
or a large database is a search of the wrong place, not a negative result. If no federal act
applied on that date, the county search is not ruled out.

Record the lead's literal wording: the person, the other parties, the date, the city where the
court sat and the type of action. A report that someone "appeared in court" in a city with a
federal court is not enough by itself. Confirm from the lead or the court's own records that the
court was federal. In the teaching example, a newspaper report of a court appearance in Pittsburgh
led to a suit in the federal Western District of Pennsylvania [S3].

## Pick the court for the date and follow an appeal elsewhere

The federal courts had four levels [S2]:

- **District courts** were the trial courts. Their jurisdiction included admiralty, equity,
  bankruptcy and naturalization. Their start dates vary by area; the presenter ties each to the
  creation of its state [S2]. Find the creation date for the district that covers the lead's place.
- **Circuit courts** were established in 1789 and exercised appellate jurisdiction until 1891,
  when it passed to the circuit courts of appeals. The presenter says they also heard federal
  matters, especially criminal ones [S2]. They were abolished by the Judicial Code of 1911,
  effective 1 January 1912, and their jurisdiction, records and pending cases passed to the
  district courts.
- **Circuit courts of appeals** were established in 1891 and heard appeals from both district and
  circuit courts.
- **The Supreme Court** was established in 1789.

For a matter before 1912, search both district and circuit court records unless the lead names
the court. An appeal does not stay with the trial file. Route it by the court that heard the
appeal on its date. Before 1891 the circuit courts heard appeals themselves, and those records
are in RG 21. From 1891 the circuit courts of appeals heard them, and their records are in
RG 276. Appeals to the Supreme Court are in RG 267. The circuit courts of appeals had different
geographic jurisdictions from the trial courts [S2], so an appeal from 1891 on may be held for
another state. Look up the appellate circuit that covered the trial district on the appeal date
before you name a custodian.

## Map the court to its record group and custodian

Use this crosswalk [S2]:

- **RG 21:** U.S. district and circuit courts.
- **RG 276:** U.S. courts of appeals.
- **RG 267:** U.S. Supreme Court.
- **RG 123:** U.S. Court of Claims.
- **RG 578:** U.S. bankruptcy courts. Bankruptcy case files are in RG 21 or RG 578.

The record group number is stable. What you must look up each time is the NARA facility that
holds the specific district's records, because it is often not in the city where the court sat.
Western District of Pennsylvania records are held at NARA's Philadelphia facility, not in
Pittsburgh. That is where the teaching file, a suit, came from [S3]. Bankruptcy is the
exception: NARA now holds all bankruptcy case files at the National Archives at Kansas City, in
RG 21 or RG 578. The presenter's Philadelphia bankruptcy retrieval [S2] shows where the file was
then, not where it is now. Treat the bankruptcy index as a separate request: NARA's RG 21
description lists the W.D. Pa. Pittsburgh Division bankruptcy indexes and docket books among the
Philadelphia textual records. Ask Philadelphia for the index first, then request the case file
from Kansas City with the reference copied from it.

Expect to work mostly with originals. The presenter says very little of this material was filmed
[S2]. NARA does have a microfilm catalog for federal court records, and some RG 267 series are on
film, so check that catalog before you plan a visit.

## Search the index first, then request the file

Do not ask for a case file by name. The presenter could not get a bankruptcy file in Philadelphia
just by naming the person. He went through the finding aids, requested the index books, found the
name, copied the file reference and then requested the file [S2]. Do the same:

1. Find the record group's finding aid and identify the index or docket that covers the court,
   the type of action and the period. Check the dates covered: for the Western District of
   Pennsylvania, Pittsburgh Division, NARA's RG 21 description lists bankruptcy indexes for
   1898–1947 and 1969–80 and docket books for 1939–79. A date outside an index's span needs
   another series.
2. Search it, and copy the exact entry: case number, term, volume and page, and the spelling as
   written.
3. Request the case file with that reference.
4. If the index and the file are in different record groups, make a separate request for each.
   The presenter reported in 2019 that different record groups were pulled at separate pull
   times [S2]. Ask the facility for its current retrieval schedule.

Book before you go. The presenter's 2019 description of walk-in, same-day service is out of date
for Philadelphia. Its research room is now open by appointment only, Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to
4 p.m. Book through <philadelphia.archives@nara.gov> or 215-305-2044. For any other facility,
check its current page for appointment and remote-request rules.

## Inventory every series, not just the docket

A docket entry tells you that a case existed. It does not hold the case papers. List each series
that applies and mark it retrieved, requested, not found or not yet searched [S2]:

- **Docket** (court calendar): lists the cases heard.
- **Minutes**: the clerk's brief daily record of court actions, such as appeals or appointments.
- **Orders**: specific judgments, for example an order granting citizenship.
- **Briefs**: the parties' written arguments.
- **Mandates**: the presenter describes these as a criminal defendant's undertaking in exchange
  for a suspended sentence [S2]. Do not rely on that as a definition. Read the series description
  in the finding aid and record what the mandate series for your court actually contains.
- **Loose case file**: the largest body of papers, sometimes tied in a bundle.

Keep each series' locator separate. In the teaching example, the loose file was handwritten on
lined paper and covered a suit that started in the 1870s and ran until 1881 [S3]. Note the full
span of dates in the papers rather than only the filing date.

## Record a complete-index negative, then widen to relatives

If the expected person is not in an index you searched from beginning to end, record the index
title, the court, the date range covered and the spellings you searched. That is a bounded
negative. It does not show that no family member was involved. Search the same index for every
relative living in the same period. The presenter found no entry for the ancestor named in the
newspaper lead. He then found a bankruptcy for a different ancestor, a farmer who had briefly run
a general store [S2]. The file you find may belong to a different family member than the lead
suggested. Keep the new person's identity open until it is correlated through the proof recipe.

## Turn schedules and parties into local leads

From the case papers, take out each named person or business and its role. Keep each entry as a
separate lead, not as a conclusion:

- **Bankruptcy schedules:** extract each creditor's name and street address, the amount owed and
  the goods the debt was for. The presenter's schedule listed page after page of wholesale
  creditors and a total of almost $10,000 in debt in 1867 [S2].
- **Suits:** record each co-defendant and plaintiff. The teaching suit named a co-defendant and a
  sewing-machine company as plaintiff [S3]. Also record any sureties or guarantors that appear in
  the papers.

Use these entries to look for local records. The presenter found a business licence listed in a
local newspaper, which gave the township where the store stood [S2]. Each associate gets a
separate search. A creditor or co-defendant is not a relative unless other evidence shows it.

## Close with a case packet

Return:

- the court and record group, the holding facility, and how the facility was confirmed;
- the index citation exactly as copied, the index's covered dates, and the case-file locator;
- the series inventory with the status of each series;
- any complete-index negative with its scope, and the relatives searched in that index;
- the extracted creditors, parties, sureties, amounts and goods, each marked as a lead.

If access is closed or the file is not found, the index entry is still a lead. Name the next
series or custodian to try.
