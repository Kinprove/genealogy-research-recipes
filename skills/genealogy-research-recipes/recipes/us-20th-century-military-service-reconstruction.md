# Rebuild a 20th-century U.S. Army or Air Force service record after file loss or restriction

Use this when a 20th-century Army or Air Force personnel file burned, came back thin or is restricted, or when a family says a man served but he does not appear in federal lists. The task is to rebuild his service from records held separately from that file, and to log each record's custodian and scope. For older compiled service records, pension files and choosing a custodian, use [military pension file recovery § 1](us-military-pension-file-recovery.md). For the grave and cemetery-record packet, use [burial evidence packet](burial-evidence-packet.md). For naturalization C-files, use [U.S. immigration search recovery](usa-immigration-search-recovery.md). Identity and proof rulings belong in [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## Find out the file's actual status before saying "it burned"

Do not claim that the records burned and nothing survives. On July 12, 1973 a fire at the National Personnel Records Center (NPRC) in St. Louis destroyed about 16–18 million Official Military Personnel Files (OMPFs). NARA gives these losses ([NARA, 1973 fire](https://www.archives.gov/personnel-records-center/fire-1973); presenter account in [S11], [S12]):

- Army: about 80% of files lost for men discharged November 1, 1912 – January 1, 1960.
- Air Force: about 75% of files lost for discharges September 25, 1947 – January 1, 1964, but only for names alphabetically after "Hubbard, James E."
- Navy, Marine Corps and Coast Guard: NARA's fire page lists no losses for these branches. Strauss calls them largely unaffected [S11].

A discharge date inside a range means the file *may* be lost, not that it is lost. These are exactly the ranges for which NPRC keeps a burned ("B") file registry of about 6.5 million recovered records and a reconstructed ("R") file registry ([NARA](https://www.archives.gov/personnel-records-center/fire-1973)). Request the file anyway.

Then check access. OMPFs become archival and open to the public 62 years after separation, on a rolling basis. Before that, full access is limited to the veteran or next of kin ([NARA, OMPF access](https://www.archives.gov/personnel-records-center/ompf-access)). Work out the cutoff from the discharge date. Do not reuse the presenter's example year [S11].

An archival OMPF is open to the public [H22 @2026-11-19]. For access beyond the releasable information in a non-archival file, a third party needs signed, dated authorization from the living veteran or, if deceased, the eligible next of kin with proof of death. NARA defines eligible kin as an unremarried surviving spouse, parent, child or sibling [H3 @2026-11-19]. A grandchild requesting the full non-archival file therefore needs authorization unless acting in another eligible capacity.

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> A personnel-file request sends the veteran's identity and, for a non-archival file, a signed authorization and any proof of death; the veteran may be living, so answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before sending it to NPRC.

For a Navy veteran who stayed in the Naval Reserve after active duty, use the final Reserve separation date for the rolling archival cutoff. Keep any later civilian shipyard retirement out of that calculation. Use the separation question from the available excerpt to identify which date needs proof [S128 @2026-11-23]. Recheck a refusal based on non-archival status and use the archival request route once the file qualifies [S129 @2026-11-23], [S147 @2026-11-23].

For proof of death, send one of NARA's examples: a copy of the death certificate, a letter from the funeral home or a published obituary [H3 @2026-11-16], [S42 @2026-11-16].

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: contact --> Seeking the authorization for the rest of a non-archival file means asking the veteran, who may be living, or his next of kin to sign a release for the user's request; answer the privacy gate and follow Living people in `SKILL.md` before contacting them.

Without the veteran's or next of kin's consent, NPRC releases only limited information from a non-archival file [S43 @2026-11-16], [S42 @2026-11-16]. NPRC's FOIA page gives examples of what may be released: name, service number, dates and branch of service, final duty status, final rank, assignments and geographical locations, military education level, award eligibility (not the medals), photograph, court-martial trial transcripts, and place of entrance and separation. For a deceased veteran it adds place of birth, date and place of death, and place of burial [S44 @2026-11-16]. Log such a reply as a limited release, not as the file and not as a thin file. Use its assignments and dates to choose unit records, and seek the authorization above for the rest.

Request routes: NARA's online request system, eVetRecs ([vetrecs.archives.gov](https://vetrecs.archives.gov/)); Standard Form 180 (SF-180) by mail; an in-person visit to St. Louis by appointment; or an independent researcher ([NARA](https://www.archives.gov/personnel-records-center/military-personnel/morning-reports-and-unit-rosters); [S11]). eVetRecs is not limited to the family. A requester who is neither the veteran nor his next of kin chooses "Other", and every user verifies identity through ID.me ([NARA, eVetRecs help](https://www.archives.gov/veterans/military-service-records/evetrecs-help.html)).

Before submitting the OMPF request to NPRC, gather the full name used in service, branch, service dates and the service number or Social Security number required for the request. When the service number is unknown, have the birth date and birthplace ready. For a file that may have burned, use the three identifiers from the available excerpt [S125 @2026-11-22]. Add the place of discharge, last assigned unit and place of entry if known. Use the request above even if the file’s fate is unresolved [S82 @2026-11-22], [S83 @2026-11-22].

Check a reported fire date and repository against NARA’s description: the fire was at NPRC in St. Louis on 12 July 1973. A presentation placed it in 1979 [S16], [H2]. Use the actual discharge date and affected branch to assess a possible loss.

## Request each file product by name and log what comes back

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> Each product request concerns a veteran who may be living, and a returned pay roll can name other men of his unit; answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before sending it.

Name each product in the request, and log the product returned and its page count [S9], [S10]:

- **OMPF:** the personnel file itself.
- **Burnt (B) file:** a file that survived with fire or water damage, listed in NPRC's B-file registry. The presenter says the custodian may scan and email it rather than show the original [S9].
- **Reconstructed (R) file:** check the sources used in the reconstruction, such as a VA claim, state record, hospital record, final pay voucher or a discharge copy supplied by the veteran [H2], [S9]. Record the documents and page count actually returned.
- **Auxiliary records:** if the personnel file is missing, ask which auxiliary series cover the branch and period. A final pay voucher can give the service number, dates and character of service [H5]. A final pay roll instead may list the veteran alongside others from the unit [S11]. Transcribe any last unit, duty station, pay and overseas status as recorded for that period [S9]. Log the product under the custodian’s label.

- **Officer pay card:** for an Army officer, also ask for his pay card. NARA St. Louis holds Army officer pay cards for 1917–1921 and 1940–1951 [H5 @2026-11-16], [S42 @2026-11-16]. A WWII officer's card can give his serial number, grade, branch, dependents, date he entered and place he was ordered to active duty, years of service, mailing address, prior enlisted service, promotions, and the date and authority of his relief from active duty. These are archival records: NARA takes written requests by mail, fax or email and needs no veteran's or next-of-kin signature for copies [S45 @2026-11-16].
- **Other auxiliary series:** NARA St. Louis also lists Army Surgeon General's Office admission records (about 7.8 million hospital or treatment-facility admissions: 1942–1945 for the Army and Army Air Corps, and Army records listed as "1950 and 1954") and VA index cards for 1917 to 19 September 1940 and for 20 September 1940 to January 1972 [H5 @2026-11-16], [S42 @2026-11-16]. Its holdings page gives no access route for these series, only "Check back later for more information". Name each series in the auxiliary request, log each reply as its own product, and keep an unanswered series open. An admission record shows health status: treat it as sensitive data under the privacy gate. The earlier VA cards' span matches the VA Master Index on FamilySearch (below); search there first.

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: third-party-tool --> Ancestry receives the veteran’s name in a search of the Army deserter pay cards. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before entering it.

For a documented WWII Army desertion lead in 1943–1945, search *U.S., WWII Army Deserters Pay Cards, 1943–1945* (Ancestry collection 61993). Check that the collection opens in your account before logging an absence [S82 @2026-11-22], [S86 @2026-11-22].

If the collection is unavailable, use the series title and NAID 4713145 to locate the NARA description. Copy the service number, organization and date from the card. Use them to pursue any trial record and verify the final disposition independently of the pay entry [S85].

Before choosing final pay as a substitute, distinguish a discharge from an in-service
death. NARA's 2023 presentation says a final pay voucher required discharge: a person
killed in service would have one only from an earlier break before the final reenlistment
[S27]. Preserve the evidence for that earlier separation and request the voucher for its
actual period; an in-service death alone does not supply that target. Use the separate
death-file route below. A missing voucher is not evidence of non-service.

A payroll entry dates the end of service and names the last unit. It is not a full service history. The recorded unit may be a separation unit rather than the operational unit [S27]. Log "no OMPF; auxiliary returned, 3 pp." rather than "file destroyed".

Copy the character of discharge exactly as written, and read the discharge papers for the stated reason [S30]. During WWII about 50,000 people received "blue" discharges, named for the paper, for "undesirable habits and traits of character". About 9,000 of them were men and women discharged because of their sexuality, and African American men were the largest group of recipients. The practice ended on 1 July 1947, when the undesirable discharge replaced the blue discharge and the general discharge was created [S41]. The VA's reading of the GI Bill barred blue-discharge recipients from veterans' benefits [S31], so a missing VA claim file can follow from the discharge type, not from non-service. A blue or other-than-honorable discharge is a lead to the reason for separation; it does not by itself establish his sexuality or any other reason.

Before closing a WWII Army decorations search, read the darkened pages of surviving
copies for award references and use any order number in the award-order search below
[H2], [S69]. One incomplete, darkened set of papers reportedly yielded a Good Conduct
Award reference [S69].

For possible Quaker affiliation, use the suggestion from the available excerpt of *Just
Genealogy* and check surviving military papers for a religion entry [S62].
For WWI, look for a burial-related entry on a service health form. For WWII, check the
personnel papers for a recorded religious preference [S63]. If the WWII Army papers include
a stateside Hospital Report of Death (WD AGO Form 52-9), check item 7 [S64]. Copy the entry
as written. A broad category or a blank field leaves Quaker membership unresolved.

## Request records that were never part of the OMPF

- **In-service deaths:** request the WWI burial case file (also called a mortuary or casualty file) or the WWII Individual Deceased Personnel File (IDPF) separately from the OMPF. Do not merge it with the OMPF in the register. Route the request by custodian:
  - WWI-era burial case files (1915–1939, RG 92) were held at the National Archives in College Park and moved to NARA St. Louis in 2012 [S25].

  - WWII Army IDPFs: search the National Archives Catalog for his file first, then ask NARA St. Louis, whatever his surname. NARA St. Louis lists IDPFs for 1939–1975 among its holdings [H6 @2026-11-19] and states that WWII Army IDPFs for surnames M–Z are open [H7 @2026-11-19]. The catalogue check on 7 November 2026 found digitized A–E surname files in the IDPF series [H23 @2026-11-19]. Older accounts split custody by surname between NARA and the Army, so do not route by surname alone. If NARA St. Louis says it does not hold the file, send a FOIA request to the U.S. Army Human Resources Command at Fort Knox, Kentucky. A 2025 History Hub reply gives the address and what to include [H24 @2026-11-19].

  - Expect cause and date of death, temporary and permanent burial, medical records and sometimes a burial-site map [S10].
- **VA claim file (VA C-file or XC-file):** request it by FOIA. It exists only if a benefits claim was made ([H8]; [S18], [S20], [S21]).

  - Reclaim The Records offers a fax-based FOIA request for a deceased veteran’s claim file and publishes information on appeals [H8 @2026-11-19], [H9 @2026-11-19].

  - Inspect the claim file for a separation document such as DD Form 214 or WD AGO Form 53-55. Copy its actual form number, unit and decorations separately from the claim decision [S18].
  - Log the submission and reply dates. One request’s turnaround does not predict another’s [S18].
  - Record whether the reply supplies copies or only a summary. Reclaim The Records reported a change to summary-only replies in July 2025 [H9]. Preserve any stated reason for withholding records and the appeal instructions [S21]. A withheld file remains distinct from a search that found no file.
  - Name: keep the VA C-file apart from the USCIS C-file, which shares the label [S21].

- **VA Master Index card (1917–1940):** NARA St. Louis holds the index, and FamilySearch offers a free searchable collection [H10 @2026-11-19]. Log the card separately from a VA claim file [S20 @2026-11-19].
- **BIRLS entry:** BIRLS (Beneficiary Identification Records Locator Subsystem) is a VA index of veterans and beneficiaries who claimed benefits, built from the 1980s on. Death does not create a BIRLS record. The public release covers only veterans the VA has confirmed as deceased ([H8 @2026-11-19]; [S17 @2026-11-19]). A BIRLS hit tells you a claim file may exist. Log it as its own product.

- **Compensation file:** take the custodian and series from the file’s heading or citation. Extract company, battalion or division and any dated engagements it actually lists [S17].
- **State bonus file:** many states paid their own WWI bonus or compensation. The application files are state records with a state custodian, separate from the OMPF. Search the archives of the veteran's home state [S29]:

  - Pennsylvania State Archives, RG 19, Series 19.91, *World War I Veterans Service and Compensation File, 1917–1919, 1934–1948*. It records residence, unit, service and overseas dates, discharge, and dependents' names and addresses. Images are on Ancestry, with free access for Pennsylvania residents through Ancestry Pennsylvania [H16 @2026-11-19], [H17 @2026-11-19], [H18 @2026-11-19].
  - The State Historical Society of Iowa’s WWI applications have a FamilySearch index [H19 @2026-11-19].
  - FamilySearch lists the Washington State Archives collection of veterans’ compensation applications [H20 @2026-11-19].
  - Use: the state holds these files, so they can supply a dated residence and service details when the OMPF burned in 1973. Cite the state custodian and series, not the federal one.

- **BIRLS entry:** Reclaim The Records describes each entry as giving name, birth and death dates, branch of service, and enlistment and release dates [S48]; MyHeritage's 2025 release of the same index, *United States, Deceased Veteran Benefit Claims*, described the same date fields [S47]. When the OMPF burned, use these dates to bound the auxiliary, unit-record and claim-file requests. Log them as an index entry, not as a discharge document.

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: third-party-tool --> FamilySearch receives the veteran’s name in the VA Master Index search. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before entering it.

If a VA Master Index search returns extracted details alone, follow the image lookup from the available excerpt [S113 @2026-11-22]. Use FamilySearch collection 2968245 to find the card and read its number prefixes and annotations [S96 @2026-11-22].

On a VA Master Index card, copy the birth and death dates, any address, rank, unit, branch and dates of entry and discharge as labeled. Keep the date or purpose of the address unresolved if the card supplies neither. Bound a miss in the 1917–1940 index by the benefits, pension or insurance activity it indexes for veterans or heirs in that claim window. Continue with another military record series after a miss [S95], [S96].

Use the comparison from the available excerpt [S113]. Match the WWI service card to the VA Master Index card on the service number where recorded and the birth, entry and discharge dates. If the service card and gravestone leave the death date unresolved, check the index card for a recorded date and corroborate it separately [S96], [S91].

Copy VA claim, service and insurance numbers with their prefixes. Use NARA’s *VA Master Index Codes* to select the right number for the record wanted. For an F-prefixed insurance file number, check the reverse for additional numbers [S97].

### A separate Army or Air Force court-martial trial file

Keep the trial record separate from personnel-file remarks. Gather the service, trial
year or bounded interval, court type, original outcome and any later appeal or discharge
upgrade. NARA's 2024 presentation describes these historical selection distinctions [S26]:

| Described Army/Air Force trial series | Selection described in the presentation |
| --- | --- |
| General and special, 1917–1938 | Included regardless of outcome |
| General, 1939–1976 | Separate trial-record series |
| Special, 1939–1976 | Proceedings resulting in a bad-conduct discharge |

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> A trial-file request names the defendant,
who may be living, and the file can name victims and witnesses; answer the privacy gate in
`SKILL.md` before requesting it.

The presentation places surviving summary-court details in the OMPF rather than a separate
trial series. Use these distinctions to select a candidate finding description, then
verify its actual period, custodian, survival and access before requesting it. They do
not establish that an individual's file survives or was destroyed. A later discharge
upgrade alone does not settle survival; preserve the original disposition and appeal dates.

For an early Army court-martial, check the 1894–1917 case files at the National Archives
in Washington, DC (RG 153, NAID 5916762) [S60 @2026-11-18]. Use the 1891–1917
index (RG 153, entry 17) to find a missing general court-martial case number
[S61 @2026-11-18]. For a trial in 1917, check
which series covers its date before choosing the lookup procedure.

For a trial-file search without a case number:

1. Preserve any case number already supplied; otherwise use the **defendant's surname**
   as the lookup lead. The presentation describes staff card indexes arranged by service
   and surname, not by the victim or witness [S26]. Check the custodian's present finder
   and request requirements rather than assuming the 2024 index format is unchanged.
2. Supply the service, all known names and aliases, including married/maiden names, and
   the trial date or bounded interval. Keep unit and trial location as additional clues
   when the date is uncertain. For a joint trial, one known defendant can supply the
   individual card lead; the presentation does not require every defendant's name [S26].
3. Retain any separately cited appeal case number as another retrieval target. Record
   the trial file requested, the actual file or personnel-file extract returned, its
   extent and unresolved references. Personnel remarks are not the trial transcript.

### Trace WWII captivity

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: third-party-tool --> MyHeritage or NARA’s AAD receives the prisoner’s name during the captivity search. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before entering it.

Search *US World War II Prisoners of War, 1941–1946* (MyHeritage collection 10080) or NARA’s *World War II Prisoners of War Data File* in AAD (RG 389, NAID 1263907). Match the serial number and branch or civilian status. Copy the parent unit, theater of capture, reporting date and camp as separate fields. Verify the collection opens and covers the event before recording a miss [S102 @2026-11-22], [S103 @2026-11-22], [S104 @2026-11-22], [S105 @2026-11-22].

Read an RG 389 entry as a War Department compilation made using copies of International Committee of the Red Cross reports. Keep the date reported distinct from the date of capture. Some people listed in the underlying reports or cables are absent from the compilation, so continue checking capture or internment records when a search misses [S102], [S104].

## Read the headstone application as a derivative summary

Retrieve the government headstone or marker application and read **both sides**. In the 1925–1949 series, search NARA M1916 for 1925–1941 and M2113 for 1941–1949 [H11]. A WWI application reportedly had assignments written on its reverse in an unidentified hand [S1], [S4], [S5], [S7]. Record them as leads for service records.

Treat any annotation as derivative information from an unknown informant:

- Copy the unit exactly as written.
- Use it to choose unit records.
- Do not file it as the service record.

The stone itself can supply the unit when nothing else names it [S5], [S6].

A missing entry in the Nationwide Gravesite Locator cannot exclude a VA marker. The VA says it lacks private-cemetery burial information from before 1997 [S68 @2026-11-19]. Record the search and check the cemetery, marker and headstone application directly [S5 @2026-11-19].

## Bound a WWII Army enlistment search in AAD before reading a miss

NARA's WWII Army Enlistment Records in the Access to Archival Databases (AAD) hold about 9 million Army enlistments from 1938–1946, including the Women's Army Auxiliary Corps and the Enlisted Reserve Corps. They exclude Army officers and every other service: Navy, Marine Corps and Coast Guard ([NARA, WWII electronic records](https://www.archives.gov/research/military/ww2/electronic-records.html); [NARA press release, 2004](https://www.archives.gov/press/press-releases/2004/nr04-64.html)). The series is the Electronic Army Serial Number Merged File, RG 64 ([NARA Catalog](https://catalog.archives.gov/id/1263923)). If his OMPF burned, search AAD first to test a claimed Army enlistment. A hit gives his Army serial number for the later requests.

The file was converted from punch-card microfilm. 212 rolls holding about 1.5 million cards were too dark to scan, and 160,390 records only flag that cards at that point were unreadable ([Prologue, 2006](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2006/spring/aad-ww2.html)). An AAD miss is therefore not evidence that he did not enlist.

- Coded fields, such as residence state and county, need AAD's code lists and field notes. Read the code from the list; do not guess it ([Prologue, 2006](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2006/spring/aad-ww2.html)).
- Tell same-name men apart by state of residence and year of birth. For a common surname, narrow the query by state of residence before reading the results.
- AAD can refuse automated fetches: an automated fetch got HTTP 403 on 2026-11-04. Do not rely on web search to find its record pages. Log a 403 or an empty web search as an access failure, not a no-record finding, and search AAD in a browser session.

Do not order the destroyed original punch cards as a fuller version of an AAD enlistment entry. NARA explains that the originals were destroyed after microfilming; the microfilm survives. Do not describe the AAD entry as a digital-only surviving record. [S55](https://www.pricegen.com/8127-2/), [S56](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2006/spring/aad-ww2.html)

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: third-party-tool --> NARA’s AAD receives the veteran’s name or Army serial number during the enlistment search. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before entering it.

In AAD’s *Electronic Army Serial Number Merged File*, select the Enlistment Records table for general WWII Army enlistments, including Women’s Army Auxiliary Corps entries. Select the separate Reserve Corps Records table for an Enlisted Reserve Corps lead. Verify the selected table opens before logging a miss [S82 @2026-11-22], [S84 @2026-11-22], [S102 @2026-11-22].

Search by Army serial number when you have it. In a name search, enter surname, given name and middle initial in that order, and omit apostrophes [S82 @2026-11-22], [S84 @2026-11-22]. Try both a space and no space after a surname prefix. For a wildcard search, use at least three known characters followed by an asterisk. If a damaged punch-card character is suspected, retry with a quoted name in which `#` replaces the suspect character. If the result has only an initial, keep the full middle name unresolved and check a separate record that spells it out [S124 @2026-11-22]. Use the identity check from the available excerpt [S126 @2026-11-22]. Match the recovered serial number to another service record before carrying it into a separate request. Carry the verified number into the VA file search suggested by the lookup from the available excerpt [S127 @2026-11-22].

Record the AAD result as an electronic extract of the Army’s microfilmed punch cards. Use the extraction fields from the available excerpt [S125]. Copy the enlistment date and place, grade, branch, term, education, civilian occupation and marital status as recorded at that event. Read an entry with a later enlistment date as a possible reenlistment, and check the source-of-personnel field before treating the date as first entry [S82], [S84], [S102].

## Test state and local routes before calling the claim unsupported

If federal lists miss a WWI veteran, check the state National Guard and follow any transfer or attachment across state lines. In one account, the family held an Army discharge for a man whose route through the Guard had eluded the researcher’s federal searches [S1], [S2].

Keep the original enlistment and later unit designations separate. The accounts disagree about Arizona and New Mexico. Check both Guard rosters and the reported Texas attachment before assigning an originating state [S3], [S4], [S5], [S6].

Search both states' Guard records. Search the unit's later federal designation, but keep the original designation as written [S6], [S7].

Request the locally recorded discharge even when the family has Army papers. A reported Apache County copy contained details absent from the federal documents [S2]. Compare each field and retain the county’s own volume and page. Also check local veterans’ records in the state of residence [S11].

Search school yearbooks for the war years as well as the graduation year [S32]. Bangor’s June 1944 yearbook named members of the graduating class in service without giving their units [S33]. An alumni roll can instead reach back across classes: a 1919 normal-school yearbook included students from as far back as 1892, with ranks and service locations [S34]. Verify any proposed service in a military record and confine a miss to the years and lists actually checked.

Search the state archive's WWI historical-project questionnaires and Gold Star files as a
separate product from the OMPF or federal burial file. Read the collection's own inclusion
rule first: Alabama's Gold Star project collected survivors' contributions about service
members who died, so a miss is not a general test of service. Use documented state ties,
including a supported collateral soldier or next of kin living elsewhere, to choose other
collections whose scope fits. Birthplace alone need not identify the right one; a relative's
residence does not itself establish eligibility. [S23, H1]

Inventory the supplied biography, questionnaire, letters, photographs and notification papers
rather than treating the result as a service abstract. Record who supplied each family
statement, keeping apparent or unknown informants uncertain, and carry names and addresses
as attributed leads. Where both an online preview and a downloadable packet are available,
compare their page/image extents and log omissions before calling the packet inspected.
Contents and access vary by collection; these state papers do not reconstruct a complete
service history. The Reclaiming Kin examples motivate the family-file and extent checks;
their historical site controls are not current access promises. [S23]

A draft registration shows a man was present when he registered, not that he served [S5], [S14]. The 1917 card asks about prior military service, and Strauss says Guard service was often noted [S14]. Read the whole card, but don't count it as service.

Before treating a WWI draft-card miss as evidence, calculate his age on the relevant registration date. Use the age check from the available excerpt of each walkthrough [S132], [S133]. For June 5, 1917, check ages 21–30 inclusive. For June 5, 1918, check men who turned 21 after the first registration. The August 24, 1918 supplement covered those who turned 21 after June 5 of that year. For September 12, 1918, check ages 18–45 inclusive, including men who were too young or too old in June 1917. Check the applicable exemptions, including prior registration for the September round, before expecting a card [S51], [S134], [S135].

## Place him with unit records without giving him the unit's events

An OMPF is primarily an administrative record. NARA states that it does not contain detailed information about the veteran's participation in battles and engagements [S46]. Do not read the file's silence about a battle as evidence that he was not there; take engagements from the unit records below and log them as unit context [S42].

For WWI Army unit research, search the Center of Military History's *Order of Battle of
the United States Land Forces in the World War* by title and volume. Use volume 3, part 2
for a U.S. post or camp and volumes 1–2 for American Expeditionary Forces (AEF)
organizations [S66]. The suggestion from the available excerpt is to search stateside
and overseas service separately [S65]. The Zone of the Interior includes U.S. possessions,
and its *Directory of Troops* (volume 3, part 3) also lists AEF units [S67]. Verify the
unit's location for the relevant period before drawing an inference from its inclusion.

For a WWI service card naming Camp Grant, Illinois, use the lookup from the available excerpt [S113]. Open the Camp Grant entry in *Order of Battle*, volume 3, part 2 (CMH Pub 23-4), and look for the exact organization in the station complement, divisional units and nondivisional units. The 86th Division’s “N. A.” stands for National Army. If the individual went overseas with that division, check his transfer entries and the receiving unit’s records. The division’s overseas account records a transfer of personnel for replacements, so the division’s history alone cannot place him in combat [S114], [S115].

With a service number and unit, get the morning reports and after-action reports covering his dates. Morning reports and muster rolls are unit records that were not part of the OMPF, and the fire did not touch them [S10], [S16].

Check whether the selected morning-report series and period record personnel changes rather
than a full roster. McComb describes entries for changes such as promotion, illness or
absence [S24]. Where that practice is confirmed, use an independently dated status change
to choose an initial, bounded run of the supported unit or company's reports. A name missing
on one day then does not show that the man was absent from the unit.

The series span alone does not establish that the needed monthly report is online. The 7 November 2026 check found monthly files through December 1944 [H29 @2026-11-07]. Record the files and dates currently visible. If the target is absent, check offline access [H4 @2026-11-07].

## Return a routing register

Deliver three things:

1. **Register:** one row per product (OMPF/B/R/auxiliary, separate court-martial trial file, burial case file/IDPF with its custodian, VA C-file, VA Master Index card, BIRLS, AAD enlistment record, compensation file, state bonus file, headstone application both sides, Guard records per state, county discharge, state WWI questionnaire/Gold Star packet, morning/after-action reports, award GO, transport lists, unit histories). Each row gives custodian, access status, release used, date requested and result (including page count, refusal, summary-only reply, appeal or pending). For a state questionnaire packet, also retain its subject, collection inclusion rule, family informants and the received page/image extent.
2. **Dated timeline:** three marked columns: personal entries, unit-level context and derivative annotations.
3. **Negative searches:** each with its scope (database, name form, branch, state, date range).

Also give the officer pay card, each other auxiliary series named and unit rosters their own register rows; a result can also be a limited release.

When used, also log the state Statement of Service card, Alabama home-address report, Army deserter pay card, POW entry, and Korean or Vietnam casualty entry as separate products.

A pending or refused request stays open. Do not fill the gap with unit history.
