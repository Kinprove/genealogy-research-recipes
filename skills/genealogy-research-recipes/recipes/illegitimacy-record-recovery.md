# Recover records of a birth outside marriage

Use this recipe when a child may have been born outside marriage and you need the records that such a birth could create in that jurisdiction: county bastardy bonds, criminal-court fornication and bastardy proceedings, support orders, grand-jury presentments, church discipline or baptism entries, and church-court suits. It covers which records to search and how to record what they say. It does not decide parentage. For the "never legally married" hypothesis and the warning that a woman's recorded surname may not be her maiden name, use [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md). The same recipe covers keeping terms such as "natural child" and "reputed father" in their original wording.

The series checklist below covers US county and court records (North Carolina and Pennsylvania) and church records, followed by Swedish court and child-welfare retrieval. It does not list English, Welsh or Scottish parish and Poor Law series. For England and Wales, start with [English nonregister record recovery](english-nonregister-record-recovery.md); build a separate jurisdiction-specific series list for Scotland.

## Do not decide illegitimacy from weak signals

Do not label a child illegitimate because of any of the following:

- **An apprenticeship.** Apprentice bonds and bastardy bonds are separate series. The North Carolina county records guide lists them separately by county. Lisson warns that researchers wrongly treat an apprenticed child as illegitimate [S2].
- **An "alias".** In earlier records it can mark a maiden name, or simply another name the person was known by [S6].
- **A household pattern.** Examples are a child living with grandparents, an aunt, a cousin or a neighbour, a large age gap after the other children, or a child in an institution.

Sorensen treats household patterns as reasons to start looking, not as findings. She gives her own counter-example: a child born after a nine-year gap really was the mother's own child, born when the mother was in her late 40s [S6]. She also reports that women called themselves "married" or "widow" when they were not, so a stated marital status does not settle the question either way [S6].

Record each of these signals as a reason to search, along with the record it came from.

## Search under the mother's surname first

Start with the mother's surname. A child born outside marriage usually took it [S1], [S2]. Lisson says the father's surname was used mainly when he acknowledged the child, and that in that case a bastardy bond is unlikely because he had accepted financial responsibility [S2]. A child could also take the father's surname later, for example after the parents married. If the child used the father's surname, record that as a separate observation. It is not evidence that the parents were married when the child was born.

**English naming leads.** The double-surname examples below come from Sorensen's UK lecture
[S6], not a general U.S. naming rule. In U.S. records a woman's alias can also mark a maiden
or previous married name; preserve the wording without deciding which name it represents.
Record double-surname forms as leads, and test them against local usage:

- **"X otherwise Y".** Sorensen says this form almost always signals illegitimacy, usually followed by the mother's later marriage to the father [S6]. That overstates it. "Otherwise" and "alias" were also used for a change of name or for being known by two names, without any disreputable meaning, and a combined name could pass down through later generations. Treat "otherwise" as a high-priority lead. Search both surnames for a baptism under the first name and a later marriage of the mother to a man of the second name.
- **"X Y" (no hyphen).** Sorensen reads this as the mother's surname followed by the father's, with the child usually born under the mother's name [S6]. This order rule is her view only. Test both orders.

## Work a jurisdiction and period series checklist

Before searching, find out which of these series exist for the county and period. Record a status for each series: inspected with a hit, inspected with no hit, unavailable, or not yet searched.

1. **County bastardy bonds.** These bonds existed so that someone other than the county would be financially responsible for the child [S2]. The Currituck County, North Carolina bond transcription says the bonds nearly always name the mother and the father, who signed the bond with his bondsmen, and rarely name the child. So expect a named father, and do not expect the child's name. Lisson reports that she has sometimes also found the child's name, age or birth date in them [S1], [S2]. When the mother would not name the father, she and those who signed with her were responsible instead. North Carolina's original bonds are held at the **State Archives of North Carolina in Raleigh, arranged by county**; FamilySearch has filmed copies under *Bastardy bonds and records (North Carolina), 1736–1957*. Check original and filmed coverage separately rather than sending a request to a courthouse by default. [S14] Lisson's experience is mainly North Carolina [S2]. For any other state, establish the series' custodian and coverage before searching it.
2. **County court order books and minutes.** Search them under the mother's name, the named man's name and the couple's names, including for couples who never married [S1]. Record any apprenticeship of the child as its own series entry. Apprenticeship alone is not a bastardy finding. If the instrument expressly describes the child's status, preserve that wording as the instrument's statement, keeping the apprenticeship distinct from the bastardy-bond series. If it names the mother under one surname and says she is now married to a named man, retain both as a dated name-and-role crosswalk. That wording establishes neither the earlier surname as her maiden name nor the current husband as the biological father. [S8]
3. **Quarter sessions (criminal court) fornication and bastardy proceedings and support orders.** In 1800s Pennsylvania, fornication and bastardy was prosecuted in the county Court of Quarter Sessions, which ordered child support, lying-in costs and fines. Allen describes Centre County, where an inquest into an unmarried pregnant woman led to court files and support records naming the mother, the accused man and the child [S4], [S5]. Centre County holds civil and criminal court papers for 1800–1875. Allen says volunteers built a fornication and bastardy index to these papers [S4], [S5]. The library's published list of indexes does not include one, so confirm it with the library's Pennsylvania Room before relying on it or describing its coverage. In other Pennsylvania counties, search the Quarter Sessions papers directly and ask whether a local index exists.
4. **Grand-jury presentments or indictments for adultery or fornication.** Search these when a presumed couple has no marriage or divorce record. In Lisson's own family, a grand-jury indictment for living in adultery, about 20 years after the couple's supposed marriage, led her into further court and grand-jury research, and she reports concluding that the couple never married [S3].
5. **Church records.** First find out how the family's denomination treated an unwed mother and her child. Then search its baptism, discipline and excommunication records for both of them [S2]. Quaker monthly meetings, for example, recorded charges and disownments for being with child while unmarried. In Latin registers, look for *ignotus* ("unknown") in place of the father, for example *nomine ignoto* ("name unknown") [S2].
   - **Church-court suits.** A blank father in the baptism is no reason to stop at the parish register: a diocese may keep its own list of impregnation cases, and such a list can name the accused man. One reported example is the Hungarian Archdiocese of Kalocsa's spreadsheet of illegitimate impregnations, divorces and separations [S7]. To work a list like this, sort on the case-type column (*pertípus*), pick out the rows coded *impraegnatio* (Latin for impregnation), and order those rows by locality [S7]. Expect gaps. The list does not hold every such pregnancy, and the source guesses that only cases that went to a suit (*Klage*) appear in it [S7], so a missing case is uninformative. For a listed case, record the suit as an allegation, not as paternity. Trace it to the underlying record where one survives, then search for a later marriage of the couple, because some of the listed parties married afterwards and some did not [S7]. The source describes only the list. Reading such lists as a diocesan-court series with case files behind them is this recipe's own inference, and the Kalocsa list and its coverage rest on a single forum post. Before you describe such a list, ask the diocesan archive what it covers and whether files survive behind it.
6. **Legitimation by subsequent marriage.** A later marriage can change the child's legal status under the applicable law; North Carolina G.S. 49-12 is one explicit example. [S15]
   - **Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> A legitimation or amended-birth-record request can identify the child and parents who may be living and disclose sensitive parentage. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before sending a request or proof documents.
   - Check the law for the jurisdiction and event date, then seek the marriage entry and any legitimation, acknowledgment or amended birth record. Keep legal status, a later surname and biological paternity separate. A surname change alone does not establish any of them.
7. **Adoption and sealed records.** Treat an adoption as a separate legal proceeding, not an explanation inferred from a surname or foster placement. Identify the court, child-placement agency and vital-record authority for the place and date, then check their access rules.
   - **Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> An adoption-record request can disclose the adoptee's and birth or adoptive relatives' identities and sensitive parentage. Answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` before sending any request or proof documents.
   - Request adoption papers or an original birth certificate only through the route the responsible court, agency or vital-record office authorizes. Sealed files are not ordinary public court files: North Carolina, for example, provides non-identifying information and a confidential-intermediary route subject to eligibility and consent. Record a refusal or restriction separately from an inspected no-hit; do not try to bypass sealing or identify living relatives through public tracing. [S16]

For an agency adoption, request the agency's background (non-identifying) information as its own request, separate from the court decree or sealed court file. In California, for example, the licensed agency that handled the adoption, or the Department of Social Services, gives an adoptee general facts about the birth parents and their medical history. A written request sent to the Department when the agency is unknown is either processed there or returned with the agency's address [S17 @2026-11-12].

Do not treat the decree as the whole adoption record. MyHeritage's adoptee guide says agencies often kept far more than the final legal paperwork [S19]. Washington's current rule shows what an agency file can hold: the report on the birth parents that an agency must give prospective adoptive parents includes heritage, physical description, education, occupation, family and social history, the circumstances leading to the adoption, and medical history [S18]. Record the agency's reply separately from the court's.

## Swedish unknown-parent record retrieval

Choose the record-creating authority for the child's dates before searching: a court proceeding, a child-welfare file and a statement in a later church record are separate evidence [S9], [S10]. Have the archive confirm the historical authority and applicable series dates before switching between court and child-welfare routes; the examples below do not supply a universal legal cutoff.

### Fines-list paragraph to court proceeding

For an unknown-father inquiry about a child born in 1852, ArkivDigital suggests starting with the *häradsrätt* covering the mother's locality and its *domböcker* for the birth year and following year [S9]. The mother's locality is a search lead; the child's birth parish does not establish the offense venue. Establish the period's court jurisdiction and legal context before expecting a proceeding, and do not presume prosecution.

Browse backward in a surviving court-minute volume for a *saköreslängd* (fines list). If you find an entry for the mother, retain any named man and copy the paragraph reference, then retrieve that paragraph in the corresponding court and term's *dombok*. Retain court, year, term, volume, paragraph and image locators beside the entry and proceeding [S9].

Account for every court term in the selected interval: terms may be bound together or occupy separate volumes [S9]. Check actual catalogue holdings and keep unavailable or uninspected volumes distinct from inspected no-hits. Record the named people and the court's actual finding in their recorded roles; neither establishes biological parentage. An absent list or proceeding does not establish paternity or exclude a candidate. The example does not establish a universal prosecution cutoff.

### Child-welfare file to its later custodian

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> This inquiry sends the child's and the mother's names, the birth date and residences to a municipal archive, and asks for a welfare file about parentage and family circumstances. The child and the mother may be living: answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` for each of them before the first request, and send only what the archive needs to find the file.

Where the historical responsibility points to a *barnavårdsman* or *barnavårdsnämnd*, ask the responsible municipal archive for both the child's register entry (*liggare*) and individual case file (*akt*), supplying the child, mother, birth date and known residence or move dates [S10], [S11]. Request the file references and surviving documents, rather than treating a name-only reply as the complete case.

**Privacy gate:** <!-- privacy-gate: request --> The authority that received a transferred file is a new destination; answer the privacy gate in `SKILL.md` again before you send the child's and the mother's details there.

Follow the file across documented moves. If the first archive does not hold it, ask whether it was transferred, and request the destination board, transfer date and any register or file reference. Use those references to request the receiving authority's entry and file [S10], [S11]. Linköping's archive guide describes the forwarding of child-welfare papers when a child moved and became registered in another municipality; it also warns of gaps in particular rural series [S11]. A birth-municipality no-hit therefore leaves transfer and survival unresolved.

ArkivDigital reports that a file could close when the parents married or the child was adopted [S10]. Ask for the closing entry, its date and any reference to another authority or case. Closure does not establish destruction, successful identification of the father or an adoption decision; retain the welfare file and any separate court proceeding as distinct records.

Follow the child into later confirmation records, moving certificates or court statements when those records survive: the presenter describes them as possible later statements of the father's identity [S10]. Preserve who supplied the name and when, and test that statement against the mother, child and alleged man's dated records before combining it with the earlier case.

### Allmänna barnhuset roll to admission papers

If this inquiry already identifies a child in an Allmänna barnhuset roll, open the original roll image and retain the child's name, dates, admission number and any written reference or abbreviation [S12]. The roll can record foster placements and information about the mother, while her name may remain absent [S13]. Keep institutional admission separate from a finding about the child's birth outside marriage.

Follow the roll's actual reference to the linked papers, usually admission documents. Use the admission number or referenced category to select the corresponding series in Allmänna barnhuset, SE/SSA/0809A [S12], [S13]:

| Roll or reference category | Supporting-paper series |
| --- | --- |
| *Barnrulla* | E5A |
| *Rulla över späda barn* (SpB) | E5B |
| *Rulla över äldre barn* (ÄB) | E5C |
| *Rulla över utlämnade barn* | E5F |

Request the actual surviving papers with the roll, entry, year and reference; preserve their own names, roles and statements after inspection. The table does not establish what a particular file contains or identify a parent. If the roll instead gives a diary number/year, follow that distinct reference to *Huvuddiarium* C1 and its accompanying papers E4A [S13]. Keep the different identifiers separate, and record an unavailable or uninspected file without treating it as evidence about parentage.

If the roll records that the mother served as a wet nurse, retain that statement's date and any nurse reference, and use the guide's *Amrullor* D1BA (1816–1937) as the next retrieval target [S13]. Follow the explicitly recorded role; a nurse with a similar name is not thereby the child's mother.

If the entry describes home support (*understödsbarn*), use the separate rolls D2GA and their register D2GB [S13]. The guide says these children received support while remaining at home, so that category does not establish residence at the institution.

## Record allegations, not paternity

A bond, court file or church-court suit records an accusation, a reputation or a court outcome. It does not prove biological fatherhood. A "reputed" father is the man known or believed to be the father. Lisson contrasts him with an "imputed" father, whom the mother accused and who may have disputed it [S2]. "Imputed" is her term. The usual legal term for the accused man is "putative father". Copy the record's own term exactly.

Record each of these as a separate observation, each with its record locator:

- the mother
- the child, with the age or birth date as written
- the named man, as an allegation
- who posted the bond, if it was someone other than the named man
- the court action or outcome

Pass the paternity question to [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md).

## Read absences and surnames narrowly

**A missing bond proves nothing about legitimacy or paternity.** Lisson says no bond was needed when the father acknowledged the child or when relatives raised it, because the county faced no cost [S2]. That is her reasoning from what the bond was for. The mother and her co-signers could be held responsible, so a family taking charge can still leave a bond. Search the series either way.

**Stop repeating marriage searches that keep failing.** If marriage searches have come up empty, open the "never married" branch and search the court and grand-jury series above.

**A surname in a Bible or will is not proof of a maiden name.** In Lisson's case, the woman appeared as Sarah Blanks in the family Bible and her father-in-law's will, but her maiden name was Talley [S3].
