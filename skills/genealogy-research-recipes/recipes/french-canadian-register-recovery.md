# Recover French-Canadian ancestors from combined registers, post and Montréal entries and notarial acts

Use when a French-Canadian person seems to be missing from an index, or when an event at an early
French post in the Great Lakes or Mississippi region seems to have gone unrecorded. Also use when
a marriage or estate is known only from the church entry. Inputs: the name as found, a parish or
post, and a date range. For the general method of searching name components and roles separately,
use [name searches across scripts and OCR, § Build a small attestation inventory](corpus-seeded-name-search.md#build-a-small-attestation-inventory).
This recipe adds only the French-Canadian forms. Route
identity rulings to [evidence/proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md)
and FamilySearch Catalog/film navigation to [FamilySearch workflows](familysearch-practical-record-workflows.md).

## Do not call the person absent until the name forms are exhausted

A miss under the name the family used is not a finding. Before reporting absence, re-run each
search with these forms and log every form and its result. Run them in every index you use,
including Généalogie Québec and the PRDH database, which the presenter lists among the Québec
resources. [S1]

- **Baptismal prefix.** Québec children usually had several given names, and later records often
  use a different one from the baptism. The presenter says almost all girls (especially in the
  17th–18th centuries) were baptized *Marie* plus a second and sometimes a third name, and were
  known by the second name. Censuses often show only that name. She also says many boys were
  *Jean-Baptiste* plus another name. So search *Marie Cécile* as well as *Cécile*, and
  *Jean-Baptiste Pierre* as well as *Pierre*. Use the French spelling (*Jean*, *Baptiste*). Also
  try the abbreviated prefix (*M. Louise*, as in the presenter's example), hyphenated and
  unhyphenated given names, and the forms with the prefix dropped. [S1]
- **Dit names.** One person's dit name varies across their records. Search each surname alone,
  the full *X dit Y* form, *Y dit X*, and the feminine form (*dite*). In the presenter's example,
  one woman appears as *Briault dite Lamarche*, as *M. Louise* under each surname, and with the
  order reversed. The presenter says a dit name can reflect where the father or grandfather came
  from, or a parish the family left. Do not assume which one applies to your person. [S1]
- **Place names.** Search *St*/*Ste* and the spelled-out *Saint*/*Sainte*. Family trees and
  indexes often use the abbreviation. FamilySearch standard place names spell *Saint* out in
  full. [S1]

A hit under a variant only makes that record a candidate. Do not merge candidates because a dit
name or given name matches.

## Join variants through documented ties, starting with the sacramental entries

The presenter treats the baptism, marriage and burial entries as often the only way to show that
the different name forms belong to one person. [S1] Search those entries first. For each
candidate link, record:

- which entry (B, M or S) or other record carries which name form;
- the parents, spouse, children or other people that tie the entries together;
- whether the link is **reconciled**, meaning tied through those named people in the sacramental
  entries or in other records (such as the children's entries), or **unreconciled**, meaning the
  match rests on name, age or place only.

Keep unreconciled links in a separate list. A census or index entry under a shortened name can be
attached only after the link is reconciled. If the person's own B/M/S chain is incomplete and the
link rests on other records, send that correlation to evidence/proof judgment for the ruling.

## Read a combined register as one chronological book

The presenter describes this as a common feature of Québec registers: baptisms, marriages and
burials appear in a single book in date order. A margin letter marks each entry type: **B** for
baptême (baptism), **M** for mariage (marriage) and **S** for sépulture (burial). When the letter
carries a number, record it as part of the locator; in her example the marriage is marked
**M 5**, the fifth marriage of the year. [S1] To work through such a book:

- To find one event type, scan the margins for that letter. Do not look for a separate burial or
  marriage book until you have checked that this volume does not cover the event.
- If a baptism calls a parent *défunt* or *défunte*, use that as a cue. Search the same volume
  backwards and forwards for that parent's **S** entry and for the parents' **M** entry. The
  presenter's example baptism shows the mother as deceased at the time of the baptism. [S1]
- A marriage entry gives details for both the bride and the groom: parents and whether each was
  of age. In the presenter's example it also states that the bride's mother is deceased. Extract
  each of those statements separately. [S1]

Record the volume, year and entry letter/number for each entry, and keep the literal French
wording beside your translation.

## Use the second copy when the parish copy fails

Do not say Québec had no civil registration before 1994. Clergy and other legally authorized
officers kept the civil-status registers in duplicate: one copy stayed in the parish, and the
civil copy went each year to the local court clerk (prothonotary). [S3], [S4] The duty to keep
two copies dates from 7 November 1678, when the Conseil souverain at Québec registered the royal
Ordonnance of 1667. The Ordonnance also made priests hand the second copy to the civil
authority after each year. [S6] How far priests complied in the 17th century cannot be
measured, so do not assume a court copy for every early parish or year. [S6] The Civil Code of
Québec, in force on 1 January 1994, made the Directeur de l'état civil (registrar of civil
status) the sole officer of civil status. [S4]

- The court copy is a separately written copy of the same entries. BAnQ holds it once the record
  is more than 100 years old; more recent registers are with the Directeur de l'état civil. [S3]
- Because the registers were made in duplicate, one copy may survive where the other was
  destroyed. [S5] When the parish copy is damaged, illegible or missing, or the entry you expect
  is not in the copy you read, search the same dates in the other copy. The PRDH team filled some
  gaps in the parish series from the court copies this way. [S6]
- Image sets such as the Drouin collection or FamilySearch's Québec Catholic parish registers can
  show either copy. Check which one the image shows, and log the copy you read with the entry.

## Search the post's own register first, then Notre-Dame de Montréal

Before treating an event as unrecorded, write down the date the local register begins. A register
from the right place can still start too late for the event. [S1]

- **Post registers come first.** Holy Family parish at Cahokia kept its own register from 1699;
  the Diocese of Belleville holds it. Events in the Green Bay region turn up in the
  Michilimackinac (Ste-Anne) register, 1695–1821. Search these before going to Montréal.
- **Montréal is a second route, reported by the presenter.** She says the thinly settled posts
  were served by priests on circuit from Montréal, who wrote up baptisms and marriages after they
  got back. [S1], [S2] That does not hold as a general rule for Cahokia or Green Bay, which had
  their own registers. Use it for events before a post's register begins, or where a post entry
  is missing.
- **The Montréal register is Notre-Dame de Montréal.** The presenter says to search "the
  cathedral", but in the 1700s Montréal was one parish, Notre-Dame. The Diocese of Montréal was
  not created until 1836. Notre-Dame de Montréal is among the Québec Catholic parish registers on
  FamilySearch.
- **Clusters.** If circuit entries are present, the presenter expects baptisms and marriages to
  fall within a few days of each other, because they waited for the priest's visit. [S2]
- **Burials.** The presenter expects burials to have taken place at the time of death. Do not
  assume they were written up in Montréal. [S2]

Log each route separately: the register searched, its start date, the volume, the date window you
scanned, and the result. Do not report the event as unrecorded. Report the registers, volumes and
date windows you actually searched. If you did not search Notre-Dame de Montréal for an event
before the post register begins, or for a missing post entry, list it as an unsearched lead.

**Non-Catholic settlers.** The presenter describes the circuit-served posts as having no
established church of their own. Search the Catholic registers even for a family that was later
Protestant, and do not read a hit or a miss as evidence of the family's religion.

## Find marriage contracts and estate acts by notary

The church marriage entry is not the marriage contract. Québec marriage contracts, including
dowry terms, and probate/estate acts (inventaires après décès, partages) are notarial
records. [S1] Tutelles (guardianships) are judicial acts: BAnQ's Montréal centre classes the
Montréal judicial district's tutelles and curatelles as judicial acts under CC601, and
FamilySearch has "Canada, Quebec, Quebec Judicial District, Guardianships, 1639-1930". For a
tutelle, search the judicial series for the district first, then the notary. To search the
notarial records:

- For an act before 1810, search Parchemin first. It is an
  inventory of Québec notarial minutes that gives the names of the parties and the kind of
  transaction. Its publisher gives its span as 1626–1809 and sells access to institutions only,
  so ask BAnQ or a genealogical library whether it subscribes. Treat a Parchemin entry as a lead
  to the notary and act date, then find and inspect the act. [S7]
- Then, or when Parchemin is not available, search Ancestry's "Quebec, Canada, Notarial Records,
  1637-1935". Many hits there are répertoire (notary's index) pages only. Use a hit as a lead to
  the notary and act date, then find and inspect the act itself. [S1]
- When neither finds the act, browse by notary. Québec notarial records are arranged by notary
  and date, not by the names of the people involved. FamilySearch's "Canada, Quebec, Notarial
  Records, 1800-1920" is browse-only images with no name index. To browse it, find the notary
  working in the family's town, or one named in a compiled genealogy. [S1]

Log notarial leads by notary: the notary's name, the town, the date range browsed, the act
date/number if shown, and whether you actually inspected the act or only saw a répertoire line. A
notary chosen because he worked in the town gives you a place to browse; it does not tell you the
family used him.

Before you read any sum in an inventaire après décès, find the act's own statement of the money
of account. A September 1818 inventory from Saint-Luc says the goods were appraised "aux livres et
sols ancien cours" (in livres and sols of the old currency), and its transcription writes an item
priced at 48 sols as 2£ 8s [S8]. There £ and s mean livres and sols, not pounds sterling. Count
20 sols to the livre and 12 deniers to the sol [S9]. Do not trust a machine translation of these
amounts: Google Translate rendered the heading as "books and old course floors" and turned livres
into pounds [S8]. Transcribe each sum as written, with its unit.

## Close with a register packet

Return the following:

1. **Variant log:** every form and order searched, with the database and result for each.
2. **Combined-register entries:** B/M/S entries with volume and entry locators, the copy read
   (parish or court), plus literal wording.
3. **Register routes:** the post or regional register and its start date, any Notre-Dame de
   Montréal searches (or the unsearched lead), and the date window for each.
4. **Notarial leads:** grouped by notary, with each marked as inspected act, répertoire only,
   Parchemin entry only, or not inspected; tutelle searches in judicial series listed by series.
5. **Person links:** two separate lists, reconciled and unreconciled.

Name the next register, volume or notary still to be searched. Do not fill the gap with a
merged identity.
